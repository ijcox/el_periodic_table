Overview

Radon is a radioactive, colorless, odorless, tasteless noble gas. It is one of the densest gases and is the only gas under normal conditions that has only radioactive isotopes. Radon is formed as part of the normal radioactive decay chains of uranium and thorium, and is the immediate decay product of radium.

Radon is a health hazard; it is the second leading cause of lung cancer after smoking and the leading cause among non-smokers. It accumulates in buildings, particularly in basements and ground floors, due to its high density. Radon concentrations vary widely depending on local geology and building construction.

As a noble gas, radon is generally unreactive. However, it is the most reactive of the noble gases (excluding oganesson) and can form compounds with fluorine. Radon difluoride (RnF₂) is the only confirmed compound, though others have been reported.

Isotopes

Radon has no stable isotopes. All isotopes are radioactive:

  • Radon-222 (²²²Rn) - Half-life of 3.8235 days, alpha emitter, most common isotope (from uranium-238 decay chain)
  • Radon-220 (²²⁰Rn) - Half-life of 55.6 seconds, alpha emitter, historically called "thoron" (from thorium-232 decay chain)
  • Radon-219 (²¹⁹Rn) - Half-life of 3.96 seconds, alpha emitter, historically called "actinon" (from uranium-235 decay chain)
  • Radon-218 (²¹⁸Rn) - Half-life of 35 milliseconds
  • Radon-217 (²¹⁷Rn) - Half-life of 540 microseconds
  • Radon-211 to -216 - Various isotopes with half-lives from microseconds to hours

Radon-222 is the isotope of greatest health concern due to its relatively long half-life, allowing it to accumulate in buildings.

Common Uses

  • Radiation Therapy - Historically used in cancer treatment (radon seeds implanted in tumors)
  • Earthquake Prediction Research - Monitoring radon levels as potential earthquake precursor
  • Geological Research - Tracing groundwater movement, studying rock fractures, mineral exploration
  • Industrial Radiography - Historical use as gamma ray source (largely replaced by other isotopes)
  • Calibration - Radon sources used to calibrate radiation detectors
  • Atmospheric Studies - Tracer for atmospheric transport and mixing
  • Hydrological Studies - Dating groundwater, studying water-rock interactions
  • Radon Spas - Historical use in some health spas (now recognized as hazardous)

Due to health concerns, most historical uses of radon have been discontinued. Current uses are primarily in research.

Biological Role

Radon has no biological role and is a significant health hazard:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • Lung Cancer Risk - Second leading cause of lung cancer after smoking
  • Radioactive Decay Chain - Radon-222 decays to polonium-218, then through series of short-lived isotopes
  • Alpha Radiation - Radon and its decay products emit alpha particles that damage lung tissue
  • Inhalation Hazard - Main exposure route is inhalation of radon and its decay products
  • Accumulation in Buildings - Seeps from ground into buildings, accumulates in poorly ventilated spaces
  • Smoking Synergy - Radon exposure combined with smoking dramatically increases lung cancer risk
  • Other Health Effects - Possible link to other cancers (leukemia, stomach cancer) but evidence weaker
  • No Safe Level - No threshold below which radon exposure is risk-free

The WHO estimates radon causes between 3-14% of all lung cancers worldwide, depending on average radon levels and smoking prevalence.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Radon occurs naturally from radioactive decay:

  • Soil and Rocks - Produced from radium in soil, rocks, and building materials
  • Groundwater - Dissolved in groundwater, released when water is used
  • Outdoor Air - Typical concentration 10-100 Bq/m³ (varies by location)
  • Indoor Air - Can accumulate to much higher levels (up to thousands of Bq/m³)
  • Geological Variations - Higher in areas with granite, shale, phosphate, uranium ores
  • Building Materials - Some materials (certain concretes, natural stones) can emit radon

Artificial Production: Radon collected from natural sources or produced artificially:

  1. Radium Sources - Radon collected from radium-226 sources (radon emanates from radium)
  2. Separation - Radon separated from other gases by cooling or adsorption
  3. Purification - Further purification if needed for specific applications
  4. Sealed Sources - For medical or calibration use, sealed in small capsules

Radon is not produced commercially on large scale due to health risks. Small quantities produced for research and calibration.

Environmental Impact

Radon is a significant environmental health concern:

  • Indoor Air Pollution - Major contributor to indoor air pollution worldwide
  • Building Regulations - Many countries have regulations for radon in new buildings
  • Testing and Mitigation - Radon testing recommended for all homes; mitigation systems available
  • Water Contamination - Radon in well water can be released into air during showers
  • Mining Operations - Uranium and other mines can have high radon levels; ventilation required
  • Waste Management - Radon from uranium mill tailings and other radioactive waste
  • Natural Variation - Levels vary by geology, season, weather, building construction
  • Climate Change Impact - Changing building practices and climate may affect indoor radon levels
  • Public Health Programs - Many countries have national radon action plans

Radon exposure is preventable through testing and mitigation, making it an important public health issue.

Discovery and History

Radon was discovered in 1900 by German physicist Friedrich Ernst Dorn while studying the decay chain of radium. He noticed that radium compounds emitted a radioactive gas that he called "radium emanation." In 1908, William Ramsay and Robert Whytlaw-Gray isolated the gas and determined its density, finding it to be the heaviest known gas. They named it "niton" from the Latin word "nitens" meaning "shining."

The name "radon" was adopted internationally in 1923. The health effects of radon were first noted in miners. In the 16th century, Agricola described a high incidence of fatal lung disease among miners in the Erz Mountains. This was later identified as lung cancer caused by radon exposure. The link between residential radon and lung cancer was established in the 1980s.

Radon was the fifth radioactive element to be discovered, after uranium, thorium, radium, and polonium. Its discovery helped elucidate the natural radioactive decay chains.

Interesting Facts

  • Radon is the heaviest known gas under normal conditions
  • It was originally called "niton" from Latin "nitens" meaning "shining"
  • Radon is the only gas that is radioactive at room temperature
  • The first health effects were noticed in miners 500 years ago
  • Radon causes about 21,000 lung cancer deaths annually in the US
  • Some houses have radon levels equivalent to smoking several packs of cigarettes daily
  • Radon is responsible for majority of human exposure to natural radiation
  • The element was used in luminous paint for watch dials (mixed with zinc sulfide)

Safety Information

Health Hazard: Radon is carcinogenic. Second leading cause of lung cancer.

Inhalation Risk: Main exposure route. Radon decay products (polonium isotopes) deposit in lungs.

No Warning Signs: Colorless, odorless, tasteless. Can only be detected with special equipment.

Testing: All homes should be tested for radon. Test kits available commercially.

Action Level: US EPA recommends mitigation if radon levels exceed 4 pCi/L (148 Bq/m³).

Mitigation: Radon reduction systems (sub-slab depressurization) can reduce levels by 80-99%.

New Construction: Radon-resistant construction techniques recommended in high-risk areas.

Occupational Exposure: Miners, certain industrial workers may need monitoring and protection.

Water Supply: Radon in water can be removed by aeration or carbon filtration.

First Aid: No specific first aid for radon exposure. Prevention is key through testing and mitigation.

Regulations: Many countries have regulations for radon in workplaces and new buildings.

Public Health Recommendation: Test your home for radon. If high, install mitigation system.
