Overview

Helium is a colorless, odorless, tasteless, non-toxic, inert, monatomic gas that heads the noble gas group in the periodic table. Its boiling point is the lowest among all the elements, and it exists only as a gas except in extreme conditions.

Helium was first detected on August 18, 1868, as a bright yellow line with a wavelength of 587.49 nanometers in the spectrum of the Sun's chromosphere, making it the first element discovered outside Earth. It was named after the Greek Titan of the Sun, Helios.

Isotopes

Helium has two stable isotopes:

  • Helium-4 (⁴He) - The most common isotope, making up 99.99986% of naturally occurring helium. It has two protons and two neutrons.
  • Helium-3 (³He) - Contains two protons and one neutron. It makes up about 0.00014% of natural helium on Earth but is more abundant in lunar soil and gas giants.

Helium-3 has unique properties including superfluidity at temperatures near absolute zero and is used in cryogenics and nuclear fusion research.

Common Uses

  • Cryogenics - Essential for cooling superconducting magnets in MRI machines, NMR spectrometers, and particle accelerators
  • Pressurization and Purging - Used in rocket propulsion systems and to purge fuel tanks
  • Welding - Provides inert shielding atmosphere for arc welding of materials like aluminum and stainless steel
  • Breathing Mixtures - Used in deep-sea diving (heliox) to prevent nitrogen narcosis and oxygen toxicity
  • Balloons and Airships - Lifting gas for weather balloons, party balloons, and blimps due to its non-flammability
  • Leak Detection - Used as a tracer gas to detect leaks in high-vacuum systems and containers
  • Chromatography - Carrier gas in gas chromatography
  • Scientific Research - Used in studies of superfluidity, quantum mechanics, and low-temperature physics

Biological Role

Helium has no known biological role and is not essential for life. However, it has important medical and scientific applications:

  • Used in respiratory treatments for conditions like asthma and COPD when mixed with oxygen (heliox)
  • Critical for cooling superconducting magnets in MRI machines used for medical imaging
  • Non-toxic and inert, but can displace oxygen in enclosed spaces, posing an asphyxiation risk
  • When inhaled, temporarily changes voice timbre by increasing the speed of sound in vocal cords
  • Used in lung diffusion tests to measure gas transfer in the lungs

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Helium is the second most abundant element in the observable universe (about 24% by elemental mass), but relatively rare on Earth. Most terrestrial helium is created by the radioactive decay of heavier elements like uranium and thorium.

Production Methods:

  • Natural Gas Extraction - Most commercial helium is extracted from natural gas fields, particularly in the United States, Qatar, and Algeria
  • Fractional Distillation - Separating helium from other gases at low temperatures
  • Air Separation - Extracting helium from air, though this is less common due to low concentration (5.2 ppm)
  • Radioactive Decay - Naturally produced by alpha decay of radioactive elements in Earth's crust

Conservation: Helium is a non-renewable resource on Earth and can escape the planet's gravity into space once released into the atmosphere.

Environmental Impact

Helium is environmentally inert and does not contribute to ozone depletion, global warming, or air pollution. It is non-toxic and does not react with other elements in the environment.

The primary environmental concern is resource depletion. Helium is a finite resource on Earth, and once released into the atmosphere, it can escape into space. Conservation and recycling of helium, particularly in scientific and medical applications, are increasingly important.

Discovery and History

Helium was first detected on August 18, 1868, by French astronomer Jules Janssen during a total solar eclipse in Guntur, India. He observed a yellow line in the Sun's spectrum that didn't match any known element.

Later that year, English astronomer Norman Lockyer independently observed the same yellow line and proposed it was caused by a new element, which he named helium after the Greek sun god Helios. Terrestrial helium was first isolated in 1895 by Sir William Ramsay from cleveite, a uranium mineral.

Helium was first liquefied by Heike Kamerlingh Onnes in 1908, and its superfluidity was discovered by Pyotr Kapitsa in 1937.

Interesting Facts

  • Helium is the second most abundant element in the universe, making up about 24% of elemental mass
  • Helium was first discovered in the Sun's spectrum before being found on Earth, hence its name from Helios, the Greek sun god
  • Helium gas is the second lightest gas, with a density of only 0.1786 g/L at standard conditions
  • Helium has the lowest boiling point of any element at -268.93°C (-452°F), just above absolute zero
  • Liquid helium is used to cool superconducting magnets in MRI machines and particle accelerators
  • Helium is a non-renewable resource on Earth and can escape the planet's gravity into space

Safety Information

Asphyxiation Risk: Helium is non-toxic but displaces oxygen in enclosed spaces, creating a serious asphyxiation hazard. It is odorless and colorless with no warning signs.

Storage: Must be stored in well-ventilated areas away from heat sources. High-pressure cylinders require proper handling and securing.

Voice Alteration Hazard: Inhaling helium directly from pressurized tanks can cause lung injury, embolism, or asphyxiation due to oxygen displacement.

Cryogenic Hazards: Liquid helium is extremely cold (-269°C) and can cause severe frostbite or cryogenic burns on contact with skin or eyes.
