Overview

Oganesson is a synthetic chemical element and the last element of period 7, making it currently the heaviest known element on the periodic table. It is a highly radioactive element that does not occur naturally and can only be produced in particle accelerators. Oganesson is predicted to be a solid at room temperature and possibly even a semiconductor, which is highly unusual for a noble gas (all other noble gases are gases at room temperature). This predicted behavior results from extreme relativistic effects that dominate the chemistry of superheavy elements.

Oganesson was first synthesized in 2002 and confirmed in 2005 by a joint Russian-American team at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR) in Dubna, Russia. The element was named after Yuri Oganessian, a Russian nuclear physicist of Armenian descent who has made major contributions to superheavy element research. Oganesson is only the second element to be named after a living person at the time of naming (the first was seaborgium).

Oganesson's predicted physical state as a solid at room temperature makes it unique among noble gases. This results from relativistic effects that significantly increase the van der Waals forces between atoms. Its predicted melting and boiling points are hundreds of degrees higher than radon's (melting point -71°C, boiling point -61.7°C). Some calculations even suggest oganesson might be a semiconductor, which would be unprecedented for a noble gas.

Chemical Behavior Predicted to be more reactive than other noble gases, possibly forming compounds

Oganesson's chemistry is predicted to be dominated by extreme relativistic effects. Unlike other noble gases which are chemically inert (with only xenon and radon forming a few compounds under extreme conditions), oganesson may be significantly more reactive. The 7p electrons are predicted to be so destabilized by relativity that oganesson might readily form compounds. Possible compounds include OgF₂ (oganesson difluoride), OgF₄ (oganesson tetrafluoride), and OgF₆ (oganesson hexafluoride). Some calculations even suggest oganesson might form a +8 oxidation state compound (OgF₈), though this is controversial. Oganesson is predicted to have significantly lower ionization energy than radon, making it easier to remove electrons. The Og²⁻ ion might also be stable, which would be unprecedented for noble gases.

Isotopes

Oganesson has no stable isotopes. All isotopes are radioactive and synthetic:

  • Oganesson-293 (²⁹³Og) - Half-life: 0.12 milliseconds? (alpha decay, tentative)
  • Oganesson-294 (²⁹⁴Og) - Half-life: 0.69 milliseconds (alpha decay, spontaneous fission) - Most stable known isotope
  • Oganesson-295 (²⁹⁵Og) - Half-life: 0.18 milliseconds? (alpha decay, tentative)
  • Oganesson-296 (²⁹⁶Og) - Half-life: ? (tentative)

Oganesson-294 is the most stable known isotope with a half-life of approximately 0.69 milliseconds. The very short half-lives of oganesson isotopes have prevented any chemical studies so far, though theoretical predictions are extensive. Some calculations suggest that more neutron-rich isotopes with longer half-lives may exist near the "island of stability," but these have not yet been synthesized.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Oganesson does not occur naturally. It is entirely synthetic.

Artificial Production Methods:

  1. Hot Fusion Reactions:
    ²⁴⁹Cf + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁹⁴Og + 3n (original JINR synthesis, 2002, confirmed 2005)
    ²⁴⁹Cf + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁹³Og + 4n
  2. Production Challenges:
    Californium-249 target required (difficult and expensive to produce)
    Extremely low production cross-section (~0.5 picobarns)
    Only a few atoms produced after months of beam time
  3. Other Possible Reactions:
    ²⁵¹Cf + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁹⁶Og + 3n (theoretical)
    ²⁴⁸Cm + ⁵⁴Cr → ²⁹⁹Og + 3n (theoretical)
  4. Separation - Oganesson atoms separated using gas-filled recoil separators. The Dubna Gas-Filled Recoil Separator (DGFRS) was used for its discovery and study.
  5. Detection - Individual atoms detected by their characteristic decay patterns using silicon detectors. Decay by alpha emission or spontaneous fission.
  6. Quantity - Production rates extremely low (a few atoms total in history).

Production Sites: Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR, Dubna, Russia - primary site), Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (USA, collaboration). Total world production is only about 5 atoms.

Discovery and History

The discovery of oganesson completed period 7 of the periodic table and represented a pinnacle of superheavy element research:

First Claim (1999): In 1999, researchers at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory claimed discovery of element 118, but retracted in 2002 when the results could not be reproduced and evidence emerged of scientific misconduct by one researcher.

Actual Discovery (2002): In 2002, a joint Russian-American team at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR) in Dubna, Russia, synthesized oganesson by bombarding californium-249 with calcium-48 ions. They observed three atoms of oganesson-294. The results were published in 2006 after careful verification.

Confirmation (2005): The discovery was confirmed in 2005-2006 by the same collaboration with additional experiments producing more atoms and studying the decay chains in detail.

Independent Confirmation (2011): In 2011, a team at the GSI Helmholtz Centre in Germany independently confirmed the discovery of oganesson, providing definitive validation.

Naming: The discovery team proposed the name "oganesson" after Yuri Oganessian, who has made major contributions to superheavy element research, including the discovery of several elements and development of the "hot fusion" technique using calcium-48 beams. This was only the second time an element was named after a living person.

Official Recognition (2016): On November 28, 2016, IUPAC officially announced the name "oganesson" with symbol Og. The name was approved after the usual period of public comment and review.

Significance: Oganesson's discovery completed period 7 of the periodic table (though period 7 is not completely filled due to the placement of lanthanides and actinides). It represents the current frontier of known elements and demonstrates the power of modern nuclear physics to create and study matter at the extreme end of the periodic table.

Theoretical Interest: Oganesson has attracted enormous theoretical interest due to predictions that it might behave completely differently from other noble gases, possibly being a solid reactive element that forms compounds, fundamentally challenging our understanding of chemical periodicity.

Safety Information

Radioactivity: All isotopes of oganesson are highly radioactive. Primary hazards are alpha radiation and spontaneous fission (which produces neutrons and fission fragments).

Production: Produced only in specialized nuclear facilities with heavy ion accelerators. Not available outside research laboratories.

Quantity: Only atom quantities produced; insufficient for bulk handling or conventional chemical study.

Alpha Radiation: Most isotopes decay by alpha emission; external hazard minimal but internal hazard serious if ingested or inhaled.

Spontaneous Fission: Some isotopes undergo spontaneous fission producing neutrons and fission fragments requiring neutron shielding.

Daughter Products: Decay chains include other radioactive elements; secondary radiation hazards.

Handling: Atom-at-a-time techniques; remote handling essential. The extremely short half-lives require extremely fast experimental setups.

Containment: Produced and studied in sealed systems; strict containment protocols to prevent release.

Regulations: Subject to nuclear materials regulations despite minute quantities.

Waste: Radioactive waste from production experiments managed as nuclear waste.

First Aid: Not applicable due to production scale; standard radiation emergency procedures if contamination suspected.

Protective Equipment: Standard radiation laboratory protections. Neutron shielding may be required for spontaneous fission.

Storage: Not stored; atoms exist momentarily then decay.

Environmental: No environmental release; all material contained and decays in milliseconds.
