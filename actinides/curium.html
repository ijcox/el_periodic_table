Overview

Curium is a synthetic radioactive chemical element with the symbol Cm and atomic number 96. It is a hard, dense, silvery metal with a relatively high melting point. Curium is a member of the actinide series, named after Marie Curie and her husband Pierre Curie, both pioneers in the study of radioactivity. Like other actinides, curium is highly radioactive and must be handled with appropriate precautions.

The element was first intentionally produced and identified in 1944 by Glenn T. Seaborg, Ralph A. James, and Albert Ghiorso at the University of California, Berkeley. They bombarded plutonium-239 with alpha particles in the 60-inch cyclotron. Curium is primarily produced in nuclear reactors and has few practical applications outside scientific research, though it has been used as a power source in space missions.

Curium is a reactive metal. It reacts with oxygen, steam, and acids, but not with alkalis. The most common oxidation state is +3, in which curium compounds are stable in aqueous solutions. Curium(III) compounds are typically colorless or pale yellow. Curium can also exist in +4 oxidation state under strong oxidizing conditions. The chemistry of curium is similar to that of the lanthanides, particularly gadolinium.

Isotopes

Curium has no stable isotopes. The most important isotopes are:

  • Curium-247 (²⁴⁷Cm) - Half-life of 15.6 million years, alpha decay, most stable isotope
  • Curium-248 (²⁴⁸Cm) - Half-life of 348,000 years, alpha decay
  • Curium-250 (²⁵⁰Cm) - Half-life of 8,300 years, spontaneous fission
  • Curium-245 (²⁴⁵Cm) - Half-life of 8,500 years, alpha decay
  • Curium-242 (²⁴²Cm) - Half-life of 162.8 days, alpha decay
  • Curium-244 (²⁴⁴Cm) - Half-life of 18.1 years, alpha decay, used in alpha particle X-ray spectrometers
  • Curium-243 (²⁴³Cm) - Half-life of 29.1 years, alpha decay

Curium-242 and curium-244 are the most commonly produced isotopes in nuclear reactors. Curium-244 has been used as a power source in space missions.

Common Uses

  • Space Exploration - Curium-244 as alpha particle source in alpha particle X-ray spectrometers (APXS) on Mars rovers (Pathfinder, Spirit, Opportunity, Curiosity, Perseverance)
  • Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generators - Curium-244 considered as heat source for RTGs in space missions
  • Scientific Research - Production of heavier transuranium elements (berkelium, californium, etc.)
  • Neutron Sources - Curium-242/beryllium neutron sources for research and industrial applications
  • Medical Research - Potential use in radiotherapy (limited due to availability and handling difficulties)
  • Industrial Gauges - Thickness and density gauges using curium isotopes
  • Smoke Detectors - Historical use in some ionization smoke detectors (largely replaced by americium-241)

Curium's most significant application has been in space exploration, where alpha particle X-ray spectrometers containing curium-244 have analyzed Martian soil and rocks.

Biological Role

Curium has no biological role and is highly toxic:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • High Radiotoxicity - Alpha radiation from curium isotopes causes cellular damage
  • Bone Seeker - If internalized, accumulates in bones like other actinides
  • Liver Accumulation - Also accumulates in liver, though to lesser extent than bones
  • Low Environmental Exposure - Artificial element; not present in natural environment except from human activities
  • Research Settings Only - Only encountered in specialized nuclear facilities
  • Space Applications Safety - Curium-244 in space probes safely encapsulated; minimal risk during launch

Due to its artificial nature and controlled production, curium poses minimal biological risk to the general population.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Curium does not occur naturally in significant amounts:

  • Artificial Element - Produced artificially in nuclear reactors
  • Trace Amounts - Minute quantities possibly from natural neutron capture in uranium ores
  • Nuclear Accidents - Released in very small amounts in nuclear accidents

Artificial Production: Curium is produced in nuclear reactors:

  1. Neutron Irradiation - Americium or plutonium irradiated with neutrons:
    • ²⁴¹Am + n → ²⁴²Am (beta decay) → ²⁴²Cm
    • ²³⁹Pu + multiple n → various Cm isotopes
  2. Alpha Particle Bombardment - Plutonium-239 bombarded with alpha particles (original discovery method)
  3. Separation - Curium separated from other actinides by ion exchange or solvent extraction
  4. Purification - Further purification for specific applications
  5. Source Preparation - Curium oxide prepared and encapsulated for use in devices

Production Scale: Gram quantities produced, primarily in research reactors. Total world production estimated at kilograms.

Environmental Impact

Curium's environmental impact is limited but present:

  • Nuclear Weapons Testing - Atmospheric testing released curium isotopes in small amounts
  • Nuclear Accidents - Released in nuclear accidents, though in smaller amounts than other actinides
  • Space Missions - Curium-244 sources on space probes; potential release in launch accidents
  • Waste Management - Curium in spent nuclear fuel requires long-term management
  • Long Half-lives - Some isotopes have long half-lives (Cm-247: 15.6 million years)
  • Research Facilities - Potential releases from research facilities handling curium
  • Regulations - Curium regulated as radioactive material; strict disposal requirements

Given its artificial production and limited quantities, curium's environmental impact is much smaller than more abundant radioactive elements like plutonium or americium.

Discovery and History

Curium was first intentionally produced and identified in July 1944 by Glenn T. Seaborg, Ralph A. James, and Albert Ghiorso at the University of California, Berkeley. They bombarded plutonium-239 with alpha particles (helium ions) in the 60-inch cyclotron, producing curium-242 along with a free neutron.

The discovery was kept secret until after World War II due to its connection to the Manhattan Project. The element was named after Marie Curie and her husband Pierre Curie, pioneers in radioactivity research. This followed the tradition of naming elements after scientists (einsteinium, fermium, mendelevium, etc.).

The first isolation of curium in weighable amounts occurred in 1947. Curium metal was first prepared in 1951 by reduction of curium(III) fluoride with barium vapor. The longest-lived isotope, curium-247, was discovered later. Curium-244 found practical application in alpha particle X-ray spectrometers for space exploration beginning in the 1990s.

Interesting Facts

  • Curium is named after Marie Curie and Pierre Curie
  • Curium-244 has been used to analyze Martian soil on multiple Mars rovers
  • It glows in the dark with a pale blue light due to radioactive decay ionizing air
  • Curium was the third transuranium element to be discovered
  • The element is so radioactive that it visibly heats its surroundings
  • Curium-247 has a half-life of 15.6 million years - longer than many other transuranium elements
  • Only micrograms of curium were available until the 1950s
  • Curium compounds can have unusual colors, including pale yellow and green

Safety Information

High Radiotoxicity: Curium highly radioactive. Alpha emitter; also emits gamma radiation from decay products.

Internal Hazard: Particularly dangerous if internalized (inhaled, ingested). Accumulates in bones.

Heat Generation: Curium produces significant heat from radioactive decay; thermal management required.

Criticality Hazard: Some curium isotopes can achieve criticality with appropriate moderator.

Protective Equipment: Use in glove boxes with inert atmosphere. Full protective suits for significant quantities. Strict contamination control.

Radiation Protection: Lead shielding for gamma radiation. Remote handling for significant quantities.

Storage: Store in double-contained sealed containers with heat dissipation. Specialized facilities required.

Disposal: High-level radioactive waste. Deep geological disposal required for long-lived isotopes.

First Aid: For contamination: immediate decontamination, medical evaluation. Chelation therapy (DTPA) for internal contamination.

Regulations: Strictly regulated as radioactive material and nuclear material. Special licenses required.

Space Applications: Curium-244 sources on space probes designed to withstand launch accidents.
