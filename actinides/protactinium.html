Overview

Protactinium is a chemical element with the symbol Pa and atomic number 91. It is a dense, silvery-gray metal which readily reacts with oxygen, water vapor, and inorganic acids. Protactinium is one of the rarest and most expensive naturally occurring elements. It is found in minute quantities in uranium ores, as it is an intermediate decay product of uranium-235.

The element was first identified in 1913 by Kasimir Fajans and Oswald Helmuth Göhring, who named it "brevium" (from Latin "brevis," meaning brief) because of the short half-life of the specific isotope they found (protactinium-234). In 1917-1918, Lise Meitner and Otto Hahn, and independently Frederick Soddy and John Cranston, discovered a longer-lived isotope (protactinium-231) and named it "protactinium," meaning "parent of actinium" because it decays to actinium-227.

Protactinium exhibits four oxidation states (+2, +3, +4, +5) in aqueous solution, with +4 and +5 being the most common. The +5 state is strongly hydrolyzed and polymerizes easily, making protactinium chemistry complex. In its +5 state, protactinium shows chemical similarity to niobium and tantalum.

Isotopes

Protactinium has no stable isotopes. The most important isotopes are:

  • Protactinium-231 (²³¹Pa) - Half-life of 32,760 years, alpha decay to actinium-227, most stable isotope
  • Protactinium-234 (²³⁴Pa) - Two isomers:
    • ²³⁴ᵐPa (protactinium-234m) - Half-life of 1.17 minutes, beta decay to uranium-234
    • ²³⁴Pa (protactinium-234) - Half-life of 6.70 hours, beta decay to uranium-234
  • Protactinium-233 (²³³Pa) - Half-life of 26.967 days, beta decay to uranium-233, important in thorium fuel cycle
  • Protactinium-230 (²³⁰Pa) - Half-life of 17.4 days
  • Protactinium-232 (²³²Pa) - Half-life of 1.31 days

Protactinium-231 occurs naturally in the uranium-235 decay chain. Protactinium-233 is an important intermediate in the thorium fuel cycle, where it decays to fissile uranium-233.

Common Uses

Protactinium has very few practical applications due to its rarity, high radioactivity, and high cost:

  • Scientific Research - Study of actinide chemistry and nuclear physics
  • Geological Dating - Protactinium-231 used in uranium-thorium dating of marine sediments and corals
  • Nuclear Research - Study of thorium fuel cycle (protactinium-233 as intermediate)
  • Historical - Small quantities used in early research on radioactivity

In the 1960s, the UK Atomic Energy Authority produced 125 grams of 99.9% pure protactinium from 60 tons of waste material, which was the world's total supply for many years. This sample is now housed at the Harwell Laboratory in England.

Biological Role

Protactinium has no biological role and is highly toxic:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • High Radiotoxicity - All isotopes radioactive; alpha and beta emissions
  • Chemical Toxicity - Heavy metal toxicity similar to other actinides
  • Bone Seeker - If internalized, accumulates in bones like other actinides
  • Liver and Kidney Damage - Can cause damage to these organs
  • Extreme Rarity - Natural occurrence so low that environmental exposure negligible
  • Research Only - Only encountered in specialized research laboratories

Due to its extreme rarity and the small quantities involved in research, protactinium poses minimal biological risk outside controlled laboratory settings.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Protactinium is extremely rare:

  • Uranium Ores - Occurs in uranium ores as decay product of uranium-235
  • Abundance - About 0.3-3 parts per trillion in uranium ores
  • Natural Isotopes - Protactinium-231 (from uranium-235 decay) and protactinium-234 (from uranium-238 decay)
  • Total Earth's Crust - Estimated less than 500 grams at any time

Artificial Production: Protactinium is produced artificially:

  1. From Uranium Processing - Recovered as byproduct from uranium ore processing
  2. Neutron Irradiation - Thorium-232 irradiated with neutrons produces protactinium-233:
    • ²³²Th + n → ²³³Th (beta decay, half-life 22.3 minutes)
    • ²³³Th → ²³³Pa + e⁻
  3. Separation - Complex chemical separation from other elements using ion exchange or solvent extraction
  4. Purification - Multiple purification steps required due to chemical similarity to other elements

Production Scale: Very small quantities (grams total worldwide). Mostly produced for research purposes.

Environmental Impact

Protactinium has negligible environmental impact due to its extreme rarity:

  • Natural Background - Trace amounts from uranium decay; negligible environmental impact
  • Production Impact - Produced in minute quantities at controlled facilities
  • No Industrial Use - No commercial applications that could cause environmental release
  • Research Safety - Produced and studied under controlled laboratory conditions
  • Waste Management - Generated in such small amounts that waste is negligible
  • Regulatory Status - Regulated as radioactive material but poses minimal risk

Given that the total amount of protactinium in Earth's crust is less than 500 grams, its environmental impact is essentially zero.

Discovery and History

Protactinium was first identified in 1913 by Kasimir Fajans and Oswald Helmuth Göhring at the University of Karlsruhe in Germany. They discovered the short-lived isotope protactinium-234 (then called "brevium") during their studies of the uranium decay series. The name "brevium" emphasized the brief half-life of this isotope.

In 1917-1918, two groups independently discovered the longer-lived isotope protactinium-231. Lise Meitner and Otto Hahn at the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute in Berlin, and Frederick Soddy and John Cranston in Glasgow, Scotland, both isolated this isotope. They named it "protactinium," meaning "parent of actinium," because it decays to actinium-227.

For many years, only minute quantities of protactinium were available. In 1927, Aristid V. Grosse prepared the first sample of protactinium oxide. In 1934, he prepared the first sample of metallic protactinium. The largest amount ever produced was 125 grams of 99.9% pure protactinium isolated in 1961 by the UK Atomic Energy Authority from 60 tons of waste material.

Interesting Facts

  • Protactinium is one of the rarest and most expensive naturally occurring elements
  • The name means "parent of actinium" because it decays to actinium
  • Only about 125 grams have ever been isolated in pure form
  • It was discovered twice, with two different names (brevium and protactinium)
  • Protactinium is more scarce in Earth's crust than gold, platinum, or even astatine
  • Lise Meitner, co-discoverer of protactinium-231, was the first woman to become a full professor of physics in Germany
  • The world's supply of protactinium was once valued at about $500,000 per gram
  • It sits between thorium and uranium in the periodic table, both in position and properties

Safety Information

High Radiotoxicity: Protactinium highly radioactive. Protactinium-231 alpha emitter with 32,760 year half-life.

Internal Hazard: Particularly dangerous if internalized. Accumulates in bones like other actinides.

Chemical Toxicity: Heavy metal toxicity similar to other actinides.

Extreme Rarity: Natural abundance so low that exposure risk is negligible outside laboratories.

Research Hazards: Only hazard in research settings where artificially produced or isolated.

Protective Equipment: Use in fume hoods or glove boxes. Wear gloves, lab coat, safety glasses. Consider respiratory protection for powders.

Radiation Protection: Lead shielding for gamma radiation from decay products.

Storage: Store in sealed containers with adequate shielding. Label clearly as radioactive.

Disposal: High-level radioactive waste. Special disposal facilities required.

First Aid: For contamination: remove contaminated clothing, wash skin thoroughly, seek medical attention.

Regulations: Strictly regulated as radioactive material. Special licenses required for possession.
