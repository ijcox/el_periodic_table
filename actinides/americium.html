Overview

Americium is a synthetic radioactive chemical element with the symbol Am and atomic number 95. It is a transuranic member of the actinide series, located in the periodic table under the lanthanide element europium, and thus named after the Americas, by analogy with europium being named after Europe. Americium is a silvery-white metal that tarnishes slowly in air.

The element was first produced in 1944 by Glenn T. Seaborg, Ralph A. James, Leon O. Morgan, and Albert Ghiorso at the Metallurgical Laboratory (now Argonne National Laboratory) at the University of Chicago. They bombarded plutonium-239 with neutrons to produce americium-241. Americium is used in commercial ionization smoke detectors and as a source of alpha particles for various industrial gauges.

Americium is quite electropositive and reacts with oxygen, steam, and acids, but not with alkalis. The most common oxidation state is +3, in which americium compounds are stable in aqueous solutions. Americium(III) compounds are typically pink or rose-colored in solution. Americium can also exist in +2, +4, +5, +6, and +7 oxidation states under specific conditions.

Isotopes

Americium has no stable isotopes. The most important isotopes are:

  • Americium-241 (²⁴¹Am) - Half-life of 432.2 years, alpha decay to neptunium-237, most commonly used isotope
  • Americium-243 (²⁴³Am) - Half-life of 7,370 years, alpha decay, most stable isotope
  • Americium-242 (²⁴²Am) - Two isomers:
    • ²⁴²ᵐAm - Half-life of 141 years
    • ²⁴²Am - Half-life of 16.02 hours
  • Americium-240 (²⁴⁰Am) - Half-life of 50.8 hours
  • Americium-239 (²³⁹Am) - Half-life of 11.9 hours

Americium-241 is the most commonly encountered isotope, produced from the beta decay of plutonium-241. It is used in smoke detectors and various industrial devices.

Common Uses

  • Smoke Detectors - Americium-241 in ionization smoke detectors (about 0.3 micrograms per detector)
  • Industrial Gauges - Thickness, density, and level gauges using americium-241 as alpha source
  • Neutron Sources - Americium-241/beryllium neutron sources for well logging, research, and industrial applications
  • Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generators - Potential heat source for space missions (americium-241)
  • Medical Diagnostics - Historical use in bone densitometry (largely replaced by other techniques)
  • Research - Source of alpha particles in scientific research
  • Glass Tinting - Historical use to produce green or yellow tint in glass (discontinued)
  • Moisture Gauges - For measuring moisture content in soil and building materials

Americium-241 is the most widely used isotope, with millions of smoke detectors containing this isotope in homes worldwide.

Biological Role

Americium has no biological role and is toxic:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • Radiotoxicity - Alpha radiation from americium-241 causes cellular damage
  • Bone Seeker - If internalized, accumulates in bones, particularly on bone surfaces
  • Liver Accumulation - Also accumulates in liver, though to lesser extent than bones
  • Low Risk from Smoke Detectors - Amount in smoke detectors (0.3 μg) poses minimal risk if contained
  • Industrial Exposure - Workers in facilities handling americium require monitoring and protection
  • Environmental Presence - Trace amounts from nuclear weapons testing and nuclear accidents
  • Treatment - Chelation therapy (DTPA) can remove americium from body if administered promptly

While americium is radioactive, the tiny amounts used in consumer products like smoke detectors pose negligible health risk when properly contained.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Americium does not occur naturally in significant amounts:

  • Artificial Element - Produced artificially in nuclear reactors
  • Trace Amounts - Minute quantities from atmospheric nuclear weapons testing
  • Nuclear Accidents - Released in small amounts in nuclear accidents

Artificial Production: Americium is produced in nuclear reactors:

  1. From Plutonium - Plutonium-241 beta decays to americium-241:
    • ²⁴¹Pu → ²⁴¹Am + e⁻ (beta decay, half-life 14.3 years)
  2. Neutron Irradiation - Plutonium-239 or -240 irradiated with neutrons produces americium
  3. Separation - Americium separated from plutonium and other actinides by chemical processes
  4. Purification - Further purification for specific applications
  5. Source Preparation - Americium oxide prepared and encapsulated for use in devices

Production Scale: Kilogram quantities produced, primarily as americium-241 for smoke detectors and industrial devices.

Environmental Impact

Americium's environmental impact is relatively limited but present:

  • Smoke Detector Disposal - Millions of smoke detectors containing americium-241 require proper disposal
  • Nuclear Weapons Testing - Atmospheric testing released americium-241 globally
  • Nuclear Accidents - Released in nuclear accidents, though in smaller amounts than other actinides
  • Waste Management - Americium in spent nuclear fuel requires long-term management
  • Industrial Releases - Potential releases from facilities producing or using americium sources
  • Regulations - Americium-241 regulated as radioactive material; disposal requirements vary by country
  • Recycling - Some programs recover americium from discarded smoke detectors

The primary environmental concern is proper disposal of smoke detectors containing americium-241 to prevent release into the environment.

Discovery and History

Americium was first produced in 1944 by Glenn T. Seaborg, Ralph A. James, Leon O. Morgan, and Albert Ghiorso at the University of Chicago's Metallurgical Laboratory (part of the Manhattan Project). They bombarded plutonium-239 with neutrons in a nuclear reactor, producing plutonium-240, then plutonium-241, which beta decays to americium-241.

The discovery was kept secret until after World War II and was announced in 1945. The element was named "americium" after the Americas, by analogy with its periodic table homolog europium, which was named after Europe. The name also recognized the Americas' contribution to the Manhattan Project.

The first isolation of americium in pure compound form occurred in 1945. Weighable amounts (40-200 micrograms) were isolated in late 1945. Americium metal was first prepared in 1951 by reduction of americium(III) fluoride with barium vapor. Commercial production of americium-241 for smoke detectors began in the 1970s.

Interesting Facts

  • Americium is named after the Americas, by analogy with europium (Europe)
  • It's the only synthetic element found in most households (in smoke detectors)
  • Americium-241 produces about 0.1 watts of heat per gram from radioactive decay
  • The element glows blue in the dark due to ionization of air by alpha particles
  • Americium was the fourth transuranium element discovered
  • Each smoke detector contains about 0.3 micrograms of americium-241
  • Americium can be used to produce other elements, including curium and berkelium
  • The first sample of americium large enough to be visible was produced in 1945

Safety Information

Radiotoxicity: Americium radioactive. Americium-241 alpha emitter with 432-year half-life.

Internal Hazard: Dangerous if internalized (inhaled, ingested). Accumulates in bones.

Smoke Detector Safety: Americium in smoke detectors safely encapsulated; poses minimal risk if not tampered with.

Disposal: Smoke detectors should not be disassembled. Check local regulations for disposal.

Industrial Sources: Industrial gauges containing americium require proper handling and disposal.

Protective Equipment: Use gloves, lab coat, safety glasses when handling americium sources. Respiratory protection for powders.

Storage: Store in sealed containers. Label clearly as radioactive.

Disposal of Sources: Radioactive waste. Dispose according to radioactive waste regulations.

First Aid: For contamination: remove contaminated clothing, wash skin thoroughly, seek medical attention. Chelation therapy (DTPA) effective for internal contamination.

Regulations: Regulated as radioactive material. Special licenses required for significant quantities.

General Public: Smoke detectors pose minimal risk when used as intended. Do not disassemble.
