Overview

Mendelevium is a synthetic radioactive chemical element with the symbol Md and atomic number 101. It is a member of the actinide series and was named after Dmitri Mendeleev, the Russian chemist who created the Periodic Table of Elements. Mendelevium was the first element to be synthesized one atom at a time, marking a new era in element discovery.

The element was first produced in 1955 by Albert Ghiorso, Bernard G. Harvey, Gregory R. Choppin, Stanley G. Thompson, and Glenn T. Seaborg at the University of California, Berkeley. They bombarded einsteinium-253 with alpha particles in the 60-inch cyclotron. Mendelevium is highly radioactive and has only been produced in minute quantities, with no practical applications outside basic scientific research.

Mendelevium exhibits oxidation states +1, +2, and +3 in compounds, with +3 being the most stable and common. Mendelevium(III) is the predominant state in aqueous solution. Interestingly, mendelevium can be reduced to the +2 state more easily than other actinides, which has been used for its chemical separation. Due to its intense radioactivity, short half-lives, and minute available quantities, chemical studies of mendelevium are extremely limited.

Isotopes

Mendelevium has no stable isotopes. The most important isotopes are:

  • Mendelevium-258 (²⁵⁸Md) - Half-life of 51.5 days, electron capture and alpha decay, most stable isotope
  • Mendelevium-260 (²⁶⁰Md) - Half-life of 31.8 days, alpha decay and spontaneous fission
  • Mendelevium-257 (²⁵⁷Md) - Half-life of 5.52 hours, electron capture and alpha decay
  • Mendelevium-256 (²⁵⁶Md) - Half-life of 1.17 hours, electron capture and alpha decay
  • Mendelevium-255 (²⁵⁵Md) - Half-life of 27 minutes, electron capture and alpha decay
  • Mendelevium-259 (²⁵⁹Md) - Half-life of 1.60 hours, spontaneous fission

Mendelevium-258 is the longest-lived isotope. Most mendelevium isotopes have half-lives of minutes to hours, making chemical and physical studies extremely challenging. The short half-lives also mean that mendelevium must be studied immediately after production.

  • Nuclear Physics - Study of nuclear decay processes and heavy element synthesis
  • Chemical Studies - Investigation of +2 oxidation state in actinides

Mendelevium's importance is purely scientific. Its discovery marked the transition from discovering elements in nuclear debris or reactors to synthesizing them one atom at a time in particle accelerators. Studies of mendelevium have provided insights into the stability of heavy nuclei and the chemistry of the actinides.

  • Not in Nature - Does not occur in nature even in trace amounts

Artificial Production: Mendelevium is produced one atom at a time:

    • ²⁵³Es + ⁴He → ²⁵⁶Md + n (original discovery reaction)
  2. Alternative Methods - Bombardment of other actinide targets with light ions

Production Scale: Single atoms produced. Total world production is measured in atoms, not mass. In the original experiment, only 17 atoms of mendelevium-256 were produced. Modern experiments might produce a few hundred atoms at most.

Discovery and History

Mendelevium was first produced on February 19, 1955, by Albert Ghiorso, Bernard G. Harvey, Gregory R. Choppin, Stanley G. Thompson, and Glenn T. Seaborg at the University of California, Berkeley. They bombarded einsteinium-253 with alpha particles in the 60-inch cyclotron, producing mendelevium-256.

The experiment was groundbreaking because it represented the first time an element was synthesized one atom at a time. The team produced only 17 atoms of mendelevium-256, which has a half-life of 76 minutes. They used a new technique called the "recoil method" where newly formed atoms recoiled out of the target onto a catcher foil, allowing rapid chemical separation.

The element was named "mendelevium" after Dmitri Mendeleev, creator of the periodic table. The name was controversial initially because Mendeleev was Russian and this was during the Cold War, but it was eventually accepted. Mendelevium was the first element discovered using single-atom techniques, paving the way for discovery of heavier elements.
