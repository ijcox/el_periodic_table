Overview

Californium is a synthetic radioactive chemical element with the symbol Cf and atomic number 98. It is an actinide element, named after the state of California and the University of California, where it was first synthesized in 1950. Californium is a silvery-white metal that is relatively stable in air at room temperature but reacts with oxygen, steam, and acids.

The element was first produced by Stanley G. Thompson, Kenneth Street Jr., Albert Ghiorso, and Glenn T. Seaborg at the University of California, Berkeley. They bombarded curium-242 with alpha particles in the 60-inch cyclotron. Californium is primarily produced in nuclear reactors and has practical applications as a neutron source, particularly in neutron activation analysis and in nuclear reactors as a startup source.

Californium exhibits oxidation states +2, +3, and +4 in compounds, with +3 being the most stable and common. Californium(III) compounds are green in solution. The element reacts with oxygen, steam, and acids, but is relatively stable in dry air at room temperature. Californium's chemistry is similar to that of the other actinides and its lanthanide homolog dysprosium.

Isotopes

Californium has no stable isotopes. The most important isotopes are:

  • Californium-251 (²⁵¹Cf) - Half-life of 898 years, alpha decay, most stable isotope
  • Californium-249 (²⁴⁹Cf) - Half-life of 351 years, alpha decay
  • Californium-250 (²⁵⁰Cf) - Half-life of 13.08 years, alpha decay and spontaneous fission
  • Californium-252 (²⁵²Cf) - Half-life of 2.645 years, alpha decay and spontaneous fission (3% of decays), important neutron source
  • Californium-248 (²⁴⁸Cf) - Half-life of 333.5 days, alpha decay
  • Californium-254 (²⁵⁴Cf) - Half-life of 60.5 days, spontaneous fission

Californium-252 is particularly important due to its high spontaneous fission rate, making it a compact and intense neutron source. It emits an average of 3.7 neutrons per spontaneous fission.

Common Uses

  • Neutron Source - Californium-252 as portable neutron source for neutron activation analysis, neutron radiography, and well logging
  • Nuclear Reactor Startup - Neutron sources to initiate chain reaction in nuclear reactors
  • Moisture Gauges - Measuring moisture content in soil and construction materials
  • Neutron Radiography - Non-destructive testing of aircraft components, explosives, and other materials
  • Cancer Treatment - Californium-252 neutron brachytherapy for certain cancers (experimental)
  • Elemental Analysis - Neutron activation analysis for detecting trace elements
  • Security Applications - Neutron sources for detection of explosives and illicit materials
  • Scientific Research - Production of heavier elements (einsteinium, fermium, etc.)

Californium-252's ability to emit large numbers of neutrons from a small volume makes it valuable for various industrial and medical applications where portable neutron sources are needed.

Biological Role

Californium has no biological role and is highly toxic:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • High Radiotoxicity - Alpha radiation and neutron emission (from californium-252)
  • Neutron Radiation Hazard - Californium-252 emits neutrons, which are particularly penetrating and hazardous
  • Bone Seeker - If internalized, accumulates in bones like other actinides
  • Medical Use - Californium-252 investigated for neutron brachytherapy cancer treatment
  • Controlled Use - Only handled in specialized facilities with appropriate shielding

Californium's neutron emission presents unique hazards requiring specialized shielding (water, paraffin, or concrete for neutrons in addition to lead for gamma rays).

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Californium does not occur naturally:

  • Artificial Element - Produced artificially in nuclear reactors
  • Trace Amounts - Possibly formed in supernova explosions but not found on Earth

Artificial Production: Californium is produced in high-flux nuclear reactors:

  1. Neutron Irradiation - Successive neutron capture on plutonium, americium, curium, and berkelium:
    • ²³⁹Pu → multiple neutron captures → ²⁴²Pu → ²⁴³Am → ²⁴⁴Cm → ²⁴⁵Cm → ²⁴⁶Cm → ²⁴⁷Bk → ²⁴⁹Bk → ²⁴⁹Cf
  2. Separation - Californium separated from other actinides by ion exchange chromatography
  3. Source Preparation - Californium-252 encapsulated in double-walled stainless steel capsules for neutron sources
  4. Purification - Multiple purification steps due to chemical similarity to other actinides

Production Scale: Milligram to gram quantities produced. Primary production at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (USA) and Research Institute of Atomic Reactors (Russia). World production of californium-252 is about 500 milligrams per year.

Environmental Impact

Californium's environmental impact is limited but present:

  • Industrial Use - Neutron sources used in various industries; potential for accidental release
  • Waste Management - Californium in spent nuclear fuel and from source production requires disposal
  • Accidents - Potential release in accidents involving neutron sources (transportation, industrial use)
  • Long Half-lives - Some isotopes have long half-lives (Cf-251: 898 years)
  • Regulations - Strictly regulated as radioactive material; special transportation requirements for neutron sources
  • Shielding Requirements - Neutron sources require specialized shielding and handling

While californium is produced in small quantities, its use as a neutron source in various industries means it is more widely distributed than most other transuranium elements.

Discovery and History

Californium was first produced on February 9, 1950, by Stanley G. Thompson, Kenneth Street Jr., Albert Ghiorso, and Glenn T. Seaborg at the University of California, Berkeley. They bombarded a microgram-sized target of curium-242 with 35 MeV alpha particles in the 60-inch cyclotron, producing californium-245 (half-life 44 minutes) along with a free neutron.

The discovery was announced in March 1950. The element was named after the state of California and the University of California, continuing the American place-name tradition started with americium. The first weighable amounts of californium (a few micrograms) were produced in 1958 by prolonged neutron irradiation of plutonium in the Materials Testing Reactor in Idaho.

Californium-252's value as a neutron source was recognized in the 1960s, leading to its production for industrial and medical applications. The High Flux Isotope Reactor at Oak Ridge National Laboratory became the primary production facility for californium and other transuranium elements.

Interesting Facts

  • Californium is named after the state of California
  • Californium-252 is one of the few materials that emits neutrons spontaneously at room temperature
  • One microgram of californium-252 emits 170 million neutrons per minute
  • It's one of the most expensive elements - approximately $27 million per gram for californium-252
  • Californium was the sixth transuranium element to be discovered
  • The element has been used in portable metal detectors to find land mines and gold
  • Californium-252 neutron sources are about the size of a small pill but require extensive shielding
  • Only about 8 grams of californium-252 are produced worldwide each year

Safety Information

High Radiotoxicity: Californium highly radioactive. Alpha emitter; californium-252 also emits neutrons.

Neutron Radiation: Californium-252 emits 2.3×10⁶ neutrons per second per microgram. Requires special shielding (water, paraffin, concrete).

Internal Hazard: Particularly dangerous if internalized. Accumulates in bones.

Criticality Hazard: Californium-252 can achieve criticality with appropriate moderator; mass limits apply.

Heat Generation: Californium-252 produces about 39 watts of heat per gram.

Protective Equipment: Use in specially shielded facilities. Neutron shielding required in addition to gamma shielding.

Storage: Store in water-filled pools or specially designed containers with neutron shielding.

Transportation: Special Type B containers required for transportation; strictly regulated.

Disposal: High-level radioactive waste. Deep geological disposal required.

First Aid: For contamination: immediate decontamination, medical evaluation. Chelation therapy for internal contamination.

Regulations: Strictly regulated as radioactive material and nuclear material. Special licenses required.
