Overview

Thorium is a weakly radioactive metallic chemical element with the symbol Th and atomic number 90. It is a silvery metal that tarnishes black when exposed to air, forming thorium dioxide. Thorium is moderately hard, malleable, and has a high melting point. It is about three times more abundant than uranium in the Earth's crust and is chiefly refined from monazite sands.

Thorium was discovered in 1828 by Norwegian mineralogist Morten Thrane Esmark and identified by Swedish chemist Jöns Jacob Berzelius, who named it after Thor, the Norse god of thunder. All known thorium isotopes are unstable. The most stable isotope, thorium-232, has a half-life of 14.05 billion years, about three times the age of the Earth.

Thorium is relatively reactive. It reacts slowly with water but does not dissolve rapidly in most common acids, except hydrochloric acid. Powdered thorium metal is pyrophoric and will ignite spontaneously in air. Thorium dioxide (thoria) is one of the most refractory materials known, with a melting point of 3300°C.

Isotopes

Thorium has several isotopes, but only one is significantly abundant in nature:

  • Thorium-232 (²³²Th) - Half-life of 14.05 billion years, alpha decay, essentially stable, 100% natural abundance
  • Thorium-228 (²²⁸Th) - Half-life of 1.9116 years, alpha decay, part of thorium decay chain
  • Thorium-230 (²³⁰Th) - Half-life of 75,380 years, alpha decay, part of uranium decay chain
  • Thorium-229 (²²⁹Th) - Half-life of 7,340 years, alpha decay
  • Thorium-227 (²²⁷Th) - Half-life of 18.68 days, alpha decay
  • Thorium-234 (²³⁴Th) - Half-life of 24.10 days, beta decay, part of uranium decay chain

Thorium-232 is the parent of the thorium decay series, which eventually ends at stable lead-208. This decay series includes radium-228, actinium-228, thorium-228, radium-224, radon-220, polonium-216, lead-212, bismuth-212, polonium-212, and thallium-208.

Common Uses

  • Nuclear Fuel - Thorium fuel cycle in nuclear reactors (experimental and developing)
  • Gas Mantles - Thorium dioxide in gas lantern mantles for bright white light (being phased out)
  • Alloying Agent - Added to magnesium alloys to improve high-temperature strength
  • Welding Electrodes - Thorium in tungsten electrodes for TIG welding (being replaced by alternatives)
  • Catalysts - Thorium dioxide as catalyst in petroleum refining and organic chemistry
  • High-Temperature Ceramics - Thorium dioxide ceramics for crucibles and high-temperature applications
  • Optical Glass - Thorium in high-quality camera lenses and scientific instruments (historical)
  • Light Bulb Filaments - Historical use in incandescent light bulbs
  • Research - Thorium reactors research for potentially safer nuclear power
  • Medical Imaging - Thorium dioxide formerly used as contrast agent (Thorotrast, discontinued)

Biological Role

Thorium has no known biological role and is toxic due to both chemical and radioactive properties:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • Chemical Toxicity - Thorium compounds toxic to liver and other organs
  • Radioactive Toxicity - Alpha radiation from thorium and decay products
  • Thorotrast - Thorium dioxide suspension used as X-ray contrast agent 1930s-1950s; caused cancers decades later
  • Bone Seeker - Like other actinides, accumulates in bones if internalized
  • Liver Accumulation - Particularly affects liver where it can cause fibrosis and cancer
  • Lung Hazard - Inhalation of thorium dust can cause lung cancer
  • Environmental Exposure - Naturally present in environment; normal exposure very low

The Thorotrast tragedy demonstrated the dangers of internal thorium exposure, with increased rates of liver cancer and leukemia appearing decades after exposure.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Thorium is relatively abundant (9.6 ppm in Earth's crust):

  • Monazite - Primary ore (3-12% thorium oxide), rare earth phosphate mineral
  • Thorite - Thorium silicate (ThSiO₄)
  • Thorianite - Thorium oxide (ThO₂) with uranium
  • Beach Sands - Monazite concentrated in beach sands by wave action
  • Major Reserves - India, Brazil, Australia, United States, Egypt, Turkey

Industrial Production: Thorium extraction from monazite:

  1. Mining - Monazite sands mined, usually by dredging or surface mining
  2. Concentration - Magnetic and gravity separation to concentrate monazite
  3. Digestion - Monazite treated with hot concentrated sulfuric acid or sodium hydroxide
  4. Separation - Thorium separated from rare earths by solvent extraction or precipitation
  5. Purification - Further purification steps to obtain thorium compounds
  6. Metal Production - Thorium metal produced by reduction of thorium tetrachloride with magnesium

Global Production: About 2,000 tonnes annually. India is largest producer and has largest reserves.

Environmental Impact

Thorium mining and use have several environmental considerations:

  • Radioactive Waste - Mining and processing generate radioactive waste containing thorium and decay products
  • Radon Emission - Thorium decay produces radon-220 (thoron), though shorter half-life than radon-222
  • Legacy Contamination - Historical use sites (gas mantle factories, Thorotrast production) contaminated
  • Nuclear Waste - Thorium reactor waste different from uranium reactors; proponents claim advantages
  • Mining Impact - Monazite mining disturbs coastal and river environments
  • Alternative Energy - Thorium reactors proposed as cleaner nuclear option with less long-lived waste
  • Regulation - Thorium and its compounds regulated as radioactive materials
  • Natural Background - Naturally present in environment; contributes to natural background radiation

Thorium's environmental impact is primarily associated with its radioactivity and the legacy of historical uses, though proponents argue thorium nuclear power could have environmental advantages over uranium.

Discovery and History

Thorium was discovered in 1828 by Norwegian mineralogist Morten Thrane Esmark, who found a black mineral on the island of Løvøy in Norway. He sent it to his father, Jens Esmark, a noted mineralogist, who determined it was unknown. The sample was sent to Swedish chemist Jöns Jacob Berzelius for examination.

Berzelius isolated a new element from the mineral and named it "thorium" after Thor, the Norse god of thunder. He published his findings in 1829. The mineral was named "thorite." For many years, thorium had few practical applications until the invention of the gas mantle by Carl Auer von Welsbach in 1885.

In the 20th century, thorium's potential as a nuclear fuel was recognized. Glenn T. Seaborg proposed the thorium fuel cycle in the 1940s. India, with large thorium reserves but limited uranium, has pursued thorium reactor development since the 1950s. The tragic health effects of Thorotrast contrast agent, used from the 1930s to 1950s, demonstrated thorium's radiotoxicity.

Safety Information

Radioactivity: Thorium and compounds radioactive. Thorium-232 alpha emitter with long half-life.

Internal Hazard: Dangerous if internalized (inhaled, ingested). Accumulates in bones and liver.

Chemical Toxicity: Thorium compounds chemically toxic, particularly to liver.

Powder Hazard: Thorium powder pyrophoric - ignites spontaneously in air.

Historical Tragedy: Thorotrast (thorium dioxide contrast agent) caused cancers decades after use.

Radon Hazard: Thorium decay produces radon-220 (thoron), though shorter-lived than radon-222.

Protective Equipment: Use gloves, lab coat, safety glasses. Respiratory protection for dust. Lead shielding for gamma radiation.

Storage: Store in sealed containers. Separate from flammable materials due to pyrophoric nature.

Disposal: Radioactive waste. Dispose according to radioactive waste regulations.

First Aid: For inhalation, move to fresh air. For skin contact, wash with soap and water. For eye contact, flush with water for 15 minutes. For ingestion, seek medical attention.

Regulations: Regulated as radioactive material. NRC regulations apply in United States.
