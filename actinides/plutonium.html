Overview

Plutonium is a radioactive actinide metal with the symbol Pu and atomic number 94. It is a silvery-gray metal that tarnishes when exposed to air, forming a dull coating. Plutonium is primarily known for its use in nuclear weapons and as fuel in nuclear reactors. The element exhibits unusual physical properties, including six allotropes (crystal structures) at atmospheric pressure and a negative temperature coefficient of resistivity.

Plutonium was first produced and isolated in 1940 by a team led by Glenn T. Seaborg and Edwin McMillan at the University of California, Berkeley. They bombarded uranium-238 with deuterons. The element was named after Pluto, the dwarf planet, continuing the trend of naming elements after planets (neptunium for Neptune, plutonium for Pluto).

Plutonium has six allotropes at atmospheric pressure, more than any other element. The delta phase (δ) is ductile, while the alpha phase (α) is brittle. Plutonium expands when it solidifies from the melt, unlike most metals.

Plutonium is a reactive metal. It reacts with oxygen, forming PuO and PuO₂. It reacts with halogens, giving trihalides or tetrahalides. Plutonium dissolves in concentrated hydrochloric, hydroiodic, and perchloric acids. The metal is pyrophoric, meaning finely divided plutonium can ignite spontaneously at room temperature.

Isotopes

Plutonium has no stable isotopes. The most important isotopes are:

  • Plutonium-239 (²³⁹Pu) - Half-life of 24,110 years, fissile, primary isotope for nuclear weapons and reactor fuel
  • Plutonium-238 (²³⁸Pu) - Half-life of 87.7 years, alpha emitter, used in radioisotope thermoelectric generators
  • Plutonium-240 (²⁴⁰Pu) - Half-life of 6,561 years, spontaneous fission, affects weapon design
  • Plutonium-242 (²⁴²Pu) - Half-life of 373,300 years
  • Plutonium-244 (²⁴⁴Pu) - Half-life of 80.8 million years, longest-lived isotope, primordial (exists in nature)
  • Plutonium-241 (²⁴¹Pu) - Half-life of 14.3 years, beta decay to americium-241

Plutonium-239 is the most significant isotope, produced in nuclear reactors from uranium-238 via neutron capture and beta decay. Plutonium-238 is used as a heat source in space probes.

Common Uses

  • Nuclear Weapons - Plutonium-239 as fissile material in atomic bombs (Fat Man, Trinity test)
  • Nuclear Power - Mixed oxide (MOX) fuel in nuclear reactors (plutonium with uranium)
  • Spacecraft Power - Plutonium-238 in radioisotope thermoelectric generators (RTGs) for deep space missions
  • Radioisotope Heater Units - Plutonium-238 to keep spacecraft components warm
  • Neutron Sources - Plutonium-beryllium neutron sources for oil well logging and research
  • Smoke Detectors - Some ionization smoke detectors contain plutonium-239 (largely replaced by americium-241)
  • Research - Study of actinide chemistry and nuclear physics
  • Nuclear Waste - Component of spent nuclear fuel; subject of reprocessing and transmutation research

Biological Role

Plutonium has no biological role and is highly toxic:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • High Radiotoxicity - Alpha radiation from plutonium causes intense localized damage
  • Bone Seeker - If internalized, accumulates on bone surfaces, irradiating bone marrow
  • Liver Accumulation - Also accumulates in liver, causing damage
  • Lung Cancer Risk - Inhalation of plutonium dust can cause lung cancer
  • Historical Exposures - Workers at nuclear facilities (e.g., Rocky Flats, Mayak) experienced health effects
  • Environmental Contamination - Nuclear weapons testing, accidents (e.g., Chernobyl, Fukushima) released plutonium
  • Half-life in Body - Biological half-life in bones about 50 years; effectively permanent

Plutonium is considered one of the most toxic substances known. Microgram quantities can cause cancer if internalized.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Plutonium occurs in trace amounts in nature:

  • Uranium Ores - Formed by neutron capture on uranium-238 from spontaneous fission or cosmic rays
  • Plutonium-244 - Primordial isotope present since Earth's formation; extremely rare
  • Nuclear Fallout - Global distribution from atmospheric nuclear testing

Artificial Production: Plutonium is produced in nuclear reactors:

  1. Neutron Capture - Uranium-238 captures neutrons:
    • ²³⁸U + n → ²³⁹U (beta decay, half-life 23.5 minutes)
    • ²³⁹U → ²³⁹Np + e⁻ (beta decay, half-life 2.356 days)
    • ²³⁹Np → ²³⁹Pu + e⁻
  2. Separation - Plutonium separated from spent nuclear fuel by PUREX process or other methods
  3. Purification - Further purification for specific applications
  4. Weapons-grade - High purity plutonium-239 with low plutonium-240 content

Global Inventory: About 500 tonnes of civilian plutonium and 250 tonnes of military plutonium worldwide.

Environmental Impact

Plutonium has significant environmental impacts:

  • Nuclear Weapons Testing - Atmospheric testing dispersed plutonium globally
  • Nuclear Accidents - Released in major accidents (Chernobyl, Fukushima, Kyshtym)
  • Weapons Production - Contamination at production sites (Hanford, Rocky Flats, Mayak)
  • Long-lived Waste - Plutonium-239 half-life 24,110 years; requires secure disposal for millennia
  • MOX Fuel - Use of plutonium in reactor fuel reduces stockpiles but creates waste management issues
  • Spacecraft Safety - RTG launches risk release in launch accidents
  • Environmental Mobility - Plutonium(IV) forms insoluble compounds; relatively immobile in environment
  • Bioaccumulation - Can accumulate in food chain, though limited due to low solubility

Plutonium's environmental persistence and toxicity make it a major concern for nuclear waste management and environmental cleanup.

Discovery and History

Plutonium was first synthesized in 1940 by a team led by Glenn T. Seaborg, Edwin McMillan, Joseph W. Kennedy, and Arthur Wahl at the University of California, Berkeley. They bombarded uranium-238 with deuterons in the 60-inch cyclotron, producing neptunium-238, which decayed to plutonium-238.

Plutonium-239, the fissile isotope important for nuclear weapons, was discovered in 1941. The discovery was kept secret due to World War II. The first industrial production of plutonium began at the Hanford Site in Washington state as part of the Manhattan Project. The first atomic bomb test (Trinity, July 16, 1945) used plutonium, as did the Nagasaki bomb (Fat Man, August 9, 1945).

After the war, plutonium production continued for nuclear weapons during the Cold War. Civilian uses developed later, including nuclear power and space exploration. Glenn Seaborg received the 1951 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for his work on transuranium elements including plutonium.

Interesting Facts

  • Plutonium has more allotropes (crystal structures) than any other element
  • It expands when it solidifies, unlike most metals
  • Plutonium is warm to the touch due to radioactive decay
  • The delta phase of plutonium has a negative temperature coefficient of resistivity
  • Plutonium-238 powered the Voyager, Cassini, and New Horizons spacecraft
  • The Fat Man bomb dropped on Nagasaki contained 6.2 kg of plutonium
  • Plutonium can form at least eight different oxidation states in solution
  • The first sample of plutonium large enough to be visible was produced in 1942

Safety Information

Extreme Radiotoxicity: Plutonium one of most toxic substances known. Alpha radiation causes intense damage.

Internal Hazard: Particularly dangerous if internalized (inhaled, ingested). Microgram quantities can cause cancer.

Pyrophoric: Finely divided plutonium ignites spontaneously in air.

Criticality Hazard: Plutonium-239 can achieve nuclear criticality; strict mass and geometry controls required.

Chemical Toxicity: Heavy metal toxicity, though radiotoxicity is greater concern.

Protective Equipment: Use in glove boxes with inert atmosphere. Full protective suits for significant quantities. Strict contamination control.

Storage: Store in double-contained sealed containers. Specialized facilities with criticality controls.

Disposal: High-level radioactive waste. Deep geological disposal required.

First Aid: For contamination: immediate decontamination, medical evaluation. Chelation therapy (DTPA) for internal contamination.

Regulations: Strictly controlled as special nuclear material. International safeguards apply.

Security: High security required due to nuclear weapons potential.
