Overview

Actinium is a radioactive chemical element with the symbol Ac and atomic number 89. It was discovered in 1899 by French chemist André-Louis Debierne, who separated it from pitchblende. Friedrich Oskar Giesel independently discovered it in 1902 and called it "emanium." Actinium gave its name to the actinide series, a group of 15 similar elements between actinium and lawrencium in the periodic table.

The physical and chemical properties of actinium are similar to those of lanthanum, its lighter homologue in the lanthanide series. Actinium glows in the dark with a pale blue light, caused by the radioactivity of the element ionizing the surrounding air. It is about 150 times more radioactive than radium, making it a valuable source of alpha particles.

Actinium's chemistry is dominated by the +3 oxidation state, similar to lanthanum. It forms compounds with most anions, including oxides, halides, sulfates, and phosphates. Actinium compounds are generally white, like those of lanthanum, but may discolor due to radiation damage.

Isotopes

Actinium has no stable isotopes. All isotopes are radioactive:

  • Actinium-227 (²²⁷Ac) - Half-life of 21.772 years, alpha and beta emitter, most stable isotope
  • Actinium-228 (²²⁸Ac) - Half-life of 6.15 hours, beta emitter
  • Actinium-225 (²²⁵Ac) - Half-life of 10.0 days, alpha emitter, important for medical applications
  • Actinium-226 (²²⁶Ac) - Half-life of 29.37 hours, beta and alpha emitter
  • Actinium-227 (²²⁷Ac) - Occurs naturally in uranium-235 decay chain
  • 34 other isotopes - With half-lives from nanoseconds to hours

Actinium-225 is particularly important for targeted alpha therapy in cancer treatment. Actinium-227 occurs naturally and is the parent of the actinium series (one of the three natural radioactive decay series).

Common Uses

  • Neutron Sources - Actinium-227 mixed with beryllium produces neutrons (²²⁷Ac + Be → neutrons)
  • Medical Therapy - Actinium-225 for targeted alpha therapy against cancer, particularly prostate cancer and leukemia
  • Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generators - Actinium-227 considered for long-lived heat sources in space probes
  • Research - As a source of alpha particles in physics research
  • Industrial Instruments - Neutron sources for well logging in oil exploration
  • Radionuclide Production - Generator for producing other medically useful isotopes (e.g., bismuth-213 from actinium-225)
  • Calibration - Standard source for gamma spectroscopy calibration

The most promising application of actinium is in medicine, where actinium-225 shows great potential for treating cancers that are resistant to conventional therapies.

Biological Role

Actinium has no biological role and is highly toxic due to its radioactivity:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • High Radiotoxicity - About 150 times more radioactive than radium
  • Alpha Radiation - Actinium-225 and -227 decay by alpha emission; alpha particles cause intense localized damage
  • Bone Seeker - Similar to radium, actinium accumulates in bones if internalized
  • Medical Applications - Actinium-225 used in targeted alpha therapy; attached to antibodies or peptides that deliver it to cancer cells
  • Liver Accumulation - Some actinium compounds accumulate in liver
  • Environmental Presence - Trace amounts from natural uranium decay; negligible biological impact

Despite its high radioactivity, actinium-225 shows promise in cancer treatment when properly targeted to tumor cells, minimizing damage to healthy tissue.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Actinium occurs naturally in trace amounts:

  • Uranium Ores - Actinium-227 occurs in uranium-235 decay chain
  • Abundance - Extremely rare; about 0.2 mg of actinium-227 per ton of uranium ore
  • Decay Series - Parent of actinium series (one of four natural decay series)

Artificial Production: Actinium is produced artificially:

  1. Neutron Irradiation - Radium-226 irradiated with neutrons in nuclear reactor:
    • ²²⁶Ra + n → ²²⁷Ra (beta decay, half-life 42.2 minutes)
    • ²²⁷Ra → ²²⁷Ac + e⁻
  2. Alternative Methods - Proton bombardment of thorium-232 or other targets
  3. Separation - Actinium separated from target material by ion exchange or solvent extraction
  4. Medical Grade - Actinium-225 produced by proton irradiation of thorium-232 or from thorium-229 decay

Production Scale: Very small quantities (milligrams to grams annually). Actinium-225 production increasing for medical applications.

Environmental Impact

Actinium has minimal environmental impact due to its rarity:

  • Natural Background - Trace amounts from uranium decay; negligible environmental impact
  • Production Impact - Produced in small quantities at controlled facilities
  • Medical Use - Used in controlled medical settings; waste carefully managed
  • Industrial Use - Neutron sources used in controlled industrial applications
  • Waste Management - Radioactive waste from production and use requires proper disposal
  • Accidental Releases - Unlikely due to small quantities and controlled production/use
  • Regulations - Strictly regulated as radioactive material

Given its extreme rarity and the small quantities involved in applications, actinium poses negligible environmental risk compared to more abundant radioactive elements.

Discovery and History

Actinium was discovered in 1899 by French chemist André-Louis Debierne, a colleague of Marie and Pierre Curie. He separated it from the residue left after pitchblende had uranium extracted from it. Debierne named the element "actinium" from the Greek "aktis" or "aktinos," meaning "beam" or "ray," referring to its radioactivity.

In 1902, German chemist Friedrich Oskar Giesel independently discovered actinium and called it "emanium." After comparing the half-lives of the isotopes, the name "actinium" was adopted. For many years, there was controversy over who should be credited with the discovery, but Debierne is generally recognized as the discoverer.

Actinium gave its name to the actinide series, although it was not initially recognized as the first member of this series. The concept of the actinide series was proposed by Glenn T. Seaborg in 1944, drawing an analogy with the lanthanide series.

Safety Information

High Radiotoxicity: Actinium highly radioactive. About 150 times more radioactive than radium.

Alpha Radiation: Actinium-225 and -227 decay by alpha emission; alpha particles cause intense localized damage.

Internal Hazard: Particularly dangerous if internalized (inhaled, ingested, or through wounds).

Bone Accumulation: If enters body, accumulates in bones like radium; continuous irradiation of bone marrow.

External Hazard: Gamma radiation from actinium and decay products requires shielding.

Neutron Sources: Actinium-beryllium neutron sources produce neutron radiation; additional shielding required.

Protective Equipment: Lead shielding, remote handling tools, protective clothing, respiratory protection if dust present.

Contamination Control: Strict procedures to prevent spread of radioactive contamination.

Storage: Store in sealed containers with adequate shielding. Store to minimize radiation exposure.

Disposal: High-level radioactive waste. Special disposal facilities required.

First Aid: For contamination: remove contaminated clothing, wash skin thoroughly, seek medical attention. For internal contamination, chelation therapy may help.

Regulations: Strictly regulated as radioactive material. Special licenses required for possession.
