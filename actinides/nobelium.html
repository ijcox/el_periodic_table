Overview

Nobelium is a synthetic radioactive chemical element with the symbol No and atomic number 102. It is a member of the actinide series and was named after Alfred Nobel, the Swedish chemist, engineer, and inventor who founded the Nobel Prizes. Nobelium is highly radioactive and has only been produced in minute quantities, with no practical applications outside basic scientific research.

The element was first correctly identified in 1966 by scientists at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR) in Dubna, Russia, although claims of earlier discoveries were made by teams in Sweden and the United States. The discovery history of nobelium is complex and controversial, with priority disputes that took years to resolve. Nobelium is produced in particle accelerators by bombarding lighter elements with charged particles.

Nobelium exhibits oxidation states +2 and +3, with +2 being the most stable in aqueous solution. This is unusual among the actinides, where +3 is typically the most stable state. Nobelium(II) is more stable than would be expected by comparison with lanthanides, due to relativistic effects that stabilize the 5f orbitals. Due to its intense radioactivity, short half-lives, and minute available quantities, chemical studies of nobelium are extremely limited.

Isotopes

Nobelium has no stable isotopes. The most important isotopes are:

  • Nobelium-259 (²⁵⁹No) - Half-life of 58 minutes, alpha decay and electron capture, most stable isotope
  • Nobelium-255 (²⁵⁵No) - Half-life of 3.1 minutes, alpha decay and electron capture
  • Nobelium-254 (²⁵⁴No) - Half-life of 51 seconds, spontaneous fission and alpha decay
  • Nobelium-257 (²⁵⁷No) - Half-life of 25 seconds, alpha decay
  • Nobelium-262 (²⁶²No) - Half-life of 5 milliseconds, spontaneous fission
  • Nobelium-263 (²⁶³No) - Half-life of 20 seconds, alpha decay

Nobelium-259 is the longest-lived isotope. Most nobelium isotopes have half-lives of seconds to minutes, making chemical and physical studies extremely challenging. The very short half-lives mean that nobelium must be studied immediately after production using rapid chemical separation techniques.

Common Uses

  • Nuclear Physics - Study of nuclear decay processes, spontaneous fission, and heavy element synthesis
  • Chemical Studies - Investigation of +2 oxidation state stability in heavy actinides

Nobelium's importance is purely scientific. Studies of nobelium have provided important insights into the stability of the +2 oxidation state in heavy actinides and the effects of relativistic corrections on electron configurations. The element serves as a benchmark for theoretical models of superheavy elements.

Discovery and History

The discovery of nobelium is complex and controversial. The first claim was made in 1957 by an international team of scientists at the Nobel Institute of Physics in Stockholm, Sweden, who bombarded curium-244 with carbon-13 ions and reported producing an isotope with a 10-minute half-life. They proposed the name "nobelium."

However, this discovery could not be reproduced by other laboratories. In 1958, scientists at the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory bombarded curium-244 with carbon-12 ions and produced nobelium-254, confirming the element's existence but with different properties than reported by the Swedish team.

The definitive discovery is credited to the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR) in Dubna, Russia, in 1966. They produced and correctly identified nobelium-254 and nobelium-255. The name "nobelium" was retained despite the disputed discovery, honoring Alfred Nobel. The International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) officially recognized the Dubna group's work in 1992.
