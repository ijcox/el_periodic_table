Overview

Francium is a highly radioactive alkali metal with the symbol Fr and atomic number 87. It is the second-rarest naturally occurring element (after astatine) and the last element discovered in nature, rather than by synthesis. Francium is extremely unstable, with its most stable isotope, francium-223, having a half-life of only 22 minutes.

Francium is the heaviest alkali metal and is expected to have properties similar to cesium, though its extreme radioactivity and short half-life make it difficult to study. It is so rare that it's estimated there is only about 20-30 grams of francium in the Earth's crust at any one time. The largest amount of francium ever assembled was only about 300,000 atoms.

Francium is expected to be the most reactive alkali metal, with chemical properties similar to cesium but even more extreme. It should react violently with water, air, and halogens. However, its chemistry has only been studied at the tracer level.

Isotopes

Francium has no stable isotopes. All isotopes are radioactive:

  • Francium-223 (²²³Fr) - Half-life of 22.00 minutes, beta decay to radium-223, alpha decay to astatine-219, most stable isotope
  • Francium-212 (²¹²Fr) - Half-life of 20.0 minutes, beta decay
  • Francium-221 (²²¹Fr) - Half-life of 4.8 minutes, alpha decay
  • Francium-222 (²²²Fr) - Half-life of 14.2 minutes, beta decay
  • Francium-224 (²²⁴Fr) - Half-life of 3.33 minutes
  • Francium-225 (²²⁵Fr) - Half-life of 4.0 minutes
  • Francium-226 (²²⁶Fr) - Half-life of 49 seconds
  • 34 other isotopes - With half-lives from microseconds to seconds

Francium-223 occurs naturally as a decay product of uranium-235. It is also produced artificially in nuclear reactions.

Common Uses

Francium has no practical applications due to its extreme rarity and short half-life:

  • Research - Study of atomic structure, particularly testing theories of atomic physics
  • Spectroscopy - Study of francium's atomic spectra to test quantum electrodynamics
  • Fundamental Physics - Tests of fundamental symmetries in physics
  • Educational - Example of extreme radioactivity and rarity in chemistry education

Due to its extreme scarcity and radioactivity, francium has no commercial applications and is only studied in specialized research laboratories.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Francium is extremely rare in nature:

  • Decay Product - Occurs in uranium-235 decay chain (actinium-227 → thorium-227 → francium-223)
  • Abundance - Estimated 20-30 grams in Earth's entire crust at any time
  • Transient Existence - Constantly produced and decaying; never accumulates
  • Distribution - Trace amounts in uranium ores

Artificial Production: Francium is produced artificially in particle accelerators:

  1. Nuclear Reactions - Gold-197 bombarded with oxygen-18:
    • ¹⁹⁷Au + ¹⁸O → ²¹⁰Fr + 5n
  2. Other Methods - Various targets bombarded with protons, deuterons, or alpha particles
  3. Separation - Francium separated from target material by chemical methods
  4. Isolation - Typically studied as isolated atoms or ions in traps

Production Scale: Minute quantities - typically thousands to millions of atoms per experiment. Largest amount ever produced: about 300,000 atoms.

Discovery and History

Francium was the last element discovered in nature. Before its discovery, it was referred to as "eka-caesium." Several claims of discovery were made but later disproven.

Francium was finally discovered in 1939 by French chemist Marguerite Perey at the Curie Institute in Paris. She was purifying a sample of actinium-227 and noticed radiation with an energy of 80 keV. She determined this radiation came from a decay product of actinium-227, which was francium-223. Perey initially named it "actinium-K" and later proposed the name "catium" for its predicted high tendency to form cations.

The name "francium" was adopted in 1946 in honor of France (Perey's homeland). Perey was nominated for the Nobel Prize five times but never received it, though she did become the first woman elected to the French Academy of Sciences in 1962. She died of cancer in 1975, likely caused by her work with radioactive materials.
