Overview

Cesium is a soft, silvery-gold alkali metal that is liquid at or near room temperature (melts at 28.5°C/83.3°F). It is the most electropositive and most alkaline element, and has the second-lowest melting point of all metallic elements (after mercury). Cesium reacts explosively with water and ignites spontaneously in air. It is pyrophoric (catches fire spontaneously) when exposed to oxygen.

Cesium was discovered in 1860 by German chemists Robert Bunsen and Gustav Kirchhoff using the newly invented spectroscope. They discovered it in mineral water from Durkheim, Germany, by observing two bright blue spectral lines. The name "cesium" comes from the Latin word "caesius" meaning "sky blue," referring to the color of its emission spectrum. It was the first element discovered by spectrum analysis.

Isotopes

Cesium has one stable isotope:

  • Cesium-133 (¹³³Cs) - 100% abundance, only stable isotope

Cesium has several radioactive isotopes, some with important applications:

  • Cesium-137 (¹³⁷Cs) - Important fission product with half-life of 30.17 years; used in radiation therapy and industrial gauges
  • Cesium-134 (¹³⁴Cs) - Fission product with half-life of 2.065 years; environmental tracer
  • Cesium-135 (¹³⁵Cs) - Long-lived fission product with half-life of 2.3 million years
  • Cesium-131 (¹³¹Cs) - Medical isotope with half-life of 9.69 days; used in brachytherapy for prostate cancer
  • Cesium-136 (¹³⁶Cs) - Fission product with half-life of 13.16 days

Cesium-137 is particularly significant as it was released in nuclear accidents like Chernobyl and Fukushima, contaminating large areas due to its 30-year half-life.

Common Uses

  • Atomic Clocks - Cesium atomic clocks define the SI second; most accurate timekeeping devices
  • Petroleum Exploration - Cesium formate used as drilling fluid in high-pressure, high-temperature oil wells
  • Radiation Therapy - Cesium-137 in cancer treatment (brachytherapy and teletherapy)
  • Industrial Applications - Cesium in photoelectric cells, vacuum tubes, and infrared lamps
  • Chemical Catalyst - Cesium compounds as catalysts in organic synthesis and polymerization
  • Magnetohydrodynamic Power - Cesium as seed material to improve electrical conductivity in MHD generators
  • Glass Manufacturing - Cesium compounds in special optical glasses with high refractive index
  • Research - Cesium in atomic physics experiments, including laser cooling and Bose-Einstein condensates
  • GPS and Telecommunications - Cesium atomic clocks provide timing for GPS satellites and communication networks
  • Radioactive Tracers - Cesium-137 in industrial gauges for thickness measurement and density determination

Biological Role and Effects

Cesium has no essential biological role but has some biological interactions:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • Chemical Similarity to Potassium - Cesium ions similar size to potassium ions; can replace potassium in some biological systems
  • Toxicity - Cesium compounds generally low toxicity compared to other heavy metals; cesium chloride used in alternative medicine
  • Radioactive Cesium - Cesium-137 biologically dangerous; replaces potassium in body, concentrates in muscles, exposes tissues to radiation
  • Half-life in Body - Biological half-life of cesium-137 about 70 days; eliminated through urine
  • Treatment for Radioactive Cesium - Prussian blue (ferric hexacyanoferrate) used to treat cesium-137 contamination
  • Alternative Medicine - Cesium chloride promoted (controversially) for cancer treatment; not FDA-approved, can cause heart toxicity
  • Environmental Exposure - Trace amounts in human body from environmental sources; most exposure from nuclear fallout or occupational
  • Plants - Plants can absorb cesium from soil, especially in areas contaminated by nuclear accidents

Cesium's main biological significance is as a radioactive contaminant from nuclear activities.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Cesium is relatively rare in Earth's crust (3 ppm):

  • Minerals - Mostly in pollucite (CsAlSi₂O₆); also in lepidolite, beryl, avogadrite
  • Brine Deposits - Some lithium-rich brines contain cesium
  • Geographic Distribution - Major deposits in Canada (Bernic Lake), Zimbabwe, Namibia; some in United States (Maine, South Dakota)
  • Seawater - Trace amounts (0.3 ppb)
  • As Byproduct - Some cesium recovered as byproduct of lithium production

Industrial Production: Cesium is produced mainly from pollucite ore:

  1. Mining - Pollucite mined, typically from pegmatite deposits
  2. Ore Processing - Ore crushed, ground, and concentrated by flotation
  3. Acid Digestion - Pollucite treated with hydrochloric or sulfuric acid to extract cesium
  4. Purification - Cesium separated from other alkali metals by precipitation, ion exchange, or solvent extraction
  5. Reduction - Cesium compounds reduced to metal using calcium, magnesium, or electrolysis
  6. Purification of Metal - Cesium metal purified by distillation under vacuum
  7. Brine Processing - Alternative process from cesium-rich brines using solvent extraction

Global Production: About 20-30 tons annually. Limited production due to rarity and specialized applications.

Environmental Impact

Cesium production and use have several environmental considerations:

  • Radioactive Cesium - Cesium-137 major environmental contaminant from nuclear weapons testing and accidents
  • Chernobyl and Fukushima - Released large amounts of cesium-137, contaminating wide areas
  • Long Environmental Half-life - Cesium-137 persists in environment due to 30-year physical half-life
  • Soil Contamination - Cesium binds strongly to clay minerals in soil; remains in upper soil layers
  • Bioaccumulation - Radioactive cesium enters food chain; concentrates in mushrooms, berries, game animals
  • Mining Impact - Limited due to small scale of cesium mining
  • Chemical Toxicity - Stable cesium compounds have low environmental toxicity compared to other heavy metals
  • Water Contamination - Radioactive cesium can contaminate groundwater and surface water
  • Remediation

Discovery and History

In 1860, German chemists Robert Bunsen and Gustav Kirchhoff were analyzing mineral water from Durkheim using their newly invented spectroscope. They had already discovered rubidium earlier that year using the same technique.

When they examined the mineral water, they observed two bright blue spectral lines that didn't correspond to any known element. They named the new element "caesium" (later changed to "cesium") from the Latin word "caesius" meaning "sky blue," referring to the color of these spectral lines.

The first pure cesium metal was produced in 1882 by German chemist Carl Setterberg. He electrolyzed molten cesium cyanide to obtain the metal. Cesium was the first element discovered by spectrum analysis, marking a new era in elemental discovery.

Cesium's importance grew dramatically in the 20th century with the development of atomic clocks. In 1967, the second was redefined based on the radiation frequency of cesium-133 atoms, making cesium fundamental to modern timekeeping.

Interesting Facts

  • Cesium atomic clocks are so accurate they would lose less than 1 second in 300 million years
  • The SI second is defined as 9,192,631,770 periods of radiation corresponding to cesium-133 atom transitions
  • Cesium is so reactive it explodes even in ice-cold water
  • It's one of only five metals liquid at or near room temperature (with mercury, gallium, francium, and rubidium)
  • Cesium has the lowest ionization energy of all stable elements
  • Cesium-137 from Chernobyl still contaminates large areas of Europe 40+ years later
  • The largest cesium reserve is at Bernic Lake in Canada
  • Cesium was used in early photoelectric cells because it emits electrons when exposed to light

Safety Information

Reactivity: Cesium extremely reactive. Reacts violently with water, producing hydrogen gas which may ignite or explode. Reacts with air, oxidizing rapidly and may ignite spontaneously.

Water Reaction: Cesium + water → cesium hydroxide + hydrogen gas. Reaction more violent than with other alkali metals. Can shatter glass containers.

Air Reactivity: Freshly cut cesium surface shiny but rapidly tarnishes in air. Forms superoxide (CsO₂) which can explode when scratched. Store under mineral oil or argon.

Fire Hazard: Cesium fires difficult to extinguish. Do NOT use water, CO₂, or halons. Use Class D fire extinguishers (dry powder) specifically for metal fires.

Radioactive Cesium: Cesium-137 emits beta and gamma radiation. Internal exposure particularly hazardous as it distributes throughout body. External exposure also dangerous.

Toxicity: Stable cesium compounds relatively low toxicity but can cause irritation. Cesium hydroxide extremely corrosive.

First Aid: For skin contact, wash with plenty of water. For eye contact, flush with water for 15 minutes. For inhalation, move to fresh air. For ingestion, give water and seek medical attention. For cesium metal fires, use Class D extinguisher.

Protective Equipment: Use gloves, face shield, lab coat. Handle in glove box under inert atmosphere. Use spark-resistant tools.

Storage: Store under mineral oil or argon in airtight containers. Keep away from water, acids, oxidizers. Separate from halogens and other reactive substances.

Spill Cleanup: For small spills, cover with dry sand or suitable dry absorbent. Transfer to suitable container for disposal. Do NOT use water.

Radioactive Spills: Cesium-137 spills require radiation protection. Evacuate area, restrict access. Use trained personnel with proper radiation monitoring equipment.

Disposal: Cesium waste hazardous. Follow local regulations. Radioactive cesium requires special disposal as radioactive waste.
