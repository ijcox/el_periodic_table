Overview

Radium is a chemical element with the symbol Ra and atomic number 88. It is the sixth element in group 2 of the periodic table, also known as the alkaline earth metals. Pure radium is silvery-white, but it readily reacts with nitrogen (rather than oxygen) upon exposure to air, forming a black surface layer of radium nitride.

Radium was discovered in 1898 by Marie Curie and Pierre Curie, who extracted it from uraninite (pitchblende). They named it "radium" from the Latin word "radius" meaning "ray," because of its intense radioactivity. All isotopes of radium are highly radioactive, with the most stable isotope being radium-226, which has a half-life of 1600 years and decays into radon gas.

Radium is chemically similar to barium, but more reactive. It reacts vigorously with water to form radium hydroxide and hydrogen gas. Radium compounds generally have similar chemical properties to corresponding barium compounds, but are more radioactive.

Isotopes

Radium has no stable isotopes. All isotopes are radioactive:

  • Radium-226 (²²⁶Ra) - Half-life of 1600 years, alpha emitter, most stable isotope, occurs naturally in uranium decay chain
  • Radium-228 (²²⁸Ra) - Half-life of 5.75 years, beta emitter, occurs naturally in thorium decay chain
  • Radium-223 (²²³Ra) - Half-life of 11.43 days, alpha emitter, used in medicine (Xofigo)
  • Radium-224 (²²⁴Ra) - Half-life of 3.66 days, alpha emitter
  • Radium-225 (²²⁵Ra) - Half-life of 14.9 days, alpha emitter
  • Radium-227 (²²⁷Ra) - Half-life of 42.2 minutes
  • 33 other isotopes - With half-lives from nanoseconds to hours

Radium-226 is the most common isotope and is part of the uranium-238 decay chain. Radium-223 is used medically for treating bone metastases from prostate cancer.

Common Uses

  • Historical: Luminous Paint - Mixed with zinc sulfide to create self-luminous paint for watch dials, aircraft instruments (largely discontinued due to health risks)
  • Medical Therapy - Radium-223 dichloride (Xofigo) for treating bone metastases from prostate cancer
  • Historical: Cancer Treatment - Radium needles and seeds for brachytherapy (largely replaced by safer isotopes)
  • Neutron Sources - Mixed with beryllium to create portable neutron sources
  • Industrial Radiography - Historical use as gamma ray source (replaced by cobalt-60 and iridium-192)
  • Research - Standard source for gamma radiation in research laboratories
  • Historical: Consumer Products - Radium-containing cosmetics, toothpaste, and "health" products (all discontinued)
  • Radon Production - Source of radon gas for some medical and research applications

Most historical uses of radium have been discontinued due to recognition of its health hazards. Current medical use of radium-223 represents a revival of therapeutic applications with improved safety.

Biological Role

Radium has no essential biological role and is highly toxic due to its radioactivity:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • Calcium Mimic - Radium chemically similar to calcium; accumulates in bones
  • Bone Seeker - Once in body, radium replaces calcium in bones, particularly areas of active bone growth
  • Radiation Damage - Alpha particles from radium decay damage bone marrow, leading to anemia and leukemia
  • Historical Poisonings - Radium dial painters ("Radium Girls") suffered severe health effects from ingestion
  • Medical Use - Radium-223 targets bone metastases; alpha radiation kills cancer cells in bone
  • Environmental Exposure - Naturally occurring in some groundwater; can accumulate in food chain
  • Half-life in Body - Biological half-life in bones about 25 years; effectively permanent once deposited

Radium's similarity to calcium makes it particularly dangerous as it becomes permanently incorporated into bone tissue, continuously irradiating bone marrow.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Radium occurs naturally in uranium and thorium ores:

  • Uranium Ores - Radium-226 occurs in uranium-238 decay chain (1 gram radium per 3 tons uranium)
  • Thorium Ores - Radium-228 occurs in thorium-232 decay chain
  • Groundwater - Some mineral waters contain dissolved radium
  • Soil and Rocks - Trace amounts in all soils and rocks containing uranium or thorium

Historical Production: Radium was historically extracted from pitchblende:

  1. Mining - Uranium ore mined (primarily from Joachimsthal, Colorado Plateau, Great Bear Lake)
  2. Processing - Ore crushed and treated with chemicals to extract uranium
  3. Radium Extraction - Radium separated from uranium and other elements by fractional crystallization
  4. Purification - Multiple crystallization steps to purify radium salts
  5. Massive Scale - Tons of ore processed to obtain grams of radium

Current Production: Minimal commercial production. Radium-223 for medical use produced by neutron irradiation of radium-226 or from thorium targets. Most radium now obtained from old sources or as byproduct of uranium processing.

Environmental Impact

Radium poses significant environmental and health risks:

  • Legacy Contamination - Historical radium processing sites contaminated with radium and decay products
  • Uranium Mill Tailings - Waste from uranium mining contains radium; can contaminate groundwater
  • Building Contamination - Structures using radium-containing materials (some historical buildings)
  • Water Contamination - Radium in some groundwater supplies, particularly from deep wells
  • Regulation - Radium in drinking water regulated in many countries (US EPA limit: 5 pCi/L)
  • Cleanup Challenges - Radioactive contamination difficult and expensive to remediate
  • Historical Waste - Radium dial painting facilities left contaminated sites
  • Natural Attenuation - Radium-226 half-life 1600 years; contamination persists for millennia

Radium contamination remains a concern at historical production sites and in areas with naturally high radium in groundwater.

Discovery and History

Radium was discovered by Marie Curie and Pierre Curie in 1898. They were investigating the radioactivity of pitchblende, a uranium ore, and found that it was more radioactive than could be explained by its uranium content alone. After processing tons of pitchblende, they isolated a new element which they named "radium."

The Curies faced enormous challenges in isolating radium, processing approximately one ton of pitchblende to obtain 0.1 grams of radium chloride in 1902. Marie Curie received two Nobel Prizes: Physics in 1903 (shared with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel) for research on radiation, and Chemistry in 1911 for discovering radium and polonium.

In the early 20th century, radium was hailed as a miracle substance and used in numerous consumer products. The tragic story of the "Radium Girls" - factory workers who painted watch dials with radium paint and suffered severe health effects - led to increased awareness of radiation hazards and the establishment of occupational safety standards.

Interesting Facts

  • Marie Curie carried tubes of radium in her pocket, not knowing the dangers
  • Radium glows in the dark with a faint blue light (caused by radiation exciting air molecules)
  • The "Radium Girls" case established the right of workers to sue for occupational injuries
  • Radium was once more valuable than diamonds - $100,000 per gram in 1900s
  • Eben Byers, a socialite, died from drinking radium water he believed was healthy
  • Radium was used in toothpaste, cosmetics, and even chocolate in the early 1900s
  • Marie Curie's notebooks are still too radioactive to handle without protection
  • The unit of radioactivity was originally called the "curie" in honor of the Curies

Safety Information

High Radiotoxicity: Radium highly radioactive. All isotopes emit alpha, beta, and gamma radiation.

Internal Hazard: Particularly dangerous if ingested, inhaled, or enters body. Accumulates in bones.

Alpha Radiation: Alpha particles from radium and decay products cause intense localized damage.

Historical Lessons: "Radium Girls" suffered anemia, bone fractures, jaw necrosis, and cancers from ingestion.

External Hazard: Gamma radiation from radium sources requires shielding.

Radon Daughter: Radium-226 decays to radon-222 gas; inhalation hazard from accumulated radon.

Protective Equipment: Lead shielding, remote handling tools, protective clothing, respiratory protection if dust present.

Contamination Control: Strict procedures to prevent spread of radioactive contamination.

Storage: Store in sealed containers with adequate shielding. Store to minimize radon accumulation.

Disposal: High-level radioactive waste. Special disposal facilities required.

First Aid: For contamination: remove contaminated clothing, wash skin thoroughly, seek medical attention. For internal contamination, chelation therapy may help.

Regulations: Strictly regulated as radioactive material. Special licenses required for possession.
