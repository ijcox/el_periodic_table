Overview

Ytterbium is a soft, malleable, ductile, silvery-white rare earth metal. It is relatively stable in air compared to other rare earth metals, but slowly oxidizes and should be stored in sealed containers or under mineral oil. Ytterbium has two common oxidation states: +2 and +3, with the +3 state being more common. The element exhibits an unusual property: its electrical resistivity decreases under pressure, which is opposite to most metals. Ytterbium is named after the Swedish village of Ytterby, where its mineral was first discovered.

Ytterbium was discovered in 1878 by Swiss chemist Jean Charles Galissard de Marignac. He found a new component in the earth then known as erbia and named it ytterbia. However, this ytterbia later proved to be a mixture of several rare earth oxides. In 1907, French chemist Georges Urbain separated ytterbia into two components which he called neoytterbia and lutecia. These were later renamed ytterbium and lutetium, respectively. Pure ytterbium metal was first produced in 1937.

Isotopes

Ytterbium has seven stable isotopes:

  • Ytterbium-168 (¹⁶⁸Yb) - 0.13% abundance, stable
  • Ytterbium-170 (¹⁷⁰Yb) - 3.04% abundance, stable
  • Ytterbium-171 (¹⁷¹Yb) - 14.28% abundance, stable
  • Ytterbium-172 (¹⁷²Yb) - 21.83% abundance, stable
  • Ytterbium-173 (¹⁷³Yb) - 16.13% abundance, stable
  • Ytterbium-174 (¹⁷⁴Yb) - 31.83% abundance, stable
  • Ytterbium-176 (¹⁷⁶Yb) - 12.76% abundance, stable

Ytterbium has several artificial radioactive isotopes with important applications:

  • Ytterbium-169 (¹⁶⁹Yb) - Medical isotope with half-life of 32.0 days; used as gamma source
  • Ytterbium-175 (¹⁷⁵Yb) - Artificial isotope with half-life of 4.18 days
  • Ytterbium-166 (¹⁶⁶Yb) - Artificial isotope with half-life of 56.7 hours
  • Ytterbium-177 (¹⁷⁷Yb) - Artificial isotope with half-life of 1.9 hours

Ytterbium-169 is used in portable X-ray devices and industrial radiography.

Common Uses

  • Stainless Steel Alloys - Ytterbium improves grain refinement, strength, and workability of stainless steel
  • Portable X-ray Devices - Ytterbium-169 as gamma radiation source for industrial radiography and field X-rays
  • Lasers - Ytterbium-doped lasers (Yb:YAG, Yb:glass) for industrial cutting, welding, and medical applications
  • Atomic Clocks - Ytterbium optical lattice clocks - most precise timekeeping devices ever made
  • Fiber Optic Amplifiers - Ytterbium-doped fiber amplifiers for telecommunications
  • Stress Gauges - Ytterbium monitors stress in materials due to its pressure-sensitive resistivity
  • Catalysts - Ytterbium compounds as catalysts in organic chemistry
  • Dental Alloys - Ytterbium in some dental alloys and fillings
  • Research - Ytterbium in quantum computing research and solid-state physics
  • Gamma Source - Ytterbium-169 for thickness gauges and material analysis

Biological Role and Effects

Ytterbium has no essential biological role but has some biological interactions:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • Toxicity - Ytterbium compounds have low to moderate toxicity; similar to other rare earth elements
  • Chemical Similarity to Calcium - Ytterbium ions can interfere with calcium-dependent biological processes
  • Environmental Exposure - Trace amounts in human body from environmental sources
  • Occupational Hazards - Workers in rare earth processing may be exposed to ytterbium dust
  • Plants - Ytterbium can affect plant growth; typically inhibitory at high concentrations
  • Medical Imaging - Ytterbium-169 used in some specialized medical imaging applications
  • Research - Ytterbium compounds used in some biological studies
  • Bioaccumulation - Limited bioaccumulation in food chain
  • Radiation Safety - Ytterbium-169 requires radiation safety precautions

Ytterbium's biological significance is minimal, with primary concerns related to occupational exposure.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Ytterbium is relatively rare in Earth's crust (3.2 ppm):

  • Minerals - Xenotime, monazite, bastnäsite, and euxenite are primary ores
  • Geographic Distribution - Major deposits in China (Southern China clays), United States (Mountain Pass), Australia, India
  • Seawater - Very low concentration (0.82 ng/L)
  • In Rocks - More abundant than cadmium or mercury
  • Association - Typically found with other heavy rare earth elements like lutetium and thulium

Industrial Production: Ytterbium is produced from rare earth ores:

  1. Mining - Rare earth ores (xenotime, monazite) or ion-adsorption clays mined
  2. Ore Processing - For clays: in-situ leaching; for ores: crushing and concentration
  3. Acid Digestion - Treated with sulfuric or hydrochloric acid to dissolve rare earths
  4. Solvent Extraction - Multiple solvent extraction steps separate heavy rare earth elements
  5. Separation - Ytterbium separated from other rare earths using ion exchange chromatography
  6. Precipitation - Ytterbium precipitated as oxalate or hydroxide
  7. Calcination - Precipitate calcined to ytterbium oxide (Yb₂O₃)
  8. Metal Production - Oxide reduced with lanthanum: Yb₂O₃ + 2La → 2Yb + La₂O₃
  9. Purification - Additional purification by vacuum distillation or sublimation

Global Production: About 50 tons annually as ytterbium oxide. China produces majority of world supply.

Environmental Impact

Ytterbium production and use have several environmental considerations:

  • Mining Impact - Rare earth mining causes significant environmental damage
  • Radioactive Waste - Monazite contains thorium; processing generates radioactive waste
  • Chemical Pollution - Acid leaching processes can contaminate water with acids and heavy metals
  • Industrial Applications - Ytterbium improves steel properties, potentially reducing material use
  • Precision Timing - Ytterbium atomic clocks enable more precise GPS and scientific measurements
  • Electronic Waste - Ytterbium in lasers and scientific equipment requires proper disposal
  • Limited Recycling - Very low rates of ytterbium recycling from end-of-life products
  • Toxicity to Aquatic Life - Ytterbium compounds can be toxic to aquatic organisms
  • Medical and Industrial Radiography - Ytterbium-169 sources require proper management as radioactive material
  • Scientific Research - Ytterbium enables advances in quantum physics and precision measurement

Ytterbium presents environmental trade-offs: mining impacts vs. benefits in advanced technology and scientific research.

Discovery and History

In 1878, Swiss chemist Jean Charles Galissard de Marignac was studying the rare earth mineral gadolinite. He discovered a new earth (oxide) in what was thought to be erbium oxide. He named this new substance "ytterbia" after the village of Ytterby in Sweden, where the mineral gadolinite had been found.

However, Marignac's ytterbia was not pure. In 1907, French chemist Georges Urbain separated ytterbia into two components which he called neoytterbia and lutecia. These were later renamed ytterbium and lutetium, respectively. Austrian chemist Carl Auer von Welsbach independently made the same discovery at about the same time.

There was considerable dispute over priority, but Urbain's names were eventually accepted. Pure ytterbium metal was first produced in 1937, but it contained significant impurities. High-purity ytterbium became available only after the development of ion exchange techniques in the 1950s.

Ytterbium's unique properties, such as its pressure-sensitive resistivity and utility in atomic clocks, were discovered in the late 20th century, leading to increased interest in the element.

Interesting Facts

  • Ytterbium atomic clocks are so precise they would lose less than 1 second in 15 billion years - older than the universe
  • The element's electrical resistivity decreases under pressure, opposite to most metals
  • Ytterbium is named after Ytterby, Sweden - the same village gave its name to yttrium, terbium, and erbium
  • Ytterbium-doped lasers are among the most efficient solid-state lasers known
  • Adding just 0.1% ytterbium to stainless steel can double its strength
  • Ytterbium-169 portable X-ray devices are used to inspect welds on pipelines and in other field applications
  • The element can exist in both +2 and +3 oxidation states, which is unusual for lanthanides
  • Ytterbium is being studied for use in quantum computers and quantum memory devices

Safety Information

General Toxicity: Ytterbium compounds have low to moderate toxicity. Metal dust flammable.

Metal Dust: Ytterbium powder flammable, can ignite spontaneously in air. Fire produces irritating fumes.

Occupational Exposure: Inhalation of ytterbium dust may cause lung damage. Chronic exposure may cause pneumoconiosis.

Chemical Reactivity: Ytterbium metal reacts slowly with water and reacts with acids exothermically.

Radiation Safety: Ytterbium-169 requires radiation safety protocols for handling and use. Gamma emitter.

First Aid: For inhalation, move to fresh air. For skin contact, wash with soap and water. For eye contact, flush with water for 15 minutes. For ingestion, give water and seek medical attention.

Protective Equipment: Use gloves, safety glasses, dust masks when handling ytterbium compounds. Use local exhaust ventilation for dust.

Laser Safety: Ytterbium-doped lasers require proper laser safety protocols.

Storage: Store metal under mineral oil or argon to prevent oxidation. Store compounds in tightly closed containers.

Fire Hazard: Ytterbium metal burns readily. Use Class D extinguisher for metal fires. Do not use water on burning metal.

Radioactive Considerations: Natural ytterbium ores may contain radioactive thorium. Follow radiation safety protocols when processing ores.

Environmental: Ytterbium compounds may accumulate in environment. Follow proper disposal procedures.

Disposal: Follow local regulations for disposal of ytterbium compounds and contaminated materials. Radioactive ytterbium requires special disposal.

Industrial Radiography: Ytterbium-169 industrial use requires licensed operators and radiation safety programs.
