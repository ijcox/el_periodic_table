Overview

Thulium is a silvery-gray, soft, malleable rare earth metal. It is reasonably stable in air but tarnishes slowly. Thulium is the least abundant of the naturally occurring rare earth elements (except for promethium, which is radioactive). Despite its scarcity, thulium has important specialized applications in medicine and technology. The element is named after Thule, a mythical region in Scandinavia, continuing the tradition of naming rare earth elements after geographical locations.

Thulium was discovered in 1879 by Swedish chemist Per Teodor Cleve. He was studying erbia (erbium oxide) and found two new substances in it - one brown and one green. The brown substance was holmium oxide, and the green substance was thulium oxide. Cleve named the new element "thulium" after Thule, an ancient name for Scandinavia. Pure thulium metal was first produced in 1911.

Isotopes

Thulium has one stable isotope:

  • Thulium-169 (¹⁶⁹Tm) - 100% abundance, only stable isotope

Thulium has several artificial radioactive isotopes with important medical applications:

  • Thulium-170 (¹⁷⁰Tm) - Medical isotope with half-life of 128.6 days; used in portable X-ray devices
  • Thulium-171 (¹⁷¹Tm) - Artificial isotope with half-life of 1.92 years
  • Thulium-172 (¹⁷²Tm) - Artificial isotope with half-life of 63.6 hours
  • Thulium-167 (¹⁶⁷Tm) - Artificial isotope with half-life of 9.25 days
  • Thulium-168 (¹⁶⁸Tm) - Artificial isotope with half-life of 93.1 days

Thulium-170 is particularly important for medical and industrial applications due to its suitable half-life and gamma emission.

Common Uses

  • Portable X-ray Devices - Thulium-170 as radiation source in lightweight X-ray units for field use
  • Lasers - Thulium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet (Tm:YAG) lasers for medical and industrial applications
  • Medical Therapy - Thulium lasers for kidney stone treatment (lithotripsy) and prostate surgery
  • Dosimetry - Thulium-doped materials in thermoluminescent dosimeters for radiation monitoring
  • Superconductors - Thulium in high-temperature superconducting materials
  • Euro Banknotes - Thulium compounds in anti-counterfeiting phosphors that glow blue under UV light
  • Ferrites - Thulium in some ceramic magnetic materials
  • Research - Thulium as Mossbauer spectroscopy source and in solid-state physics research
  • Nuclear Technology - Thulium as possible radiation source in radioisotope thermoelectric generators
  • Metal Alloys - Thulium in specialized alloys for high-temperature applications

Biological Role and Effects

Thulium has no essential biological role but has important medical applications:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • Medical Lasers - Thulium lasers used in urology for kidney stone treatment and prostate surgery
  • Medical Applications - Effective for lithotripsy (kidney stone fragmentation) with minimal tissue damage
  • Portable X-rays - Thulium-170 enables lightweight X-ray devices for field medicine and dentistry
  • Advantages - Thulium lasers operate at wavelengths well-absorbed by water, making them ideal for soft tissue surgery
  • Toxicity - Thulium compounds have low to moderate toxicity; similar to other rare earth elements
  • Environmental Exposure - Trace amounts in human body from environmental sources
  • Occupational Hazards - Workers in rare earth processing may be exposed to thulium dust
  • Plants - Thulium can affect plant growth; typically inhibitory at high concentrations
  • Radiation Safety - Thulium-170 requires radiation safety precautions in medical and industrial use

Thulium's biological significance is primarily its medical use in lasers and portable X-ray devices.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Thulium is the rarest naturally occurring rare earth element (0.52 ppm in Earth's crust):

  • Minerals - Monazite, bastnäsite, xenotime, and euxenite are primary ores
  • Geographic Distribution - Major deposits in China (Southern China clays), United States (Mountain Pass), Australia, India
  • Seawater - Extremely low concentration (0.17 ng/L)
  • In Rocks - About as abundant as gold or platinum
  • Association - Typically found with other heavy rare earth elements like ytterbium and lutetium

Industrial Production: Thulium is produced from rare earth ores:

  1. Mining - Rare earth ores (xenotime, monazite) or ion-adsorption clays mined
  2. Ore Processing - For clays: in-situ leaching; for ores: crushing and concentration
  3. Acid Digestion - Treated with sulfuric or hydrochloric acid to dissolve rare earths
  4. Solvent Extraction - Multiple solvent extraction steps separate heavy rare earth elements
  5. Separation - Thulium separated from other rare earths using ion exchange chromatography
  6. Precipitation - Thulium precipitated as oxalate or hydroxide
  7. Calcination - Precipitate calcined to thulium oxide (Tm₂O₃)
  8. Metal Production - Oxide reduced with calcium or lanthanum: Tm₂O₃ + 3Ca → 2Tm + 3CaO
  9. Purification - Additional purification by vacuum distillation

Global Production: About 50 tons annually as thulium oxide. China produces majority of world supply.

Environmental Impact

Thulium production and use have several environmental considerations:

  • Mining Impact - Rare earth mining causes significant environmental damage
  • Radioactive Waste - Monazite contains thorium; processing generates radioactive waste
  • Chemical Pollution - Acid leaching processes can contaminate water with acids and heavy metals
  • Medical Benefits - Thulium lasers and portable X-ray devices improve medical care in remote areas
  • Electronic Waste - Thulium in specialized devices requires proper disposal
  • Limited Recycling - Extremely low rates of thulium recycling from end-of-life products
  • Toxicity to Aquatic Life - Thulium compounds can be toxic to aquatic organisms
  • Medical Radioisotopes - Thulium-170 medical use generates radioactive waste requiring proper disposal
  • Resource Scarcity - Thulium is one of the rarest stable elements; limited supply for future applications
  • Field Medicine - Thulium-170 X-ray sources enable medical diagnostics in disaster zones and remote areas

Thulium presents environmental trade-offs: mining impacts vs. medical benefits, especially for remote healthcare.

Discovery and History

In 1879, Swedish chemist Per Teodor Cleve was studying erbia (erbium oxide) at Uppsala University. Through careful fractional crystallization, he separated it into three different substances: erbia itself, a brown substance he called holmia (holmium oxide), and a green substance he called thulia (thulium oxide).

Cleve named the new element "thulium" after Thule, an ancient name for a mythical region in the far north, often identified with Scandinavia. The name continued the tradition of naming rare earth elements after geographical locations.

However, the thulium oxide Cleve obtained was not pure. In 1886, French chemist Lecoq de Boisbaudran obtained purer thulium by separating thulia into two components: thulia itself and a new substance he called dysprosia (dysprosium oxide).

Pure thulium metal was first produced in 1911 by American chemist Charles James, who reduced thulium chloride with sodium. However, high-purity thulium wasn't available until the development of ion exchange techniques in the 1950s.

Safety Information

General Toxicity: Thulium compounds have low to moderate toxicity. Metal dust flammable.

Metal Dust: Thulium powder flammable, can ignite spontaneously in air. Fire produces irritating fumes.

Medical Lasers: Thulium lasers require proper training and safety protocols. Eye protection essential.

Portable X-ray Devices: Thulium-170 X-ray sources require radiation safety protocols for handling and use.

Occupational Exposure: Inhalation of thulium dust may cause lung damage. Chronic exposure may cause pneumoconiosis.

Chemical Reactivity: Thulium metal reacts slowly with water and reacts with acids exothermically.

First Aid: For inhalation, move to fresh air. For skin contact, wash with soap and water. For eye contact, flush with water for 15 minutes. For ingestion, give water and seek medical attention.

Protective Equipment: Use gloves, safety glasses, dust masks when handling thulium compounds. Use local exhaust ventilation for dust.

Radiation Safety: For radioactive thulium isotopes (¹⁷⁰Tm), follow radiation safety protocols. Use shielding and monitoring.

Storage: Store metal under mineral oil or argon to prevent oxidation. Store compounds in tightly closed containers.

Fire Hazard: Thulium metal burns readily. Use Class D extinguisher for metal fires. Do not use water on burning metal.

Radioactive Considerations: Natural thulium ores may contain radioactive thorium. Follow radiation safety protocols when processing ores.

Environmental: Thulium compounds may accumulate in environment. Follow proper disposal procedures.

Disposal: Follow local regulations for disposal of thulium compounds and contaminated materials. Radioactive thulium requires special disposal.

Medical Device Regulations: Thulium-170 X-ray devices and thulium lasers subject to medical device regulations.
