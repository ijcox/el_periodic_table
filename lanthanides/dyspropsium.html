Overview

Dysprosium is a rare earth element with a bright, silvery metallic luster. It is relatively stable in air at room temperature but oxidizes readily when heated, burning to form dysprosium(III) oxide. The element has high magnetic susceptibility and is used in various applications requiring strong permanent magnets. The name "dysprosium" comes from the Greek word "dysprositos" meaning "hard to get," reflecting the difficulty of isolating it.

Dysprosium was discovered in 1886 by French chemist Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran. He separated dysprosium oxide from holmium oxide using fractional crystallization of ammonium hydroxide nitrate. However, the pure metal was not isolated until the 1950s with the development of ion exchange techniques. Dysprosium has become increasingly important in recent years due to its critical role in clean energy technologies.

Common Uses

  • Permanent Magnets - Dysprosium added to neodymium-iron-boron (NdFeB) magnets to improve high-temperature performance
  • Nuclear Reactors - Dysprosium oxide-cadmium mixtures in control rods for neutron absorption
  • Terfenol-D - Dysprosium-terbium-iron alloy with giant magnetostriction for sonar, actuators, sensors
  • Data Storage - Dysprosium in magneto-optical recording disks
  • Lighting - Dysprosium iodide in metal halide lamps for high-intensity white light
  • Dosimetry - Dysprosium-activated calcium sulfate or strontium sulfate in thermoluminescent dosimeters
  • Lasers - Dysprosium-doped materials in some solid-state and fiber lasers
  • Electric Vehicles - Dysprosium in motor magnets improves performance at high temperatures
  • Wind Turbines - Dysprosium in generator magnets for direct-drive wind turbines
  • Research - Dysprosium as neutron absorber in nuclear physics experiments

Biological Role and Effects

Dysprosium has no essential biological role but has some biological interactions:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • Toxicity - Dysprosium compounds have low to moderate toxicity; similar to other rare earth elements
  • Chemical Similarity to Calcium - Dysprosium ions can interfere with calcium-dependent biological processes
  • Environmental Exposure - Trace amounts in human body from environmental sources
  • Occupational Hazards - Workers in rare earth processing may be exposed to dysprosium dust
  • Plants - Dysprosium can affect plant growth; typically inhibitory at high concentrations
  • Research - Dysprosium compounds used in some biological studies and medical research
  • Bioaccumulation - Limited bioaccumulation in food chain
  • Medical Imaging - Dysprosium complexes investigated as contrast agents for MRI
  • Radiation Dosimetry - Dysprosium-activated phosphors used in radiation dose measurement

Dysprosium's biological significance is minimal, with primary concerns related to occupational exposure.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Dysprosium is relatively rare in Earth's crust (5.2 ppm):

  • Minerals - Xenotime, monazite, bastnäsite, and euxenite are primary ores
  • Geographic Distribution - Major deposits in China (Southern China clays), United States (Mountain Pass), Australia, Myanmar
  • Seawater - Very low concentration (0.9 ng/L)
  • In Rocks - More abundant than silver or mercury
  • Ion-Adsorption Clays - Important source from Southern China where dysprosium is adsorbed onto clay minerals

Industrial Production: Dysprosium is produced from rare earth ores:

  1. Mining - Rare earth ores (xenotime, monazite) or ion-adsorption clays mined
  2. Ore Processing - For clays: in-situ or heap leaching with ammonium sulfate
  3. Acid Digestion - For traditional ores: treated with sulfuric or hydrochloric acid
  4. Solvent Extraction - Multiple solvent extraction steps separate heavy rare earth elements
  5. Separation - Dysprosium separated from other heavy rare earths using ion exchange chromatography
  6. Precipitation - Dysprosium precipitated as oxalate or hydroxide
  7. Calcination - Precipitate calcined to dysprosium oxide (Dy₂O₃)
  8. Metal Production - Oxide reduced with calcium: Dy₂O₃ + 3Ca → 2Dy + 3CaO
  9. Purification - Additional purification by vacuum distillation

Global Production: About 100 tons annually as dysprosium oxide. China produces over 95% of world supply.

Environmental Impact

Dysprosium production and use have several environmental considerations:

  • Mining Impact - Rare earth mining, especially ion-adsorption clay mining, causes significant environmental damage
  • Radioactive Waste - Monazite contains thorium; processing generates radioactive waste
  • Chemical Pollution - Heap leaching of clays can contaminate groundwater with acids and ammonium
  • Critical for Clean Energy - Dysprosium essential for electric vehicle motors and wind turbine generators
  • Supply Chain Vulnerability - China's near-monopoly creates geopolitical and supply security concerns
  • Electronic Waste - Dysprosium in magnets and electronics requires proper recycling
  • Limited Recycling - Very low rates of dysprosium recycling from end-of-life products
  • Research Alternatives - Intensive research to reduce or eliminate dysprosium in magnets
  • Toxicity to Aquatic Life - Dysprosium compounds can be toxic to aquatic organisms
  • Energy Transition - Dysprosium availability could constrain growth of renewable energy technologies

Dysprosium is critical for the energy transition but presents significant environmental and supply chain challenges.

Discovery and History

In 1886, French chemist Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran was working with holmium oxide. Through repeated fractional crystallization of the ammonium hydroxide nitrate, he obtained two different fractions: one brown and one white.

The white fraction yielded spectral lines that didn't correspond to any known element. Lecoq de Boisbaudran named the new element "dysprosium" from the Greek word "dysprositos" meaning "hard to get," reflecting the difficulty he had in isolating it.

However, the material he obtained was not pure dysprosium but contained other rare earth elements as well. Pure dysprosium oxide was not obtained until the early 20th century, and relatively pure dysprosium metal was not produced until the 1950s when ion exchange techniques were developed.

Dysprosium's importance grew dramatically in the 1980s with the invention of neodymium-iron-boron magnets. Researchers found that adding dysprosium to these magnets greatly improved their performance at high temperatures, making them suitable for demanding applications like electric vehicle motors.

Interesting Facts

  • Dysprosium's name means "hard to get" in Greek - reflecting its difficult isolation
  • Adding just 2-3% dysprosium to neodymium magnets allows them to work at temperatures up to 200°C
  • Dysprosium is critical for electric vehicles - a typical EV motor contains about 100 grams of dysprosium
  • The element has the highest magnetic strength of any element at low temperatures
  • Dysprosium is one of the most "critical" rare earths due to its importance in clean energy and limited supply
  • Terfenol-D, containing dysprosium, can change shape in a magnetic field and is used in sonar systems
  • Dysprosium iodide lamps produce light very close to natural sunlight
  • China produces over 95% of the world's dysprosium, mostly from ion-adsorption clays in Southern China

Safety Information

General Toxicity: Dysprosium compounds have low to moderate toxicity. Metal dust flammable.

Metal Dust: Dysprosium powder flammable, can ignite spontaneously in air. Fire produces irritating fumes.

Occupational Exposure: Inhalation of dysprosium dust may cause lung damage. Chronic exposure may cause pneumoconiosis.

Chemical Reactivity: Dysprosium metal reacts slowly with water and reacts with acids exothermically.

First Aid: For inhalation, move to fresh air. For skin contact, wash with soap and water. For eye contact, flush with water for 15 minutes. For ingestion, give water and seek medical attention.

Protective Equipment: Use gloves, safety glasses, dust masks when handling dysprosium compounds. Use local exhaust ventilation for dust.

Storage: Store metal under mineral oil or argon to prevent oxidation. Store compounds in tightly closed containers.

Fire Hazard: Dysprosium metal burns readily. Use Class D extinguisher for metal fires. Do not use water on burning metal.

Radioactive Considerations: Natural dysprosium ores may contain radioactive thorium. Follow radiation safety protocols when processing ores.

Magnet Safety: Dysprosium-containing magnets are extremely strong. Handle with care to prevent pinching injuries and damage to electronic devices.

Environmental: Dysprosium compounds may accumulate in environment. Follow proper disposal procedures.

Disposal: Follow local regulations for disposal of dysprosium compounds and contaminated materials.

Supply Chain Issues: Due to critical importance and limited supply, dysprosium subject to export restrictions and geopolitical concerns.
