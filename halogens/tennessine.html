Overview

Tennessine is a synthetic chemical element and a member of the transactinide series. It is a highly radioactive element that does not occur naturally and can only be produced in particle accelerators. Tennessine is predicted to be a solid at room temperature, which is unusual for a halogen (fluorine and chlorine are gases, bromine is liquid, iodine is solid but sublimes easily, astatine is also solid). This predicted behavior results from extreme relativistic effects that dominate the chemistry of superheavy elements.

Tennessine was first synthesized in 2010 by a joint Russian-American team at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR) in Dubna, Russia, in collaboration with Oak Ridge National Laboratory and Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory in the USA. The element was named after the state of Tennessee, USA, in recognition of the contributions of Oak Ridge National Laboratory (located in Tennessee), Vanderbilt University, and the University of Tennessee to superheavy element research.

Tennessine's predicted physical state as a solid at room temperature makes it unique among halogens. This results from relativistic effects that increase the binding energy of the 7p electrons and may cause tennessine to behave more like a metalloid or poor metal than a typical halogen. Its predicted melting point is hundreds of degrees higher than iodine's (113.7°C) and astatine's (302°C).

Chemical Behavior Predicted to be less reactive than other halogens, possibly metalloid-like

Tennessine's chemistry is predicted to be dominated by extreme relativistic effects. Unlike other halogens which strongly favor the -1 oxidation state, tennessine may show reduced stability in the -1 state and increased stability in positive oxidation states (+1, +3, +5). The Ts⁻ ion is predicted to be less stable than I⁻ or At⁻. Tennessine may behave more like a metalloid or poor metal than a typical halogen. It could form compounds like TsF (tennessine monofluoride), TsF₃ (tennessine trifluoride), and TsF₅ (tennessine pentafluoride). Some calculations suggest tennessine might even form a +7 oxidation state compound (TsF₇), though this is controversial. Tennessine is expected to be the least reactive halogen and might not react with hydrogen to form HTs (tennessine hydride).

Tennessine-294 is the most stable known isotope with a half-life of approximately 51 milliseconds. There are indications that more neutron-rich isotopes like tennessine-297 might have longer half-lives (possibly around 1 second), which if verified, would be relatively long-lived for such a heavy element and might indicate proximity to the "island of stability."

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Tennessine does not occur naturally. It is entirely synthetic.

Artificial Production Methods:

  1. Hot Fusion Reactions:
    ²⁴⁹Bk + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁹⁴Ts + 3n (original JINR synthesis, April 2010)
    ²⁴⁹Bk + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁹³Ts + 4n
  2. Production Challenges:
    Berkelium-249 target required (half-life 330 days, difficult to produce)
    Target preparation required at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, then transported to Dubna
    Experiment conducted before significant decay of berkelium target
  3. Other Possible Reactions:
    ²⁵¹Cf + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁹⁶Ts + 3n (theoretical)
    ²⁴⁸Cm + ⁵¹V → ²⁹⁶Ts + 3n (theoretical)
  4. Separation - Tennessine atoms separated using gas-filled recoil separators. The Dubna Gas-Filled Recoil Separator (DGFRS) was used for its discovery and study.
  5. Detection - Individual atoms detected by their characteristic decay patterns using silicon detectors, often requiring correlation with daughter decays through alpha decay chains to moscovium and nihonium.
  6. Quantity - Production rates extremely low (a few atoms per experiment).

Production Sites: Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR, Dubna, Russia - primary site), with target material from Oak Ridge National Laboratory (USA). Total world production is only about 6 atoms.

Discovery and History

The discovery of tennessine represented a significant achievement in international collaboration for superheavy element research:

Discovery (2010): In April 2010, a joint Russian-American team at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR) in Dubna, Russia, synthesized tennessine by bombarding berkelium-249 with calcium-48 ions. The berkelium target was produced at Oak Ridge National Laboratory in Tennessee, USA, and shipped to Dubna for the experiment. The team produced atoms of tennessine-293 and tennessine-294. The discovery was announced in April 2010.

Confirmation (2012): The discovery was confirmed in 2012 by the same collaboration with additional experiments. The decay chains were studied in detail, confirming the original discovery.

Independent Confirmation (2014): In 2014, a team at the GSI Helmholtz Centre in Germany independently confirmed the discovery of tennessine, providing additional validation.

Naming: The discovery team proposed the name "tennessine" after the state of Tennessee, USA, recognizing the contributions of Oak Ridge National Laboratory, Vanderbilt University, and the University of Tennessee. This was the second element named after a US state (californium was the first).

Official Recognition (2016): On November 28, 2016, IUPAC officially announced the name "tennessine" with symbol Ts. The name was approved after the usual period of public comment and review.

Significance: Tennessine's discovery completed the halogen group in the periodic table and demonstrated remarkable international collaboration, with target material produced in the USA and experiments conducted in Russia. The successful synthesis despite the short half-life of the berkelium target (330 days) was a logistical and scientific achievement.

Theoretical Interest: Tennessine has attracted considerable theoretical interest due to predictions that it might behave very differently from other halogens, possibly being more metalloid-like than halogen-like due to relativistic effects.

Safety Information

Radioactivity: All isotopes of tennessine are highly radioactive. Primary hazard is alpha radiation.

Production: Produced only in specialized nuclear facilities with heavy ion accelerators. Not available outside research laboratories.

Quantity: Only atom quantities produced; insufficient for bulk handling or conventional chemical study.

Alpha Radiation: Most isotopes decay by alpha emission; external hazard minimal but internal hazard serious if ingested or inhaled.

Spontaneous Fission: Some isotopes may undergo spontaneous fission producing neutrons and fission fragments.

Daughter Products: Decay chains include other radioactive elements; secondary radiation hazards.

Handling: Atom-at-a-time techniques; remote handling essential. The short half-lives require extremely fast experimental setups.

Containment: Produced and studied in sealed systems; strict containment protocols to prevent release.

Regulations: Subject to nuclear materials regulations despite minute quantities.

Waste: Radioactive waste from production experiments managed as nuclear waste.

First Aid: Not applicable due to production scale; standard radiation emergency procedures if contamination suspected.

Protective Equipment: Standard radiation laboratory protections.

Storage: Not stored; atoms exist momentarily then decay.

Environmental: No environmental release; all material contained and decays quickly.
