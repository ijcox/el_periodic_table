Overview

Astatine is a radioactive chemical element with the symbol At and atomic number 85. It is the rarest naturally occurring element in the Earth's crust, occurring only as the decay product of various heavier elements. All isotopes of astatine are short-lived; the most stable is astatine-210, with a half-life of 8.1 hours.

Astatine is a member of the halogen group, which includes fluorine, chlorine, bromine, and iodine. It shares many properties with iodine, including accumulation in the thyroid gland. However, astatine is highly radioactive and has been the subject of limited study due to its scarcity and short half-life. Element 85 was predicted to exist by Mendeleev, who called it "eka-iodine."

Isotopes

Astatine has no stable isotopes. All isotopes are radioactive:

  • Astatine-210 (²¹⁰At) - Half-life of 8.1 hours, alpha and electron capture decay, most stable isotope
  • Astatine-211 (²¹¹At) - Half-life of 7.21 hours, alpha emitter, important for medical applications
  • Astatine-217 (²¹⁷At) - Half-life of 32.3 milliseconds, alpha emitter, occurs in uranium decay chain
  • Astatine-218 (²¹⁸At) - Half-life of 1.5 seconds, alpha and beta emitter
  • Astatine-219 (²¹⁹At) - Half-life of 56 seconds, alpha emitter
  • Astatine-220 (²²⁰At) - Half-life of 3.71 minutes, alpha emitter
  • Astatine-215 (²¹⁵At) - Half-life of 0.10 milliseconds, alpha emitter
  • Astatine-216 (²¹⁶At) - Half-life of 0.30 milliseconds, alpha emitter

Astatine-211 is particularly important for medical applications due to its alpha decay and relatively convenient half-life.

Common Uses

  • Cancer Therapy - Astatine-211 for targeted alpha therapy, especially for leukemia, brain tumors, and ovarian cancer
  • Research - Study of halogen chemistry, especially to understand trends in Group 17
  • Radioimmunotherapy - Astatine-211 attached to monoclonal antibodies for targeted cancer treatment
  • Thyroid Research - Study of thyroid physiology and diseases (astatine accumulates in thyroid like iodine)
  • Radiolabeling - Astatine compounds used as radioactive tracers in research
  • Nuclear Physics - Study of nuclear structure and decay processes

Due to its extreme rarity and short half-life, astatine has very limited practical applications, with medical uses being the most promising.

Biological Role

Astatine has no biological role but has important medical applications:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • Thyroid Accumulation - Like iodine, astatine accumulates in thyroid gland
  • Medical Applications - Astatine-211 used in targeted alpha therapy for cancer
  • Alpha Radiation - Astatine-211 decays by alpha emission; alpha particles cause intense localized cell damage
  • Radiopharmaceuticals - Astatine-211 compounds being developed for cancer treatment
  • Toxicity - Highly radioactive; dangerous if internalized due to alpha emission
  • Research Tool - Used to study thyroid physiology and iodine metabolism
  • Environmental Presence - Trace amounts in environment from natural uranium and thorium decay

Astatine's similarity to iodine allows it to target tissues that concentrate iodine, particularly the thyroid, making it useful for both thyroid imaging and cancer treatment.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Astatine is extremely rare in nature:

  • Decay Chains - Occurs in uranium-235, uranium-238, and thorium-232 decay chains
  • Abundance - Estimated less than 1 gram in Earth's crust at any time
  • Isotopes - Astatine-215, -218, and -219 occur naturally in decay chains
  • Half-lives - Natural isotopes have very short half-lives (milliseconds to minutes)

Artificial Production: Astatine is produced artificially:

  1. Nuclear Reactions - Bismuth-209 bombarded with alpha particles:
    • ²⁰⁹Bi + ⁴He → ²¹¹At + 2n
  2. Cyclotron Production - Most common method using particle accelerators
  3. Separation - Astatine separated from bismuth target by dry distillation or solvent extraction
  4. Purification - Further purification for medical or research use
  5. Alternative Methods - Proton bombardment of thorium or uranium targets

Global Production: Extremely small quantities (micrograms to milligrams per year). Production occurs at specialized nuclear facilities with particle accelerators.

Environmental Impact

Astatine has minimal environmental impact due to its extreme rarity:

  • Natural Background - Trace amounts always present from natural decay chains
  • Short Half-life - Most isotopes decay quickly, limiting environmental persistence
  • Production Impact - Minimal as produced in small quantities at controlled facilities
  • Medical Use - Used in controlled medical settings; waste carefully managed
  • Accidental Releases - Unlikely due to small quantities and controlled production
  • Waste Management - Radioactive waste from production and use requires proper disposal
  • Regulations - Strictly regulated as radioactive material
  • Research Use - Limited to specialized laboratories with appropriate facilities

Given its extreme scarcity and short half-life, astatine poses negligible environmental risk compared to other radioactive elements.

Discovery and History

The existence of element 85 was predicted by Dmitri Mendeleev in the 19th century, who called it "eka-iodine" due to its predicted position below iodine in the periodic table. Several false claims of discovery were made in the early 20th century.

Astatine was first convincingly produced in 1940 by Dale R. Corson, Kenneth Ross MacKenzie, and Emilio Segrè at the University of California, Berkeley. They bombarded bismuth-209 with alpha particles in a cyclotron, producing astatine-211. The name "astatine" was proposed by the discoverers and comes from the Greek word "astatos," meaning "unstable."

In 1943, Berta Karlik and Traude Bernert discovered astatine occurring naturally as a decay product. They detected astatine-215, -218, and -219 in natural decay chains. Due to its extreme rarity and short half-life, astatine remains one of the least studied elements.

Safety Information

High Radiotoxicity: All astatine isotopes radioactive. Astatine-211 decays by alpha emission.

Internal Hazard: Particularly dangerous if internalized (inhaled, ingested, or through wounds).

Thyroid Accumulation: Like iodine, accumulates in thyroid gland; can cause thyroid damage.

Alpha Radiation: Alpha particles cause intense localized damage if astatine incorporated into body.

External Hazard: Less hazardous externally as alpha particles stopped by skin, but still requires precautions.

Handling Precautions: Must be handled in fume hoods or glove boxes. Remote handling for significant quantities.

Protective Equipment: Lab coat, gloves, safety glasses. Additional protection depending on quantity and form.

Contamination Control: Strict procedures to prevent contamination. Monitor work areas regularly.

Storage: Store in sealed containers in designated radioactive materials areas.

Disposal: Radioactive waste. Dispose according to radioactive waste regulations.

First Aid: In case of suspected contamination: evacuate area, remove contaminated clothing, wash skin thoroughly, seek medical attention.

Regulations: Strictly regulated as radioactive material. Special licenses required for possession and use.
