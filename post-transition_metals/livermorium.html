Overview

Livermorium is a synthetic chemical element and a member of the transactinide series. It is a highly radioactive element that does not occur naturally and can only be produced in particle accelerators. Livermorium is expected to be a solid under normal conditions and is predicted to have properties similar to polonium, directly above it in group 16 of the periodic table, though relativistic effects may cause significant deviations.

Livermorium was first synthesized in 2000 by a joint Russian-American team at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR) in Dubna, Russia. The element was named after the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory in Livermore, California, USA, which collaborated with JINR on the discovery. This naming honored the contributions of American scientists to superheavy element research and recognized the longstanding collaboration between Russian and American nuclear researchers.

Livermorium's physical properties are difficult to predict due to strong relativistic effects that are expected to dominate its behavior. Unlike lighter group 16 elements (oxygen, sulfur, selenium, tellurium, polonium), livermorium may show significant deviations from expected trends due to contraction of the 7s orbital and expansion of the 7p orbitals.

Chemical Behavior Predicted to resemble polonium but with stronger relativistic effects

Livermorium's chemistry is predicted to be dominated by relativistic effects and the "inert pair effect," which is particularly strong in heavy group 16 elements. Unlike oxygen and sulfur which exhibit oxidation states from -2 to +6, livermorium is expected to favor the +2 and +4 oxidation states, with +2 possibly being more stable than +4. The -2 oxidation state (forming Lv²⁻ ions) is predicted to be unstable or nonexistent for livermorium. Livermorium is predicted to be more metallic than polonium and may form stable compounds like LvCl₂ (livermorium dichloride) and LvO₂ (livermorium dioxide). The chemistry of livermorium is expected to be more like that of a poor metal than a chalcogen.

Isotopes

Livermorium has no stable isotopes. All isotopes are radioactive and synthetic:

  • Livermorium-291 (²⁹¹Lv) - Half-life: 18 milliseconds (alpha decay)
  • Livermorium-292 (²⁹²Lv) - Half-life: 18 milliseconds (alpha decay)
  • Livermorium-293 (²⁹³Lv) - Half-life: 61 milliseconds (alpha decay) - Most stable known isotope
  • Livermorium-294 (²⁹⁴Lv) - Half-life: ? (tentative)
  • Livermorium-295 (²⁹⁵Lv) - Half-life: ? (tentative)

Livermorium-293 is the most stable known isotope with a half-life of approximately 61 milliseconds. The very short half-lives of livermorium isotopes have prevented any chemical studies so far, though theoretical predictions are extensive. Some calculations suggest that more neutron-rich isotopes with longer half-lives may exist near the "island of stability."

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Livermorium does not occur naturally. It is entirely synthetic.

Artificial Production Methods:

  1. Hot Fusion Reactions:
    ²⁴⁸Cm + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁹³Lv + 3n (original JINR synthesis, July 2000)
    ²⁴⁸Cm + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁹²Lv + 4n
  2. Other Reactions:
    ²⁴⁵Cm + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁹⁰Lv + 3n
    ²⁴⁹Cf + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁹⁴Lv + 3n (theoretical)
  3. Cold Fusion Reactions (alternative approach):
    ²⁰⁸Pb + ⁸²Se → ²⁸⁹Lv + n (theoretical, not yet achieved)
    ²⁰⁹Bi + ⁸³Rb → ²⁹¹Lv + n (theoretical)
  4. Separation - Livermorium atoms separated using gas-filled recoil separators. The Dubna Gas-Filled Recoil Separator (DGFRS) was used for its discovery and study.
  5. Detection - Individual atoms detected by their characteristic decay patterns using silicon detectors, often requiring correlation with daughter decays through alpha decay chains to flerovium and copernicium.
  6. Quantity - Production rates extremely low (a few atoms per year of beam time).

Production Sites: Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR, Dubna, Russia - primary site), Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (USA). Total world production is only about 10-15 atoms.

Discovery and History

The discovery of livermorium continued the successful collaboration between Russian and American scientists in superheavy element research:

Discovery (2000): In July 2000, a joint Russian-American team at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR) in Dubna, Russia, synthesized livermorium by bombarding curium-248 with calcium-48 ions. They produced atoms of livermorium-292, which decayed by alpha emission with a half-life of about 46 milliseconds. The discovery was announced in December 2000.

Confirmation and Further Studies: The discovery was confirmed in 2001 and 2004 at JINR with the production of more atoms, including the isotope livermorium-293. The decay chains were studied in detail, confirming the original discovery.

Independent Confirmation (2009): In 2009, a team at the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory independently confirmed the discovery of livermorium, providing additional validation.

Naming: The JINR team proposed the name "livermorium" after the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory in California, USA, which had collaborated on the discovery. This honored the contributions of American scientists and recognized the international collaboration.

Official Recognition (2012): On May 30, 2012, IUPAC officially announced the name "livermorium" with symbol Lv. The name was approved after evaluation and public comment.

Significance: Livermorium's discovery represented continued progress in superheavy element research and demonstrated the effectiveness of international collaboration in fundamental science. The naming honored both the laboratory and the collaborative spirit that made the discovery possible.

Theoretical Interest: Livermorium has attracted theoretical interest due to predictions about its chemical behavior, particularly regarding which oxidation states will be stable and how metallic it will be compared to lighter chalcogens.

Safety Information

Radioactivity: All isotopes of livermorium are highly radioactive. Primary hazard is alpha radiation.

Production: Produced only in specialized nuclear facilities with heavy ion accelerators. Not available outside research laboratories.

Quantity: Only atom quantities produced; insufficient for bulk handling or conventional chemical study.

Alpha Radiation: Most isotopes decay by alpha emission; external hazard minimal but internal hazard serious if ingested or inhaled.

Spontaneous Fission: Some isotopes may undergo spontaneous fission producing neutrons and fission fragments.

Daughter Products: Decay chains include other radioactive elements; secondary radiation hazards.

Handling: Atom-at-a-time techniques; remote handling essential. The short half-lives require extremely fast experimental setups.

Containment: Produced and studied in sealed systems; strict containment protocols to prevent release.

Regulations: Subject to nuclear materials regulations despite minute quantities.

Waste: Radioactive waste from production experiments managed as nuclear waste.

First Aid: Not applicable due to production scale; standard radiation emergency procedures if contamination suspected.

Protective Equipment: Standard radiation laboratory protections.

Storage: Not stored; atoms exist momentarily then decay.

Environmental: No environmental release; all material contained and decays quickly.
