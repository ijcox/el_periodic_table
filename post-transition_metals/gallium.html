Overview

Gallium is a soft, silvery metal with a brilliant luster that melts just above room temperature (29.76°C or 85.57°F). It is one of only five elemental metals that are liquid at or near room temperature, along with mercury, cesium, rubidium, and francium. Gallium expands by 3.1% when it freezes, a property it shares with water, bismuth, and germanium.

The element was predicted by Dmitri Mendeleev in 1871 as "eka-aluminum" based on gaps in his periodic table. Gallium was discovered spectroscopically by French chemist Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran in 1875. He named it "gallium" from the Latin word "Gallia" meaning France, or possibly from his own name "Lecoq" which means "the rooster" in French ("gallus" in Latin).

Isotopes

Gallium has two stable isotopes:

  • Gallium-69 (⁶⁹Ga) - 60.11% abundance
  • Gallium-71 (⁷¹Ga) - 39.89% abundance

Gallium has several radioactive isotopes, with gallium-67 and gallium-68 being medically important:

  • Gallium-67 (⁶⁷Ga) - Radioactive isotope with half-life of 3.26 days; used in nuclear medicine imaging for inflammation and cancer detection
  • Gallium-68 (⁶⁸Ga) - Radioactive isotope with half-life of 67.71 minutes; used in positron emission tomography (PET) imaging
  • Gallium-72 (⁷²Ga) - Radioactive isotope with half-life of 14.1 hours
  • Gallium-73 (⁷³Ga) - Radioactive isotope with half-life of 4.86 hours

Gallium-67 is produced in cyclotrons and gallium-68 is typically obtained from germanium-68 generators.

Common Uses

  • Semiconductors - Gallium arsenide (GaAs) and gallium nitride (GaN) for high-speed integrated circuits, LEDs, laser diodes, solar cells, and microwave devices
  • Low-Melting Alloys - Galinstan (gallium-indium-tin alloy) melts at -19°C and replaces mercury in thermometers
  • Medical Imaging - Radioactive gallium-67 and gallium-68 for cancer detection and inflammation imaging
  • LEDs and Lasers - Gallium nitride for blue and white LEDs; gallium arsenide for infrared lasers
  • Solar Cells - Gallium arsenide for high-efficiency solar cells used in spacecraft and concentrated photovoltaics
  • Mirrors - Gallium-based liquid mirrors for telescopes (rotating mercury alternatives)
  • Neutrino Detection - Gallium-71 in gallium-germanium neutrino telescopes (GALLEX and SAGE experiments)
  • Dental Alloys - Gallium alloys as mercury-free alternative in dental amalgams
  • Thermometric Fluid - Liquid gallium for high-temperature thermometers (up to 1200°C)
  • Coolants - Liquid gallium or gallium alloys for high-temperature cooling systems

Biological Role

Gallium has no known essential biological role but has interesting biological interactions:

  • Iron Mimic - Gallium(III) ions resemble iron(III) in size and charge, allowing them to disrupt iron metabolism in bacteria and cancer cells
  • Medical Applications - Gallium nitrate used to treat hypercalcemia (high blood calcium) and certain cancers
  • Antimicrobial Properties - Gallium compounds show activity against antibiotic-resistant bacteria like Pseudomonas aeruginosa
  • Radiopharmaceuticals - Gallium-67 citrate scans detect inflammation, infection, and certain cancers (lymphomas)
  • Bone Seeking - Gallium accumulates in areas of rapid bone turnover, useful for detecting bone metastases
  • Toxicity - Gallium compounds are generally of low toxicity but can cause kidney damage at high doses
  • Environmental Exposure - Natural gallium levels in human body are extremely low (<0.001 mg)
  • Research Tool - Gallium used to study iron metabolism pathways in biological systems

Gallium's ability to mimic iron makes it useful both as a therapeutic agent and as a research tool in biology.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Gallium is the 34th most abundant element in Earth's crust (19 ppm). It does not occur free in nature but is found:

  • As Trace Element - In bauxite (aluminum ore) and zinc ores
  • Germanite - Cu₁₃Fe₂Ge₂S₁₆ (contains up to 1% gallium)
  • Sphalerite - (Zn,Fe)S (zinc sulfide, contains small amounts of gallium)
  • Coal - Some coal deposits contain elevated gallium concentrations
  • Byproduct Source - Over 90% of gallium is extracted as byproduct of aluminum production from bauxite

Industrial Production: Gallium is produced through several processes:

  1. From Bayer Process Liquor - Gallium accumulates in sodium aluminate solution during aluminum production; extracted by electrolysis or precipitation
  2. From Zinc Processing - Gallium recovered from zinc refinery residues
  3. Purification - Crude gallium purified by fractional crystallization, zone refining, or electrochemical methods
  4. High-Purity Gallium - Zone refining produces gallium with purity >99.9999% for semiconductor applications

Global Production: About 300 tons annually. China produces about 80%, followed by Germany, Japan, and Ukraine.

Environmental Impact

Gallium production and use have relatively low environmental impact compared to many metals:

  • Byproduct Recovery - Most gallium is recovered as byproduct, minimizing additional mining impacts
  • Low Toxicity - Elemental gallium and its compounds have low toxicity to humans and ecosystems
  • Arsenic Concerns - Gallium arsenide production involves toxic arsenic requiring careful handling
  • Energy Intensive - Gallium production, especially high-purity gallium, requires significant energy
  • E-Waste Recycling - Gallium recovery from electronic waste is challenging but increasingly important
  • Water Conservation - Gallium extraction from Bayer liquor conserves water by recycling process streams
  • Green Technology - Gallium enables energy-efficient LEDs and high-efficiency solar cells, reducing overall energy consumption
  • Supply Constraints - Limited production capacity could constrain growth of gallium-dependent technologies
  • Strategic Material - Gallium classified as critical/strategic material by EU, USA, and Japan due to limited supply and important applications

Discovery and History

The existence of gallium was first predicted by Dmitri Mendeleev in 1871. Based on his newly created periodic table, he predicted an element he called "eka-aluminum" with atomic weight about 68, density 5.9 g/cm³, and low melting point.

In 1875, French chemist Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran discovered gallium spectroscopically. He observed two previously unreported violet lines in the spectrum of a zinc blend from the Pyrenees. Later that year, he isolated the metal by electrolysis of gallium hydroxide in potassium hydroxide solution.

When Mendeleev learned of the discovery, he noted the close match between his predictions and the actual properties of gallium. This successful prediction provided strong evidence for the validity of the periodic table.

Gallium remained a laboratory curiosity until the mid-20th century when its semiconductor properties were discovered. The development of gallium arsenide in the 1960s revolutionized electronics, enabling devices that operate at higher frequencies and temperatures than silicon.

Interesting Facts

  • Gallium can supercool - remain liquid below its melting point - down to -40°C if undisturbed
  • It wets glass and skin, making it difficult to handle (leaves gray stains)
  • Gallium has the largest liquid range of any element (over 2200°C between melting and boiling points)
  • The "gallium beating heart" experiment shows gallium pulsating due to surface tension changes when placed in sulfuric acid and potassium dichromate
  • Gallium is one of four metals (with cesium, rubidium, and mercury) liquid near room temperature
  • It can be used to make spoons that melt when stirring hot tea (a classic chemistry prank)
  • Gallium infiltrates aluminum's crystal structure, making aluminum brittle - gallium can cause aluminum to crumble like wet cardboard

Safety Information

Skin Contact: Liquid gallium wets skin and leaves gray stains; not particularly toxic but can be difficult to remove. Use gloves when handling.

Metal Embrittlement: Gallium causes rapid failure of aluminum structures by liquid metal embrittlement. Avoid contact with aluminum containers or components.

Gallium Arsenide: Highly toxic due to arsenic content; handle with appropriate precautions for arsenic compounds.

Gallium Trichloride: Corrosive and releases HCl in moist air; causes severe burns to skin and eyes.

Inhalation: Gallium compounds may cause respiratory irritation; use local exhaust ventilation.

Environmental: Gallium has low environmental toxicity but gallium arsenide requires special disposal as hazardous waste.

Medical Use: Gallium-67 and gallium-68 are radioactive; follow radiation safety protocols.

Storage: Store in glass or plastic containers (not aluminum or steel). Keep away from acids and oxidizers.

First Aid: For skin contact, wash with soap and water. For eye contact, flush with water for 15 minutes. For ingestion, drink water and seek medical attention.

Spill Cleanup: Small amounts can be solidified by cooling below 29°C. Larger spills require professional cleanup.
