Overview

Moscovium is a synthetic chemical element and a member of the transactinide series. It is a highly radioactive element that does not occur naturally and can only be produced in particle accelerators. Moscovium is expected to be a solid under normal conditions and is predicted to have properties similar to bismuth, directly above it in group 15 of the periodic table, though relativistic effects may cause significant deviations.

Moscovium was first synthesized in 2003 by a joint Russian-American team at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR) in Dubna, Russia. The element was named after the Moscow Oblast region of Russia, where the JINR laboratory is located. This continued the tradition of naming elements after geographical locations associated with their discovery laboratories (berkelium, californium, dubnium, hassium, darmstadtium).

Moscovium's physical properties are difficult to predict due to strong relativistic effects that are expected to dominate its behavior. Unlike lighter group 15 elements (nitrogen, phosphorus, arsenic, antimony, bismuth), moscovium may show significant deviations from expected trends due to contraction of the 7s orbital and expansion of the 7p orbitals.

Chemical Behavior Predicted to resemble bismuth but with stronger relativistic effects

Moscovium's chemistry is predicted to be dominated by relativistic effects and the "inert pair effect," which is particularly strong in heavy group 15 elements. Unlike nitrogen and phosphorus which can exhibit oxidation states up to +5, moscovium is expected to favor the +1 and +3 oxidation states, with +3 being more stable than +5 (unlike lighter group 15 elements). The +5 state may be unstable or nonexistent for moscovium. Moscovium is predicted to be more metallic than bismuth and may form stable compounds like McCl₃ (moscovium trichloride) and Mc₂O₃ (moscovium trioxide). The Mc³⁺ ion is expected to be more stable than Bi³⁺ due to relativistic stabilization of the 7s² electron pair.

  • Moscovium-291 (²⁹¹Mc) - Half-life: ? (tentative)
  • Moscovium-292 (²⁹²Mc) - Half-life: ? (tentative)
  • Moscovium-293 (²⁹³Mc) - Half-life: ? (tentative)

Moscovium-290 is the most stable known isotope with a half-life of approximately 0.65 seconds. The very short half-lives of moscovium isotopes have prevented any chemical studies so far, though theoretical predictions are extensive. Some calculations suggest that more neutron-rich isotopes with longer half-lives may exist near the "island of stability."

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Moscovium does not occur naturally. It is entirely synthetic.

Artificial Production Methods:

  1. Hot Fusion Reactions:
    ²⁴³Am + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁸⁸Mc + 3n (original JINR synthesis, 2003)
    ²⁴³Am + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁸⁹Mc + 2n
  2. Other Reactions:
    ²⁴⁵Am + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁹⁰Mc + 3n
    ²⁴⁸Cm + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁹²Mc + 4n (theoretical)
  3. Cold Fusion Reactions (alternative approach):
    ²⁰⁹Bi + ⁸⁴Sr → ²⁹²Mc + n (theoretical, not yet achieved)
    ²⁰⁸Pb + ⁸³Rb → ²⁹⁰Mc + n (theoretical)
  4. Separation - Moscovium atoms separated using gas-filled recoil separators. The Dubna Gas-Filled Recoil Separator (DGFRS) was used for its discovery and study.
  5. Detection - Individual atoms detected by their characteristic decay patterns using silicon detectors, often requiring correlation with daughter decays through alpha decay chains to nihonium and roentgenium.
  6. Quantity - Production rates extremely low (a few atoms per year of beam time).

Production Sites: Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR, Dubna, Russia - primary site), Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (USA). Total world production is only about 10-15 atoms.

Discovery and History

The discovery of moscovium was part of JINR Dubna's systematic exploration of superheavy elements:

Discovery (2003): In February 2003, a joint Russian-American team at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR) in Dubna, Russia, synthesized moscovium by bombarding americium-243 with calcium-48 ions. They produced atoms of moscovium-288 and moscovium-287, which decayed by alpha emission with half-lives of about 100 milliseconds. The discovery was announced in February 2004.

Confirmation and Further Studies: The discovery was confirmed in 2004 and 2010 at JINR with the production of more atoms. The decay chains were studied in detail, confirming the original discovery.

Independent Confirmation (2013): In 2013, a team at the GSI Helmholtz Centre in Germany independently confirmed the discovery of moscovium, providing additional validation.

Naming: The JINR team initially proposed the name "moscovium" after the Moscow Oblast region of Russia, where Dubna is located. This followed the tradition of naming elements after geographical locations associated with their discovery.

Official Recognition (2016): On November 28, 2016, IUPAC officially announced the name "moscovium" with symbol Mc. The name was approved after the usual period of public comment and review.

Significance: Moscovium's discovery represented continued progress in superheavy element research and demonstrated the viability of the "hot fusion" approach using calcium-48 beams on actinide targets. This approach has proven particularly successful for producing elements 113-118.

Theoretical Interest: Moscovium has attracted theoretical interest due to predictions about its chemical behavior, particularly regarding the strength of the inert pair effect and possible unusual oxidation states.

Safety Information

Radioactivity: All isotopes of moscovium are highly radioactive. Primary hazard is alpha radiation.

Production: Produced only in specialized nuclear facilities with heavy ion accelerators. Not available outside research laboratories.

Quantity: Only atom quantities produced; insufficient for bulk handling or conventional chemical study.

Alpha Radiation: Most isotopes decay by alpha emission; external hazard minimal but internal hazard serious if ingested or inhaled.

Spontaneous Fission: Some isotopes may undergo spontaneous fission producing neutrons and fission fragments.

Daughter Products: Decay chains include other radioactive elements; secondary radiation hazards.

Handling: Atom-at-a-time techniques; remote handling essential. The short half-lives require extremely fast experimental setups.

Containment: Produced and studied in sealed systems; strict containment protocols to prevent release.

Regulations: Subject to nuclear materials regulations despite minute quantities.

Waste: Radioactive waste from production experiments managed as nuclear waste.

First Aid: Not applicable due to production scale; standard radiation emergency procedures if contamination suspected.

Protective Equipment: Standard radiation laboratory protections.

Storage: Not stored; atoms exist momentarily then decay.

Environmental: No environmental release; all material contained and decays quickly.
