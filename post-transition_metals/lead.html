Overview

Lead is a heavy metal that is denser than most common materials. It is a relatively unreactive post-transition metal that is soft, malleable, and has a relatively low melting point. Freshly cut lead has a bluish-white color that tarnishes to a dull gray when exposed to air. Lead has the highest atomic number of any stable element and three of its isotopes are endpoints of major nuclear decay chains of heavier elements.

Lead has been used by humans for thousands of years. The Romans used lead extensively for water pipes, cooking utensils, and as a sweetener in wine (lead acetate). The English word "plumbing" comes from the Latin word for lead, "plumbum," which is also the origin of lead's chemical symbol Pb. While lead has many useful properties, its toxicity has led to a dramatic reduction in its use in recent decades.

Isotopes

Lead has four stable isotopes:

  • Lead-204 (²⁰⁴Pb) - 1.4% abundance (primordial, not radiogenic)
  • Lead-206 (²⁰⁶Pb) - 24.1% abundance (radiogenic, from uranium-238 decay)
  • Lead-207 (²⁰⁷Pb) - 22.1% abundance (radiogenic, from uranium-235 decay)
  • Lead-208 (²⁰⁸Pb) - 52.4% abundance (radiogenic, from thorium-232 decay)

Lead has several radioactive isotopes:

  • Lead-210 (²¹⁰Pb) - Half-life of 22.3 years, used in dating sediments
  • Lead-212 (²¹²Pb) - Half-life of 10.64 hours, used in cancer therapy
  • Lead-214 (²¹⁴Pb) - Half-life of 26.8 minutes, part of uranium decay chain

Lead isotopes are important in radiometric dating. The ratio of lead-206 to lead-204, lead-207 to lead-204, and lead-208 to lead-204 are used in uranium-lead dating to determine the age of rocks.

Common Uses

  • Lead-Acid Batteries - Primary use today (about 80% of consumption); automotive and backup power batteries
  • Radiation Shielding - X-ray rooms, nuclear applications, radioactive source containers
  • Construction - Roofing, flashing, stained glass, and in some older water pipes
  • Historical Uses - Paint pigment (white lead), gasoline additive (tetraethyllead), ammunition
  • Solder - Lead-tin alloys for electronics and plumbing (now largely replaced by lead-free solders)
  • Weights - Fishing sinkers, diving weights, wheel balancing weights
  • Glass Manufacturing - Lead crystal glass (24-40% lead oxide) for fine glassware
  • Cable Sheathing - Protecting underground and underwater cables
  • Pigments - Some specialty paints and artists' colors still contain lead compounds
  • Historical Medicine - Formerly used in various medicinal preparations (now recognized as toxic)

Biological Role

Lead has no known essential biological role and is highly toxic:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • Neurotoxin - Particularly harmful to developing nervous systems in children
  • Calcium Mimic - Lead ions mimic calcium ions; disrupt calcium-dependent processes
  • Enzyme Inhibition - Binds to sulfhydryl groups in enzymes, inhibiting their function
  • Hematological Effects - Inhibits heme synthesis, causing anemia
  • Developmental Effects - Childhood exposure causes reduced IQ, learning disabilities, behavioral problems
  • Renal Damage - Chronic exposure damages kidneys
  • Reproductive Effects - Can cause miscarriage, reduced fertility, developmental problems
  • Accumulation - Lead accumulates in bones (replaces calcium), with half-life of years to decades

There is no safe level of lead exposure, particularly for children. Even low levels can cause neurological damage.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Lead is relatively abundant (14 ppm in Earth's crust):

  • Galena (PbS) - Primary lead ore (86.6% lead by weight)
  • Other Minerals - Cerussite (PbCO₃), anglesite (PbSO₄)
  • Byproduct - Also obtained as byproduct from zinc, silver, and copper mining
  • Major Deposits - China, Australia, United States, Peru, Mexico

Industrial Production: Lead extraction from galena:

  1. Mining - Usually underground mining of galena ore
  2. Concentration - Ore crushed and concentrated by froth flotation
  3. Roasting
  4. Smelting
  5. Refining

Global Production: About 4.5 million tonnes annually. China produces about 50% of world supply. Recycling provides about 60% of lead supply in developed countries.

Environmental Impact

Lead pollution is a major global environmental health problem:

  • Historical Contamination - Centuries of lead use have left widespread contamination
  • Lead Paint - Major source of childhood lead poisoning in older housing
  • Soil Contamination - From leaded gasoline, industrial emissions, paint
  • Water Contamination - Lead pipes and solder can leach lead into drinking water
  • Mining and Smelting - Major sources of environmental lead pollution
  • Recycling - Lead-acid battery recycling can cause contamination if not properly managed
  • Wildlife Impacts
  • Atmospheric Transport
  • Cleanup Costs
  • Regulations

Despite regulations, lead remains one of the most significant environmental health threats globally.

Discovery and History

Lead has been used since prehistoric times. It is one of the metals known to the ancients, along with gold, silver, copper, iron, and tin. Lead beads dating back to 6400 BCE have been found in Turkey. The Romans used lead extensively - for water pipes (from which we get the word "plumbing" from the Latin "plumbum"), as a sweetener in wine (lead acetate), and in cooking utensils.

During the Industrial Revolution, lead production increased dramatically. In the 20th century, lead use expanded with the invention of lead-acid batteries and the addition of tetraethyllead to gasoline as an anti-knock agent. The toxicity of lead was recognized by the ancient Greeks and Romans, but was largely ignored until the 20th century when systematic studies demonstrated its harmful effects, particularly on children.

The phase-out of leaded gasoline beginning in the 1970s is considered one of the great public health successes of the 20th century, dramatically reducing blood lead levels in populations worldwide.

Interesting Facts

  • The chemical symbol Pb comes from Latin "plumbum" meaning "liquid silver"
  • Lead is the final stable element in the periodic table
  • Lead was used by alchemists who believed it could be turned into gold

Safety Information

High Toxicity: Lead and lead compounds toxic, especially to children. No safe exposure level.

Exposure Routes: Inhalation of dust/fumes, ingestion (especially hand-to-mouth in children), skin absorption for some compounds.

Acute Poisoning Symptoms: Abdominal pain, constipation, headaches, irritability, memory problems, infertility, tingling in hands/feet.

Chronic Effects: Anemia, kidney damage, high blood pressure, reproductive problems, developmental delays in children.

Children at Special Risk: More susceptible due to developing nervous systems; lower exposure levels cause effects.

Lead in Homes: Lead-based paint (pre-1978), contaminated soil, lead pipes/solder in plumbing.

Occupational Exposure: Battery manufacturing, lead smelting, construction (renovation), shooting ranges.

Protective Equipment: Use respirators for dust/fumes, gloves, protective clothing. Wash hands before eating/smoking.

Storage: Store away from food and beverages. Label containers clearly.

Disposal: Lead and lead-containing waste must be disposed as hazardous waste.

First Aid: For inhalation, move to fresh air. For skin contact, wash with soap and water. For eye contact, flush with water for 15 minutes. For ingestion, seek medical attention. Chelation therapy used for treatment.
