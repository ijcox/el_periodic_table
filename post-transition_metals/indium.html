Overview

Indium is a soft, silvery-white, highly ductile post-transition metal with a bright luster. It is so soft (Mohs hardness 1.2) that it can be cut with a knife and leaves a visible line on paper. Indium has a low melting point for a metal and wets glass, making it useful for sealing glass to metal. The metal emits a high-pitched "cry" when bent, similar to tin. Indium is relatively rare in Earth's crust and is primarily recovered as a byproduct of zinc refining.

Indium was discovered in 1863 by German chemists Ferdinand Reich and Hieronymous Theodor Richter while they were testing zinc ore samples from the Freiberg mines. They were looking for thallium using spectroscopy but instead found a bright indigo-blue spectral line, which indicated a new element. Richter isolated the metal in 1864 and named it "indium" from the Latin "indicum" meaning "indigo," referring to the color of its spectral line.

Isotopes

Indium has two naturally occurring isotopes:

  • Indium-113 (¹¹³In) - 4.29% abundance, stable
  • Indium-115 (¹¹⁵In) - 95.71% abundance, radioactive with half-life of 4.41×10¹⁴ years (441 trillion years)

Indium-115 is technically radioactive but its half-life is so long (100,000 times the age of the universe) that it is effectively stable for most purposes.

Indium has several artificial radioactive isotopes with medical and industrial applications:

  • Indium-111 (¹¹¹In) - Radioactive isotope with half-life of 2.8047 days; used in nuclear medicine for diagnostic imaging
  • Indium-114m (¹¹⁴ᵐIn) - Metastable isomer with half-life of 49.51 days
  • Indium-113m (¹¹³ᵐIn) - Metastable isomer with half-life of 1.658 hours; used in nuclear medicine
  • Indium-115m (¹¹⁵ᵐIn) - Metastable isomer with half-life of 4.486 hours

Indium-111 is particularly important for medical imaging, especially for labeling white blood cells and antibodies.

Common Uses

  • Transparent Conductive Coatings - Indium tin oxide (ITO) on glass and plastic for touch screens, LCD displays, solar panels, and aircraft windows
  • Semiconductors - Indium compounds (indium phosphide, indium arsenide, indium antimonide) in high-speed electronics, infrared detectors, and LEDs
  • Solders and Alloys - Low-melting point indium alloys for soldering, sealing, and fusible links
  • Bearings and Coatings - Indium coatings for engine bearings to improve performance and reduce wear
  • Nuclear Medicine - Indium-111 for diagnostic imaging (white blood cell labeling, octreotide scans)
  • Photovoltaic Cells - Copper indium gallium selenide (CIGS) thin-film solar cells
  • Lighting - Indium in some fluorescent lamps and as component in blue and white LEDs
  • Thermal Interface Materials - Indium foil as thermal interface material in high-performance electronics
  • Glass Sealing - Indium seals for glass-to-glass or glass-to-metal joints in vacuum systems
  • Dental Alloys - Indium in some dental amalgams to reduce mercury evaporation

Biological Role

Indium has no known essential biological role but has some medical applications:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • Medical Imaging - Indium-111 used in nuclear medicine for diagnostic procedures:
    • Labeling white blood cells to detect infections and inflammation
    • Octreotide scans for neuroendocrine tumors
    • ProstaScint® scans for prostate cancer
  • Low Toxicity - Elemental indium and most compounds have low to moderate toxicity
  • Indium Lung Disease - Inhalation of indium compounds (especially indium tin oxide) can cause severe lung damage; "indium lung" recognized occupational disease
  • Environmental Exposure - Trace amounts in human body from environmental sources; no known health effects at normal levels
  • Research - Indium compounds studied for potential anticancer and antimicrobial properties
  • Dietary Sources - Extremely low levels in foods; typical daily intake less than 1 μg

Indium's primary biological significance is in medical imaging with indium-111, though occupational exposure to indium compounds poses health risks.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Indium is a rare element in Earth's crust (0.1 ppm). It occurs:

  • In Zinc Ores - As trace impurity in sphalerite (ZnS) and other zinc minerals (primary commercial source)
  • In Lead and Tin Ores - Trace amounts in some lead and tin minerals
  • Rare Minerals - Indite (FeIn₂S₄) and dzhalindite (In(OH)₃) are rare indium minerals
  • As Byproduct - Over 95% of indium produced as byproduct of zinc refining
  • In Seawater - Extremely low concentration (0.1 μg/L)

Industrial Production: Indium is recovered during zinc production:

  1. Zinc Production - Zinc ores roasted to produce zinc oxide
  2. Leaching - Zinc oxide leached with sulfuric acid; indium dissolves with zinc
  3. Purification - Solution purified by zinc dust cementation; indium concentrates in residue
  4. Extraction - Indium extracted from residues by leaching with acid
  5. Precipitation - Indium precipitated as hydroxide or cementated with aluminum or zinc
  6. Electrolysis - Indium sponge dissolved and electrorefined to produce high-purity indium (99.99%+)
  7. Zone Refining - Ultra-high purity indium (99.9999%+) produced by zone refining for semiconductor applications

Global Production: About 900 tons annually. China produces about 60%, followed by South Korea, Japan, and Canada.

Environmental Impact

Indium production and use have several environmental considerations:

  • Byproduct Recovery - Recovery from zinc refining minimizes additional mining impacts
  • Energy Intensive - Production and refining require significant energy, especially for high-purity indium
  • Electronic Waste - Indium in electronics (especially ITO coatings) rarely recovered due to technical challenges
  • Supply Constraints - Limited reserves and byproduct nature create supply chain vulnerabilities
  • Occupational Health - "Indium lung" disease from inhalation of indium compounds in manufacturing
  • Recycling - Limited recycling from manufacturing scrap; end-of-life recycling from products challenging
  • Toxicity - Indium compounds have low to moderate toxicity; indium tin oxide dust particularly hazardous
  • Green Technology Component - Indium enables energy-efficient displays and solar cells
  • Price Volatility - Small market and supply constraints lead to price volatility

Indium's use in energy-efficient technologies provides environmental benefits, but sustainable supply and safe handling are concerns.

Discovery and History

In 1863, German chemists Ferdinand Reich and Hieronymous Theodor Richter at the Freiberg University of Mining and Technology were examining zinc ore samples from the Himmelsfürst mine. Reich, who was colorblind, asked his assistant Richter to observe the spectral lines.

When they tested the ore spectroscopically, they observed a bright indigo-blue line that didn't correspond to any known element. This indicated the presence of a new element. Because of the line's color, they named the element "indium" from the Latin "indicum" meaning "indigo."

Richter isolated the metal in 1864 by treating the zinc ore with hydrochloric acid and precipitating indium as hydroxide. He then reduced the oxide with charcoal to obtain the metal. Initially, they thought indium was divalent like zinc, but later work showed it was trivalent.

For many years, indium had few applications. Its importance grew with the development of semiconductors in the mid-20th century and especially with the invention of indium tin oxide (ITO) in the 1950s. Today, indium is crucial for modern electronics and display technologies.

Interesting Facts

  • Indium is so soft you can mold it with your fingers and cut it with a knife
  • The element makes a high-pitched "scream" or "cry" when bent, similar to tin
  • Indium tin oxide (ITO) makes touch screens work - it's transparent but conducts electricity
  • Indium was named for the indigo-blue line in its spectrum
  • The metal wets glass, making it useful for creating vacuum-tight seals between glass and metal
  • Most indium comes from zinc mining - it's too rare to mine on its own
  • Indium-115 is technically radioactive but with a half-life of 441 trillion years

Safety Information

Indium Lung Disease: Inhalation of indium compounds, especially indium tin oxide (ITO) dust, causes severe lung damage: interstitial pneumonia, pulmonary fibrosis, emphysema. Can be fatal.

Metal Fume Fever: Inhalation of indium oxide fumes may cause metal fume fever with flu-like symptoms.

Chemical Compounds: Indium compounds generally have low to moderate toxicity. Soluble compounds more toxic than insoluble forms.

Occupational Exposure: Workers in ITO manufacturing, semiconductor, and soldering industries at risk. Use engineering controls and respiratory protection.

Medical Monitoring: Workers with indium exposure should have regular medical exams including chest X-rays and pulmonary function tests.

First Aid: For inhalation, move to fresh air. For skin contact, wash with soap and water. For eye contact, flush with water for 15 minutes.

Protective Equipment: Use gloves, safety glasses, and appropriate respiratory protection when handling indium compounds. Use local exhaust ventilation.

Storage: Store indium metal in cool, dry place. Store compounds in well-sealed containers.

Radioactive Materials: Indium-111 requires radiation safety precautions for medical use.

Environmental: Indium compounds have low to moderate environmental toxicity. Proper disposal required.
