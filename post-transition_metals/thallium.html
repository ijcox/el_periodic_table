Overview

Thallium is a soft, malleable, silvery-white post-transition metal that resembles tin but discolors when exposed to air. It is highly toxic and was used in rat poisons and insecticides, though its use has been restricted in many countries. Thallium is not found free in nature and is typically obtained as a byproduct from refining heavy metal sulfide ores.

The metal was discovered in 1861 by William Crookes and Claude-Auguste Lamy independently, both working on residues from sulfuric acid production. The name comes from the Greek "thallos," meaning "green shoot" or "twig," because of the bright green spectral emission lines that identified the element. Thallium compounds have been infamously used as poisons in real-life murder cases and mystery novels.

Isotopes

Thallium has two stable isotopes:

  • Thallium-203 (²⁰³Tl) - 29.524% abundance
  • Thallium-205 (²⁰⁵Tl) - 70.476% abundance

Thallium has several radioactive isotopes:

  • Thallium-201 (²⁰¹Tl) - Half-life of 73 hours, used in nuclear medicine for cardiac imaging
  • Thallium-204 (²⁰⁴Tl) - Half-life of 3.78 years, beta emitter used in industrial gauges
  • Thallium-207 (²⁰⁷Tl) - Half-life of 4.77 minutes
  • Thallium-208 (²⁰⁸Tl) - Half-life of 3.053 minutes

Thallium-201 is important in nuclear medicine for myocardial perfusion imaging (stress tests) to diagnose coronary artery disease.

Common Uses

  • Nuclear Medicine - Thallium-201 for cardiac stress tests and tumor detection
  • Historical Rodenticides - Thallium sulfate used as rat poison (now banned in many countries)
  • Optical Lenses
  • Electronics - Thallium compounds in semiconductors, photoelectric cells, and infrared detectors
  • Low-Temperature Thermometers - Thallium amalgams for thermometers measuring below -40°C
  • Glass Manufacturing - Thallium oxide in high-refractive-index optical glass
  • Historical Medical Use - Formerly used to treat ringworm and other infections (now discontinued due to toxicity)
  • Superconductors - Thallium-based high-temperature superconductors
  • Industrial Gauges - Thallium-204 in thickness gauges and density meters
  • Research - Thallium compounds in chemical and materials research

Biological Role

Thallium has no known essential biological role and is highly toxic:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • Extreme Toxicity - More toxic than lead, mercury, or cadmium
  • Potassium Mimic - Thallium(I) ions similar size to potassium ions; disrupts potassium-dependent processes
  • Neurotoxicity - Damages peripheral nerves; causes painful neuropathy ("burning feet" syndrome)
  • Hair Loss - Characteristic symptom of thallium poisoning (alopecia)
  • Gastrointestinal Effects - Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, constipation followed by diarrhea
  • Medical Imaging - Radioactive thallium-201 used safely in diagnostic imaging
  • Environmental Exposure - Trace amounts from coal combustion and metal smelting
  • Accumulation - Thallium accumulates in bones, kidneys, and nervous tissue

Thallium's toxicity arises from its ability to mimic potassium, disrupting essential cellular processes throughout the body.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Thallium is a rare element (0.7 ppm in Earth's crust):

  • In Sulfide Ores - Associated with zinc, lead, copper, and pyrite ores
  • As Trace Element - In minerals like crookesite, lorandite, and hutchinsonite
  • Coal - Trace amounts in coal; released during combustion
  • Byproduct Source - Primarily from flue dust and residues from smelting other metals

Industrial Production: Thallium obtained as byproduct:

  1. Smelting Residues - Collected from flue dust in lead and zinc smelters
  2. Leaching - Residues leached with sulfuric acid or other solvents
  3. Precipitation
  4. Electrolysis
  5. Purification

Global Production: About 10 tonnes annually. Production limited due to toxicity and limited applications. Main producers: China, Kazakhstan, Russia.

Environmental Impact

Thallium is a significant environmental pollutant despite low production volumes:

  • Coal Combustion - Major source of environmental thallium release
  • Metal Smelting - Zinc, lead, and copper smelting release thallium
  • Cement Production - Trace thallium in raw materials released during production
  • Water Contamination - Mining and industrial activities can contaminate water sources
  • Bioaccumulation - Thallium accumulates in plants and aquatic organisms
  • Toxicity to Wildlife - Toxic to aquatic life, birds, and mammals
  • Historical Pesticides - Legacy contamination from historical pesticide use
  • Regulations - Thallium emissions regulated in many countries
  • Waste Management - Thallium-containing waste requires special handling

Thallium's extreme toxicity makes even low-level environmental contamination a significant concern.

Discovery and History

Thallium was discovered independently in 1861 by William Crookes in England and Claude-Auguste Lamy in France. Crookes was using spectroscopy to study selenium-containing residues from sulfuric acid production when he observed a bright green spectral line that didn't correspond to any known element. He named the new element "thallium" from the Greek "thallos," meaning "green shoot" or "twig."

Lamy isolated the metal in larger quantities and determined some of its properties. Initially, there was a priority dispute between Crookes and Lamy, but both are now credited with the discovery. Crookes received the medal at the 1862 International Exhibition in London for his discovery.

The extreme toxicity of thallium became apparent soon after its discovery. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, thallium compounds were used medicinally (for treating ringworm and as a depilatory) and as rodenticides. These uses declined as the dangers became better understood, though criminal poisonings with thallium continued to occur.

Interesting Facts

  • The name comes from Greek "thallos" meaning "green twig" for its green spectral line
  • Thallium was once called the "poisoner's poison" and featured in Agatha Christie novels
  • Thallium poisoning causes characteristic "Mee's lines" on fingernails
  • Radioactive thallium-201 saves lives by diagnosing heart disease
  • Thallium sulfate was the active ingredient in some rat poisons until banned
  • Thallium ions can replace potassium ions in biological systems, causing toxicity
  • A thallium compound was used in a famous 1950s murder case in Australia

Safety Information

Extreme Toxicity: Thallium and compounds highly toxic. LD₅₀ for humans estimated at 10-15 mg/kg.

Exposure Routes: Inhalation, ingestion, skin absorption. Soluble compounds readily absorbed through skin.

Acute Poisoning Symptoms: Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea (may be bloody), followed by neurological symptoms.

Neurological Effects: Painful peripheral neuropathy ("burning feet" syndrome), muscle weakness, tremors, psychosis, coma.

Characteristic Symptoms: Hair loss (alopecia) 2-3 weeks after exposure, Mee's lines on nails.

Delayed Effects: Symptoms may not appear for 12-24 hours after acute exposure.

Treatment: Prussian blue (ferric hexacyanoferrate) is antidote; binds thallium in gut. Potassium chloride administration may help excretion.

Protective Equipment: Use in fume hood. Wear gloves, lab coat, eye protection. Consider respiratory protection for powders.

Storage: Store in secure, labeled containers. Keep away from acids and food.

Disposal: Thallium waste must be disposed as hazardous waste according to regulations.

First Aid: For inhalation, move to fresh air. For skin contact, wash with soap and water. For eye contact, flush with water for 15 minutes. For ingestion, do NOT induce vomiting. Seek immediate medical attention for all exposures.
