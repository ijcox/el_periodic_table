Overview

Nihonium is a synthetic chemical element and a member of the transactinide series. It is a highly radioactive element that does not occur naturally and can only be produced in particle accelerators. Nihonium is the first element in group 13 and is expected to have properties similar to thallium, directly above it in the periodic table, though relativistic effects may cause significant deviations.

Nihonium was first synthesized in 2003-2004 by a joint Russian-American team at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR) in Dubna, Russia, and independently in 2004 by a Japanese team at RIKEN. After years of priority dispute, the Japanese team was credited with discovery. The element was named "nihonium" after "Nihon," one of the two ways to say "Japan" in Japanese. This made nihonium the first element discovered in an Asian country and the first named after a country in Asia.

Nihonium's physical properties are difficult to predict due to strong relativistic effects that are expected to dominate its behavior. Unlike lighter group 13 elements (boron, aluminum, gallium, indium, thallium), nihonium may show significant deviations from expected trends due to contraction of the 7s orbital and expansion of the 7p orbital.

Chemical Behavior Predicted to resemble thallium but with stronger relativistic effects

Nihonium's chemistry is predicted to be dominated by relativistic effects and the "inert pair effect," which is particularly strong in heavy group 13 elements. Unlike boron and aluminum which favor the +3 oxidation state, nihonium is expected to favor the +1 oxidation state, similar to thallium. The +3 state may be unstable or nonexistent for nihonium. Nihonium is predicted to be more metallic than thallium and may form stable monovalent compounds like NhOH (nihonium hydroxide) and NhCl (nihonium chloride). The Nh⁺ ion is expected to be more stable than Tl⁺ due to relativistic stabilization of the 7s² electron pair.

Isotopes

Nihonium has no stable isotopes. All isotopes are radioactive and synthetic:

  • Nihonium-278 (²⁷⁸Nh) - Half-life: 0.34 milliseconds (alpha decay)
  • Nihonium-282 (²⁸²Nh) - Half-life: 0.073 seconds (alpha decay)
  • Nihonium-283 (²⁸³Nh) - Half-life: 0.10 seconds (alpha decay)
  • Nihonium-284 (²⁸⁴Nh) - Half-life: 0.48 seconds (alpha decay)
  • Nihonium-285 (²⁸⁵Nh) - Half-life: 2.1 seconds (alpha decay)
  • Nihonium-286 (²⁸⁶Nh) - Half-life: 9.5 seconds (alpha decay) - Most stable known isotope
  • Nihonium-287 (²⁸⁷Nh) - Half-life: 5.5 seconds? (alpha decay, tentative)
  • Nihonium-290 (²⁹⁰Nh) - Half-life: 2.0 seconds? (alpha decay, tentative)

Nihonium-286 is the most stable known isotope with a half-life of approximately 9.5 seconds. The relatively short half-lives of nihonium isotopes have prevented detailed chemical studies so far, though theoretical predictions are extensive. Some calculations suggest that more neutron-rich isotopes with longer half-lives may exist near the "island of stability."

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Nihonium does not occur naturally. It is entirely synthetic.

Artificial Production Methods:

  1. Hot Fusion Reactions (Dubna method):
    ²⁴³Am + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁸⁸Nh + 3n (original Dubna synthesis, 2003)
    ²⁴³Am + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁸⁷Nh + 4n
  2. Cold Fusion Reactions (RIKEN method):
    ²⁰⁹Bi + ⁷⁰Zn → ²⁷⁸Nh + n (original RIKEN synthesis, 2004)
    ²⁰⁹Bi + ⁷⁰Zn → ²⁷⁹Nh (no neutrons)
  3. Other Reactions:
    ²⁴⁸Cm + ⁵¹V → ²⁹⁶Nh + 3n (theoretical)
    ²⁴⁹Bk + ⁵⁰Ti → ²⁹⁶Nh + 3n (theoretical)
  4. Separation - Nihonium atoms separated using gas-filled recoil separators. The GARIS (Gas-filled Recoil Ion Separator) at RIKEN was crucial for the Japanese discovery.
  5. Detection - Individual atoms detected by their characteristic decay patterns using silicon detectors, often requiring correlation with daughter decays through alpha decay chains to roentgenium and meitnerium.
  6. Quantity - Production rates extremely low (a few atoms per year of beam time).

Production Sites: RIKEN (Japan - primary site), Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (Dubna, Russia), Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (USA). Total world production is only about 10-20 atoms.

Discovery and History

The discovery of nihonium marked a significant achievement for Asian science:

Early Claims (2003): A joint Russian-American team at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR) in Dubna, Russia, claimed discovery of element 113 in August 2003. They bombarded americium-243 with calcium-48 ions and reported detecting atoms that decayed through a chain to dubnium.

Japanese Discovery (2004): A team at RIKEN in Japan, led by Kōsuke Morita, independently synthesized element 113 in July 2004. They used a different approach, bombarding bismuth-209 with zinc-70 ions. The RIKEN team produced only a few atoms but provided strong evidence through decay chains.

Priority Dispute: For nearly a decade, both teams claimed discovery. The Dubna team had produced more atoms earlier, but the RIKEN team provided clearer decay chains. The IUPAC/IUPAP Joint Working Party evaluated the claims.

Official Recognition (2015): In December 2015, IUPAC announced that credit for discovery would be awarded to the RIKEN team, recognizing their more definitive evidence. This made element 113 the first element discovered in Asia.

Naming (2016): The RIKEN team proposed the name "nihonium" with symbol Nh, from "Nihon," meaning Japan. The name was approved by IUPAC in November 2016. This was particularly significant as it recognized Japan's growing role in fundamental scientific research.

Significance: Nihonium's discovery represented a breakthrough for Japanese science and demonstrated that superheavy element research was no longer dominated by just a few Western and Russian laboratories. It also completed the naming of all elements up to 118 at that time.

Safety Information

Radioactivity: All isotopes of nihonium are highly radioactive. Primary hazard is alpha radiation.

Production: Produced only in specialized nuclear facilities with heavy ion accelerators. Not available outside research laboratories.

Quantity: Only atom quantities produced; insufficient for bulk handling or conventional chemical study.

Alpha Radiation: Most isotopes decay by alpha emission; external hazard minimal but internal hazard serious if ingested or inhaled.

Spontaneous Fission: Some isotopes may undergo spontaneous fission producing neutrons and fission fragments.

Daughter Products: Decay chains include other radioactive elements; secondary radiation hazards.

Handling: Atom-at-a-time techniques; remote handling essential. The short half-lives require extremely fast experimental setups.

Containment: Produced and studied in sealed systems; strict containment protocols to prevent release.

Regulations: Subject to nuclear materials regulations despite minute quantities.

Waste: Radioactive waste from production experiments managed as nuclear waste.

First Aid: Not applicable due to production scale; standard radiation emergency procedures if contamination suspected.

Protective Equipment: Standard radiation laboratory protections.

Storage: Not stored; atoms exist momentarily then decay.

Environmental: No environmental release; all material contained and decays quickly.
