Overview

Flerovium is a synthetic chemical element and a member of the transactinide series. It is a highly radioactive element that does not occur naturally and can only be produced in particle accelerators. Flerovium is predicted to be a gas or volatile liquid at room temperature, which is highly unusual for a group 14 element (carbon, silicon, germanium, tin, and lead are all solids). This predicted behavior results from extreme relativistic effects that dominate the chemistry of superheavy elements.

Flerovium was first synthesized in 1998 by a joint Russian-American team at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR) in Dubna, Russia. The element was named in honor of Georgy Flerov, the Russian physicist who founded the JINR laboratory and made significant contributions to nuclear physics. The name also recognizes the Flerov Laboratory of Nuclear Reactions at JINR, where many superheavy elements have been synthesized.

Flerovium's predicted physical state as a gas or volatile liquid at room temperature makes it unique among group 14 elements. This results from relativistic effects that increase the energy of the 7p electrons and weaken metallic bonding. If confirmed, flerovium would be the first element in group 14 to be gaseous at room temperature. Some calculations even suggest it could be a noble gas-like element.

Chemical Behavior Predicted to be more noble than lead, possibly behaving like a noble gas

Flerovium's chemistry is predicted to be dominated by extreme relativistic effects. Unlike lead which exhibits +2 and +4 oxidation states, flerovium may favor the +2 oxidation state over +4, and may even be stable in the 0 oxidation state. The "inert pair effect" is expected to be extremely strong for flerovium, making the +2 state more stable than +4. Some calculations suggest flerovium could be chemically inert - possibly behaving more like a noble gas than a metal. It may form weak van der Waals bonds but is unlikely to form strong chemical bonds under normal conditions. Flerovium is predicted to be more volatile and chemically inert than its lighter congeners.

Isotopes

Flerovium has no stable isotopes. All isotopes are radioactive and synthetic:

  • Flerovium-284 (²⁸⁴Fl) - Half-life: 2.5 milliseconds (spontaneous fission)
  • Flerovium-285 (²⁸⁵Fl) - Half-life: 0.10 seconds (alpha decay)
  • Flerovium-286 (²⁸⁶Fl) - Half-life: 0.16 seconds (spontaneous fission)

  • Flerovium-288 (²⁸⁸Fl) - Half-life: 0.66 seconds (alpha decay)
  • Flerovium-289 (²⁸⁹Fl) - Half-life: 2.1 seconds (alpha decay) - Most stable known isotope
  • Flerovium-290 (²⁹⁰Fl) - Half-life: 19 seconds? (alpha decay, tentative)
  • Flerovium-291 (²⁹¹Fl) - Half-life: 1.1 minutes? (alpha decay, tentative)
  • Flerovium-292 (²⁹²Fl) - Half-life: 1.1 minutes? (alpha decay, tentative)
  • Flerovium-293 (²⁹³Fl) - Half-life: 5? seconds (tentative)

Flerovium-289 is the most stable known isotope with a half-life of approximately 2.1 seconds. There are unconfirmed reports of flerovium-290 and flerovium-291 with half-lives of 19 seconds and 1.1 minutes respectively, which if verified, would be relatively long-lived for such heavy elements and might indicate proximity to the "island of stability."

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Flerovium does not occur naturally. It is entirely synthetic.

Artificial Production Methods:

  1. Hot Fusion Reactions:
    ²⁴⁴Pu + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁸⁹Fl + 3n (original JINR discovery reaction, December 1998)
    ²⁴⁴Pu + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁹⁰Fl + 2n
  2. Other Reactions:
    ²⁴²Pu + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁸⁷Fl + 3n
    ²⁴⁵Cm + ⁴⁸Ca → ²⁹⁰Fl + 3n
  3. Cold Fusion Reactions (alternative approach):
    ²⁰⁸Pb + ⁷⁶Ge → ²⁸³Fl + n (theoretical, not yet achieved)
    ²⁰⁹Bi + ⁷³Ga → ²⁸¹Fl + n (theoretical)
  4. Separation - Flerovium atoms separated using gas-filled recoil separators. The Dubna Gas-Filled Recoil Separator (DGFRS) was used for its discovery and study.
  5. Detection - Individual atoms detected by their characteristic decay patterns using silicon detectors, often requiring correlation with daughter decays through alpha decay chains to copernicium and darmstadtium.
  6. Quantity - Production rates extremely low (a few atoms per year of beam time).

Production Sites: Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR, Dubna, Russia - primary site), Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (USA), RIKEN (Japan). Total world production is only about 80 atoms, making it one of the more "prolific" superheavy elements.

Discovery and History

The discovery of flerovium continued the successful superheavy element research at JINR Dubna:

Discovery (1998): In December 1998, a joint Russian-American team at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR) in Dubna, Russia, synthesized flerovium by bombarding plutonium-244 with calcium-48 ions. They produced a single atom of flerovium-289, which decayed by alpha emission with a half-life of about 30 seconds. The discovery was announced in January 1999.

Confirmation and Further Studies: The discovery was confirmed in 2004 and 2006 at JINR with the production of more atoms, including the isotopes flerovium-288 and flerovium-289. The longer half-life of some isotopes enabled initial chemical studies.

Chemical Studies (2012): In 2012, a team at JINR and Paul Scherrer Institute conducted chemical experiments on flerovium, studying its adsorption on gold surfaces. The results suggested flerovium is more volatile than lead and may indeed be gaseous at room temperature, confirming theoretical predictions.

Naming: The JINR team initially proposed the name "flerovium" in honor of Georgy Flerov, founder of the JINR laboratory. After evaluation by IUPAC, the name was officially adopted in 2012.

Official Recognition (2012): On May 30, 2012, IUPAC officially announced the name "flerovium" with symbol Fl. The name recognizes both Georgy Flerov and the Flerov Laboratory of Nuclear Reactions at JINR.

Significance: Flerovium's predicted and experimentally suggested volatility represents another dramatic manifestation of relativistic effects in chemistry. It shows how these effects become increasingly important for heavier elements and can fundamentally change expected chemical behavior based on periodic table position.

Theoretical Interest: Flerovium has attracted considerable theoretical interest due to predictions that it might be chemically inert like a noble gas, despite being in group 14. This challenges traditional understanding of chemical periodicity.

Safety Information

Radioactivity: All isotopes of flerovium are highly radioactive. Primary hazards are alpha radiation and spontaneous fission (which produces neutrons and fission fragments).

Production: Produced only in specialized nuclear facilities with heavy ion accelerators. Not available outside research laboratories.

Quantity: Only atom quantities produced; insufficient for bulk handling or conventional chemical study.

Alpha Radiation: Most isotopes decay by alpha emission; external hazard minimal but internal hazard serious if ingested or inhaled.

Spontaneous Fission: Some isotopes undergo spontaneous fission producing neutrons and fission fragments requiring neutron shielding.

Daughter Products: Decay chains include other radioactive elements; secondary radiation hazards.

Volatility: Predicted to be gaseous or highly volatile; if produced in quantity, could pose inhalation hazard and be difficult to contain.

Handling: Atom-at-a-time techniques; remote handling essential. Gas-phase studies require specialized containment.

Containment: Produced and studied in sealed systems; strict containment protocols to prevent release, especially important for volatile elements.

Regulations: Subject to nuclear materials regulations despite minute quantities.

Waste: Radioactive waste from production experiments managed as nuclear waste.

First Aid: Not applicable due to production scale; standard radiation emergency procedures if contamination suspected.

Protective Equipment: Standard radiation laboratory protections. Additional respiratory protection would be needed if significant quantities of gaseous flerovium were produced.

Storage: Not stored; atoms exist momentarily then decay.

Environmental: No environmental release; all material contained and decays quickly.
