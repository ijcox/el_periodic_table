Overview

Bismuth is a brittle metal with a white, silver-pink hue, often occurring in its native form, with an oxide tarnish showing many iridescent colors ranging from yellow to blue. It is the most naturally diamagnetic element and has one of the lowest values of thermal conductivity among metals. Bismuth has long been considered the element with the highest atomic mass that is stable, but in 2003 it was discovered to be very weakly radioactive with an extremely long half-life.

The name bismuth dates from around the 1660s and is of uncertain etymology, possibly from the German "weisse Masse" or "Wismuth" ("white mass"), referring to its appearance. Bismuth was confused in early times with tin and lead because of its resemblance to those elements. It was officially identified as a separate metal in 1753 by French chemist Claude François Geoffroy.

Isotopes

Bismuth has only one naturally occurring isotope:

  • Bismuth-209 (²⁰⁹Bi) - 100% natural abundance, weakly radioactive

Bismuth-209 was long thought to be stable but was discovered in 2003 to decay via alpha emission:

  • Bismuth-209 (²⁰⁹Bi) - Half-life of 2.01×10¹⁹ years (about 20 billion billion years), making it effectively stable for most purposes

Bismuth has several artificial radioactive isotopes:

  • Bismuth-210 (²¹⁰Bi) - Half-life of 5.012 days
  • Bismuth-212 (²¹²Bi) - Half-life of 60.55 minutes
  • Bismuth-213 (²¹³Bi) - Half-life of 45.59 minutes, used in cancer therapy
  • Bismuth-214 (²¹⁴Bi) - Half-life of 19.9 minutes

Bismuth-213 is used in targeted alpha therapy for cancer treatment, particularly for leukemia.

Common Uses

  • Pharmaceuticals - Bismuth subsalicylate (Pepto-Bismol) for digestive issues, bismuth compounds in other medicines
  • Cosmetics - Bismuth oxychloride in pearlescent pigments for cosmetics, nail polishes, and paints
  • Low-Melting Alloys - Wood's metal, Rose's metal, and other fusible alloys for fire sprinklers, fuses, and molds
  • Lead Replacement - Bismuth alloys as non-toxic replacement for lead in fishing weights, shot, and other applications
  • Catalysts - Bismuth compounds as catalysts in chemical production, especially for acrylic fibers
  • Pigments - Bismuth vanadate for bright yellow pigments in paints and plastics
  • Electronics - Bismuth telluride in thermoelectric devices for cooling and power generation
  • Superconductors - Bismuth strontium calcium copper oxide high-temperature superconductors
  • Nuclear Medicine - Bismuth-213 for targeted alpha therapy cancer treatment
  • Collecting/Crystals - Synthetic bismuth crystals prized for their iridescent oxide colors and geometric hopper crystal form

Biological Role

Bismuth has no known essential biological role but has important medicinal applications:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • Medicinal Use - Bismuth compounds used safely for centuries to treat digestive disorders
  • Pepto-Bismol - Bismuth subsalicylate treats diarrhea, heartburn, nausea, indigestion
  • Helicobacter pylori - Bismuth compounds part of quadruple therapy for H. pylori eradication (stomach ulcers)
  • Low Toxicity - Bismuth has unusually low toxicity for a heavy metal; poorly absorbed from GI tract
  • Encephalopathy Risk - Very high doses or prolonged use can cause bismuth encephalopathy (reversible)
  • Antimicrobial Properties - Bismuth compounds have antibacterial activity against various pathogens
  • Cancer Treatment - Radioactive bismuth-213 used in targeted alpha therapy for cancer
  • Environmental Safety - Considered environmentally benign compared to other heavy metals

Bismuth is unique among heavy metals in having very low toxicity, making it suitable for medicinal use.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Bismuth is a rare element (0.009 ppm in Earth's crust):

  • Native Bismuth - Occurs naturally as elemental bismuth
  • Bismuthinite (Bi₂S₃) - Primary bismuth ore mineral
  • Bismite (Bi₂O₃) - Bismuth oxide mineral
  • Byproduct - Mostly obtained as byproduct of lead, copper, tin, silver, and gold refining
  • Major Producers - China, Vietnam, Mexico, Bolivia, Canada

Industrial Production: Bismuth extraction:

  1. As Byproduct - Recovered from lead and copper smelting residues
  2. Pyrometallurgical Processing - Bismuth separated by various roasting and reduction processes
  3. Electrolytic Refining - Bismuth recovered during electrolytic refining of other metals
  4. Purification - Further refined by zone refining or other methods for high purity
  5. Direct Processing - Some bismuth ores processed directly by smelting with carbon

Global Production: About 16,000 tonnes annually. China produces about 80% of world supply. Bismuth is one of the few elements where production exceeds consumption due to byproduct recovery.

Environmental Impact

Bismuth has minimal environmental impact compared to other heavy metals:

  • Low Toxicity - Considered environmentally benign; not classified as hazardous
  • Byproduct Recovery - Production as byproduct utilizes waste from other metal refining
  • Lead Replacement - Bismuth alloys replace toxic lead in many applications (fishing weights, shot, solder)
  • Mining Impact - Minimal dedicated bismuth mining; mostly from existing operations
  • Biodegradability - Bismuth compounds generally not persistent in environment
  • Water Quality - Not regulated in drinking water due to low toxicity
  • Recycling - Bismuth recycling limited but growing, especially from fusible alloys
  • Regulatory Status - Not subject to strict environmental regulations like lead or mercury
  • Green Alternative - Increasing use as "green" alternative to more toxic metals

Bismuth's environmental profile is exceptionally favorable for a heavy metal, making it attractive as a replacement for more toxic metals.

Discovery and History

Bismuth has been known since ancient times but was often confused with lead and tin. The Incas used bismuth along with copper and tin to create a bronze alloy for knives. In early times, bismuth was confused with these metals due to its similar appearance and properties.

Georgius Agricola (1494-1555) described bismuth in his works on minerals and recognized it as a separate metal. The name "bismuth" dates from around 1660s and is possibly derived from the German words "weisse Masse" or "Wismuth," meaning "white mass." Claude François Geoffroy demonstrated in 1753 that bismuth was distinct from lead.

In the 19th century, bismuth found use in fusible alloys and medicinal preparations. Its low toxicity compared to other heavy metals was recognized early. In the 20th century, bismuth's unique properties led to applications in cosmetics, electronics, and as a lead replacement. The discovery in 2003 that bismuth-209 is actually radioactive (with an extremely long half-life) was surprising, as it had been considered the heaviest stable element.

Interesting Facts

  • Bismuth is the most naturally diamagnetic element (repelled by magnetic fields)
  • It expands when it solidifies (like water), unlike most metals which contract
  • Synthetic bismuth crystals have spectacular iridescent colors from oxide film
  • Bismuth was long considered the heaviest stable element until 2003
  • Pepto-Bismol contains bismuth subsalicylate and turns stools black
  • Bismuth has unusually low toxicity for a heavy metal
  • Bismuth crystals form unique "hopper" shapes with stairstep patterns
  • It has the highest Hall coefficient of any metal (strong response to magnetic fields)

Safety Information

Low Toxicity: Bismuth has unusually low toxicity for a heavy metal. Not considered hazardous.

Medicinal Use: Bismuth compounds used safely in medicine (Pepto-Bismol). Can cause temporary blackening of stools and tongue.

Encephalopathy Risk: Very high doses or prolonged use of bismuth compounds can cause reversible encephalopathy (brain dysfunction).

Dust Inhalation: Bismuth powder may cause respiratory irritation. Use dust control measures.

Skin and Eye Contact: Bismuth metal generally not irritating, but compounds may cause irritation.

Alloys: Some bismuth alloys contain other metals (cadmium, lead, etc.) that may be toxic.

Radioactive Isotopes: Bismuth-213 and other radioactive isotopes require radiation safety precautions.

Protective Equipment: For powder handling, use dust mask, gloves, and eye protection. General handling requires minimal protection.

Storage: Store in cool, dry place. No special precautions needed for metallic bismuth.

Disposal: Bismuth and compounds can generally be disposed as non-hazardous waste, but check local regulations.

First Aid: For inhalation, move to fresh air. For skin contact, wash with soap and water. For eye contact, flush with water for 15 minutes. For ingestion of large amounts, seek medical attention.
