Overview

Tin is a silvery-white, malleable, ductile post-transition metal that is highly crystalline. It has two main allotropes: β-tin (white tin), the metallic form stable at room temperature, and α-tin (gray tin), a semiconductor form stable below 13.2°C (55.8°F). Tin is resistant to corrosion from water but can be attacked by acids and alkalis. The metal has been known since ancient times and was crucial to the development of bronze, an alloy of copper and tin that gave its name to the Bronze Age.

Tin was one of the first metals used by humans, with evidence of tin use dating back to 3000 BC. The earliest bronzes contained arsenic rather than tin, but by 2000 BC, tin bronze was being produced in the Middle East. The name "tin" comes from the Anglo-Saxon word "tin," while the chemical symbol "Sn" comes from the Latin "stannum," originally meaning an alloy of silver and lead, and later meaning tin. Tin mining was an important industry in ancient Cornwall (England), which was a major source for the Mediterranean world.

Tin-117m is used in palliative care for bone cancer pain, and tin-113 is used in some industrial applications.

Common Uses

  • Solder - Tin-lead and lead-free tin alloys for joining electronic components and plumbing
  • Tinplate - Thin steel sheets coated with tin for food cans and containers
  • Bronze - Copper-tin alloy for statues, bearings, musical instruments, and marine hardware
  • Pewter - Tin alloy with antimony and copper for decorative items, tableware, and jewelry
  • Float Glass - Molten tin bath for producing flat glass (Pilkington process)
  • Organotin Compounds - Tributyltin (TBT) as antifouling paint for ships (now restricted), and other organotins as PVC stabilizers and catalysts
  • Superconducting Wire - Niobium-tin alloy for superconducting magnets in MRI machines and particle accelerators
  • Tin Chemicals - Stannous chloride as reducing agent and tin oxide in ceramics and glass
  • Babbitt Metal - Tin-based bearing alloy for engines and machinery
  • Coinage - Tin in some coin alloys (e.g., US pennies before 1982, UK bronze coins)

Biological Role

Tin has no known essential biological role in humans but has some interactions:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes in humans
  • Low Toxicity - Elemental tin and inorganic tin compounds have low toxicity; poorly absorbed from gastrointestinal tract
  • Organotin Toxicity - Organotin compounds (especially trialkyltins) highly toxic; affect nervous system, immune system, and endocrine function
  • Dietary Sources - Canned foods (from tinplate), seafood, meat, grains; typical daily intake 1-40 mg, mostly from canned foods
  • Tin Can Corrosion - Acidic foods can dissolve tin from cans, increasing dietary intake; usually not harmful
  • Environmental Accumulation - Organotin compounds (especially TBT) accumulate in marine organisms; cause imposex in snails
  • Medical Applications - Tin-117m for palliative treatment of bone cancer pain; some tin compounds investigated for anticancer properties
  • Tin Deficiency in Animals - Some evidence tin may be essential for rats at trace levels; not established for humans
  • Occupational Exposure - Tin mining and processing can cause "stannosis" - benign pneumoconiosis from tin oxide dust inhalation

While elemental tin has low toxicity, organotin compounds pose significant environmental and health concerns.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Tin is relatively rare in Earth's crust (2.2 ppm). It occurs in:

  • Cassiterite - SnO₂ (tin dioxide, the principal commercial source)
  • Stannite - Cu₂FeSnS₄ (copper iron tin sulfide)
  • Alluvial Deposits - Cassiterite concentrated in placer deposits from weathering of primary deposits
  • Tin Belt - Southeast Asia (Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, China) has major deposits; also Bolivia, Peru, Brazil
  • In Seawater - 0.0001 mg/L concentration

Industrial Production: Tin is extracted from cassiterite:

  1. Mining - Open-pit or underground mining of hard rock deposits, or dredging of alluvial deposits
  2. Concentration - Ore crushed and concentrated by gravity methods (jigs, shaking tables, spirals)
  3. Roasting - Concentrate roasted to remove sulfur and arsenic impurities
  4. Smelting - Cassiterite reduced with carbon in reverberatory furnace or electric furnace:
    • SnO₂ + 2C → Sn + 2CO
  5. Refining - Crude tin refined by:
    • Liquation - Heating to separate higher melting point impurities
    • Poling - Stirring with green wood to reduce oxide impurities
    • Electrolysis - For high-purity tin (99.99%+)
  6. Secondary Production - Significant amounts recovered from scrap (solder, bronze, tinplate)

Global Production: About 300,000 tons annually. China produces about 35%, followed by Indonesia, Peru, and Bolivia.

Environmental Impact

Tin mining and use have several environmental considerations:

  • Alluvial Mining - Dredging and gravel pumping can severely damage river ecosystems
  • Organotin Pollution - Tributyltin (TBT) from antifouling paints highly toxic to marine life; causes imposex in snails; now banned by International Maritime Organization
  • Mining Waste - Tin mining produces large amounts of tailings that can contaminate water
  • Deforestation - Alluvial mining in tropical regions causes deforestation
  • Electronic Waste - Tin from solder in electronics contributes to e-waste; recycling important
  • Supply Chain Issues - "Conflict minerals" concerns in some tin-producing regions
  • Food Can Recycling
  • Atmospheric Emissions - Smelting releases particulates and sulfur dioxide if not controlled
  • Green Technology - Lead-free solder reduces environmental impact of electronics

Tin's environmental impact is significant, particularly from mining and organotin pollution, though lead-free solder represents an environmental improvement.

Discovery and History

Tin has been known since prehistoric times. The earliest bronze artifacts date to around 3000 BC in the Middle East, though these were arsenic bronzes. Tin bronze appeared around 2000 BC and revolutionized tool and weapon making, giving its name to the Bronze Age (c. 3300-1200 BC).

The Phoenicians traded tin from Cornwall (England) to the Mediterranean world as early as 1000 BC. Cornwall remained a major tin source for millennia. The Romans called Britain "Cassiterides" (Tin Islands) due to its tin resources.

The chemical symbol "Sn" comes from the Latin "stannum." The English word "tin" has Germanic origins. Tin was recognized as an element in the modern sense in the late 18th century, though it had been used for thousands of years.

In the 19th century, tinplate (tin-coated steel) revolutionized food preservation with the invention of the can. In the 20th century, tin became crucial for electronics through soldering. Today, despite competition from other materials, tin remains important in many technologies.

Interesting Facts

  • Tin has more stable isotopes (10) than any other element
  • The "tin cry" is the sound tin makes when bent - a crackling sound from crystal twins breaking
  • Tin can transform from metallic form to powder ("tin pest") in cold temperatures
  • The chemical symbol "Sn" comes from the Latin "stannum"
  • Napoleon's buttons may have disintegrated in the Russian winter due to tin pest
  • Tin is essential for bronze - the alloy that gave its name to the Bronze Age
  • Most flat glass (for windows, mirrors) is made by floating molten glass on a bed of molten tin

Safety Information

Elemental Tin: Low toxicity; not well absorbed from gastrointestinal tract. Inhalation of dust may cause benign pneumoconiosis (stannosis).

Organotin Compounds: Highly toxic, especially trialkyltins. Affect nervous system (headache, dizziness, vision problems), immune system, and are endocrine disruptors.

Tin Hydride (Stannane): Toxic gas similar to arsine; causes hemolytic anemia.

Inhalation: Tin oxide dust causes stannosis - benign pneumoconiosis without significant lung function impairment. Metal fume fever from inhalation of fumes.

First Aid: For inhalation, move to fresh air. For skin contact, wash with soap and water. For eye contact, flush with water for 15 minutes.

Protective Equipment: Use gloves, safety glasses, and dust masks when handling tin compounds. Use fume hood for organotin compounds.

Storage: Store organotin compounds as hazardous materials. Keep away from heat and moisture.

Environmental: Organotin compounds, especially TBT, highly toxic to aquatic life; now banned in marine antifouling paints. Proper disposal required.

Radioactive Materials: Tin-117m requires radiation safety precautions for medical use.

Food Contact: Tin from cans generally safe; acidic foods can dissolve more tin. Discard cans with visible corrosion.
