Overview

Tellurium is a brittle, silver-white metalloid that looks similar to tin. It is chemically related to selenium and sulfur. Tellurium is occasionally found in its native form as elemental crystals. The element is a semiconductor and its conductivity increases slightly when exposed to light. Tellurium burns in air with a greenish-blue flame and forms tellurium dioxide. The element and its compounds are considered toxic and should be handled with care.

Tellurium was discovered in 1782 by Franz-Joseph Müller von Reichenstein, a Romanian mining official, in gold ores from Transylvania. He initially thought it was antimony or bismuth. In 1798, Martin Heinrich Klaproth confirmed it was a new element and named it "tellurium" from the Latin word "tellus" meaning "earth." The name was chosen to match selenium, which had been named after the moon ("selene"), as tellurium's properties were between those of metals and nonmetals, just as Earth is between the moon and sun.

  • Tellurium-122 (¹²²Te) - 2.55% abundance

  • Tellurium-125 (¹²⁵Te) - 7.07% abundance

  • Tellurium-128 (¹²⁸Te) - 31.74% abundance
  • Tellurium-130 (¹³⁰Te) - 34.08% abundance

Tellurium has several radioactive isotopes, some with extremely long half-lives:

  • Tellurium-128 (¹²⁸Te) - Actually radioactive with half-life of 2.2×10²⁴ years (effectively stable)
  • Tellurium-130 (¹³⁰Te) - Actually radioactive with half-life of 7.9×10²⁰ years (effectively stable)
  • Tellurium-121m (¹²¹ᵐTe) - Metastable isomer with half-life of 154 days
  • Tellurium-127m (¹²⁷ᵐTe) - Metastable isomer with half-life of 109 days
  • Tellurium-129m (¹²⁹ᵐTe) - Metastable isomer with half-life of 33.6 days

Tellurium-121m and tellurium-127m are used in some industrial and research applications.

Common Uses

  • Solar Cells - Cadmium telluride (CdTe) thin-film photovoltaic cells for solar energy generation
  • Thermoelectric Devices - Bismuth telluride (Bi₂Te₃) for thermoelectric generators and Peltier coolers
  • Alloying Agent - Tellurium added to steel and copper to improve machinability (free-machining steels, tellurium copper)
  • Rubber Vulcanization - Tellurium compounds as secondary accelerators in rubber production
  • Optical Storage Media - Tellurium alloys in rewritable CDs and DVDs (phase-change materials)
  • Semiconductors - Tellurium as component in compound semiconductors (cadmium telluride, mercury cadmium telluride for infrared detectors)
  • Catalysts - Tellurium compounds as catalysts in petroleum refining and chemical synthesis
  • Pigments - Cadmium telluride for red and yellow pigments (declining due to toxicity concerns)
  • Glass and Ceramics - Tellurium dioxide in special glasses with high refractive index
  • Research - Tellurium in phase-change memory devices and other advanced materials research

Biological Role

Tellurium has no known essential biological role and is generally toxic:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • Toxicity - Tellurium and its compounds toxic; cause garlic breath odor (tellurium breath) due to dimethyl telluride formation
  • Garlic Breath - Even small amounts of tellurium cause persistent garlic odor on breath and sweat (tellurium breath); not harmful but socially unpleasant
  • Neurotoxicity - Tellurium compounds, especially tellurium tetrachloride, can cause neurological damage
  • Antimicrobial Properties - Some tellurium compounds show antibacterial activity; investigated for medical applications
  • Environmental Exposure - Trace amounts in human body from environmental sources; most exposure occupational
  • Tellurium Resistance - Some bacteria have tellurium resistance mechanisms; can reduce tellurium compounds to elemental tellurium
  • Research - Tellurium compounds studied for potential anticancer and antimicrobial applications
  • Dietary Sources - Extremely low levels in foods; not a significant source of exposure

Tellurium's most notable biological effect is causing garlic breath, even at very low exposure levels.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Tellurium is one of the rarest stable solid elements in Earth's crust (0.001 ppm). It occurs:

  • As Trace Element - In gold, silver, copper, and lead ores (primary commercial sources)
  • Native Tellurium - Rarely as elemental tellurium
  • Telluride Minerals - Calaverite (AuTe₂), sylvanite ((Au,Ag)₂Te₄), tellurite (TeO₂)
  • As Byproduct - Over 90% of tellurium produced as byproduct of copper refining
  • In Seawater - Extremely low concentration

Industrial Production: Tellurium is recovered during copper refining:

  1. Copper Electrorefining - Tellurium accumulates in anode slimes during electrolytic copper refining
  2. Treatment of Slimes - Anode slimes processed to recover precious metals and tellurium
  3. Roasting - Slimes roasted with sodium carbonate to convert tellurium to sodium tellurite
  4. Leaching - Roasted material leached with water; tellurium dissolves as tellurite
  5. Precipitation - Tellurium precipitated from solution by neutralization or reduction
  6. Reduction - Tellurium dioxide or tellurite reduced with sulfur dioxide or other reducing agents
  7. Purification - Crude tellurium purified by distillation, zone refining, or other methods

Global Production: About 500 tons annually. China, Japan, Russia, and United States are major producers.

Environmental Impact

Tellurium production and use have several environmental considerations:

  • Byproduct Recovery - Recovery from copper refining minimizes additional mining impacts
  • Solar Cell Production - Cadmium telluride solar cells contain toxic cadmium; require proper end-of-life management
  • Mining Waste - Tellurium in mining waste from copper and gold operations
  • Toxicity - Tellurium compounds toxic to aquatic life; environmental regulations apply
  • Electronic Waste - Tellurium in electronics and solar panels requires recycling
  • Energy Production vs. Toxicity - Cadmium telluride solar cells produce clean energy but contain toxic materials
  • Recycling - Limited recycling from end-of-life products; mostly from manufacturing scrap
  • Supply Constraints - Tellurium availability limited by copper production; potential bottleneck for CdTe solar cells
  • Green Technology - Tellurium enables renewable energy (solar cells) and energy efficiency (thermoelectrics)

Tellurium presents environmental trade-offs: toxic in some forms but enables clean energy technologies.

Discovery and History

In 1782, Franz-Joseph Müller von Reichenstein, the chief inspector of mines in Transylvania (now part of Romania), was examining a peculiar gold ore from the Fața Băii mine in Zlatna. The ore had a metallic luster but was too brittle to be gold.

Müller spent three years studying the mineral, conducting numerous tests. He initially thought it was antimony or bismuth, but its properties didn't match exactly. He eventually concluded it was a new element but didn't publish his findings widely.

In 1798, the sample came to the attention of Martin Heinrich Klaproth, a prominent German chemist. Klaproth confirmed it was a new element and named it "tellurium" from the Latin "tellus" meaning "earth." The name was chosen to create symmetry with selenium (named after the moon, "selene") that had been discovered two years earlier.

Tellurium remained a laboratory curiosity for over a century. Its commercial importance grew in the 20th century with applications in metallurgy, and more recently in electronics and solar energy.

Interesting Facts

  • Tellurium causes "garlic breath" - even tiny amounts make your breath smell like garlic for months
  • The name comes from the Latin "tellus" meaning "earth" - to match selenium (moon) discovered two years earlier
  • Tellurium is one of the rarest solid elements in Earth's crust
  • Some of the most efficient solar cells are made from cadmium telluride
  • Bismuth telluride is used in portable refrigerators and CPU coolers (Peltier effect)
  • Tellurium was involved in a famous mining swindle - the "Great Diamond Hoax" of 1872
  • The element is more abundant in the universe than on Earth - it's depleted in Earth's crust

Safety Information

Toxicity: Tellurium and its compounds toxic. Cause garlic breath odor (tellurium breath) due to dimethyl telluride formation. Higher exposures cause more serious effects.

Garlic Breath: Even minute exposures (microgram amounts) cause persistent garlic odor on breath and sweat; can last weeks to months. Socially embarrassing but not medically serious at low levels.

Inhalation: Tellurium fumes and dust cause garlic breath, metallic taste, dry mouth, headache, drowsiness. Higher exposures cause respiratory irritation. OSHA PEL: 0.1 mg/m³.

Skin Contact: Tellurium compounds can be absorbed through skin; may cause irritation.

Tellurium Dioxide: Relatively low toxicity but can cause garlic breath.

Tellurium Hexafluoride (TeF₆): Highly toxic; hydrolyzes to hydrofluoric acid and tellurous acid.

First Aid: For inhalation, move to fresh air. For skin contact, wash with soap and water. For eye contact, flush with water for 15 minutes. Seek medical attention for significant exposures.

Protective Equipment: Use gloves, safety glasses, and appropriate respiratory protection when handling tellurium compounds. Use local exhaust ventilation.

Storage: Store in cool, dry, well-ventilated area away from moisture and strong oxidizers.

Environmental: Tellurium compounds toxic to aquatic life. Proper disposal required. Cadmium telluride solar panels require special recycling.
