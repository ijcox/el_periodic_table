Overview

Polonium is a rare and highly radioactive metalloid with no stable isotopes. It is chemically similar to selenium and tellurium, but its metallic character resembles that of its horizontal neighbors in the periodic table: thallium, lead, and bismuth. Polonium is found in uranium ores as a decay product, but only in trace amounts. The element was discovered in 1898 by Marie Curie and Pierre Curie.

Polonium is named after Marie Curie's homeland of Poland. It was the first element discovered by the Curies while they were investigating the cause of radioactivity in pitchblende. Polonium is notoriously toxic due to its intense radioactivity and has gained infamy for its use in several high-profile poisoning cases.

Isotopes

Polonium has no stable isotopes. All isotopes are radioactive:

  • Polonium-208 (²⁰⁸Po) - Half-life of 2.898 years, alpha emitter
  • Polonium-209 (²⁰⁹Po) - Half-life of 125.2 years, alpha emitter, longest-lived isotope
  • Polonium-210 (²¹⁰Po) - Half-life of 138.376 days, alpha emitter, most commonly produced
  • Polonium-211 (²¹¹Po) - Half-life of 0.516 seconds, alpha emitter
  • Polonium-212 (²¹²Po) - Half-life of 0.299 microseconds, alpha emitter
  • Polonium-214 (²¹⁴Po) - Half-life of 164.3 microseconds, alpha emitter
  • Polonium-215 (²¹⁵Po) - Half-life of 1.781 milliseconds, alpha emitter
  • Polonium-216 (²¹⁶Po) - Half-life of 0.145 seconds, alpha emitter
  • Polonium-218 (²¹⁸Po) - Half-life of 3.098 minutes, alpha and beta emitter

Polonium-210 is the most widely available isotope and has been used in various applications. It is produced by neutron bombardment of bismuth-209 in nuclear reactors.

Common Uses

  • Static Eliminators - Polonium-210 used in brushes to remove static electricity in paper mills, textile plants, and photographic film
  • Nuclear Batteries - Radioisotope thermoelectric generators for spacecraft (e.g., Lunokhod rovers)
  • Neutron Sources - When mixed with beryllium, polonium-210 creates portable neutron sources
  • Industrial Instruments - Thickness gauges, moisture gauges, and other measuring devices
  • Research - As a source of alpha particles in physics research
  • Historical Use - Early atomic batteries, trigger for nuclear weapons (polonium-beryllium initiators)
  • Smoke Detectors - Early ionization smoke detectors used polonium-210 (largely replaced by americium-241)
  • Heat Sources - In space probes and satellites as heat source for thermoelectric generators

Due to security concerns and the development of alternative technologies, many applications of polonium have been phased out.

Biological Role

Polonium has no biological role and is extremely hazardous due to its intense radioactivity:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • Extreme Radiotoxicity - Milligram quantities of polonium-210 are lethal
  • Alpha Radiation - Polonium-210 decays by alpha emission; alpha particles cause intense localized damage
  • Internal Hazard - Particularly dangerous if ingested, inhaled, or enters bloodstream
  • Biological Half-life - About 50 days in humans; concentrates in soft tissues
  • Famous Poisoning Cases - Used in assassination of Alexander Litvinenko (2006) and possibly Yasser Arafat
  • Tobacco Contamination - Polonium-210 found in tobacco smoke from phosphate fertilizers
  • Environmental Presence - Trace amounts in environment from natural uranium decay and atmospheric nuclear testing
  • No Antidote - No effective treatment for polonium poisoning once incorporated

Polonium-210 is considered one of the most toxic substances known. A dose of just 50 nanograms (inhaled) or 500 nanograms (ingested) is lethal to humans.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Polonium occurs naturally in trace amounts:

  • Uranium Ores - As decay product in uranium-238 decay chain (radium-226 → radon-222 → polonium-218, etc.)
  • Atmosphere - From decay of radon-222 in air
  • Tobacco - Polonium-210 accumulates in tobacco plants from phosphate fertilizers
  • Human Body - Trace amounts from natural radon decay
  • Extremely Rare - About 100 micrograms per ton of uranium ore

Industrial Production: Polonium-210 is produced artificially:

  1. Neutron Irradiation - Bismuth-209 metal irradiated with neutrons in nuclear reactor
  2. Nuclear Reaction:
    • ²⁰⁹Bi + n → ²¹⁰Bi (beta decay, half-life 5 days)
    • ²¹⁰Bi → ²¹⁰Po + e⁻ (beta decay)
  3. Separation - Polonium separated from bismuth by various methods (sublimation, electrochemical, solvent extraction)
  4. Purification - Further purification for specific applications

Global Production: Very limited production (grams per year). Russia is the main producer. Production tightly controlled due to nuclear non-proliferation concerns.

Environmental Impact

Polonium's environmental impact is primarily through its radioactivity:

  • Natural Background - Trace polonium always present from uranium decay chains
  • Tobacco Smoke - Polonium-210 in tobacco smoke contributes to lung cancer risk
  • Nuclear Fallout - Atmospheric nuclear testing released polonium-210
  • Industrial Releases - Potential releases from facilities using polonium (strictly regulated)
  • Waste Management - Polonium waste requires special handling as radioactive waste
  • Accidents - Potential for contamination in accidents involving polonium sources
  • Regulations - Strictly controlled under nuclear materials regulations
  • Cleanup Challenges - Decontamination difficult due to alpha emission; specialized procedures required
  • Long-term Storage - Short half-life (138 days) means waste decays relatively quickly

While natural polonium poses minimal environmental risk, industrial or weapons-related releases can create serious contamination issues.

Discovery and History

Polonium was discovered in 1898 by Marie Curie and her husband Pierre Curie while they were investigating the radioactivity of pitchblende, a uranium ore. They noticed that the ore was more radioactive than the uranium extracted from it, suggesting the presence of additional radioactive elements.

After processing tons of pitchblende, the Curies isolated two new elements: polonium and radium. They named polonium after Marie's homeland of Poland, which at the time was partitioned among three empires and not an independent country. This naming was politically significant, highlighting Marie's Polish identity and her hope for Polish independence.

Polonium was the first element discovered by the Curies and helped establish the new science of radioactivity. Marie Curie's initial separation yielded only trace amounts, and she never isolated a pure sample. It wasn't until 1934 that the first macroscopic sample (0.1 mg) was isolated by Marie Curie's daughter Irène Joliot-Curie and her husband Frédéric Joliot-Curie.

Safety Information

Extreme Radiotoxicity: Polonium-210 one of most toxic substances known. Milligram quantities lethal.

Alpha Radiation: Decays by alpha emission. Alpha particles cause intense localized damage if internalized.

Inhalation Hazard: Particularly dangerous if inhaled; deposits in lungs.

Ingestion Hazard: Extremely hazardous if ingested; enters bloodstream and distributes throughout body.

External Hazard: Alpha particles stopped by skin; external exposure less hazardous but still dangerous.

Lethal Dose: LD₅₀ (inhaled): ~10 ng; LD₅₀ (ingested): ~50 ng. Smaller than visible speck of dust.

Symptoms of Poisoning: Nausea, vomiting, hair loss, bone marrow failure, multiple organ failure.

No Effective Treatment: Once incorporated, no effective treatment. Chelation therapy ineffective.

Protective Equipment: Glove boxes, remote handling, full protective suits for significant quantities. Strict contamination control.

Storage: Store in double-contained sealed sources. Specialized radioactive materials facilities.

Disposal: High-level radioactive waste. Special disposal facilities required.

First Aid: In case of suspected exposure: immediate decontamination, medical evaluation. Notify radiation safety officer immediately.

Regulations: Strictly controlled under nuclear materials regulations. Special licenses required for possession.
