Overview

Rhodium is a rare, silvery-white, hard, corrosion-resistant transition metal and a member of the platinum group metals (PGMs). It is one of the most valuable precious metals, often more expensive than gold or platinum. Rhodium has high reflectance and is highly resistant to corrosion and oxidation. The metal is inert to most acids, including aqua regia, though it dissolves in hot concentrated sulfuric acid.

Rhodium was discovered in 1803 by English chemist William Hyde Wollaston, shortly after his discovery of palladium. He isolated it from crude platinum ore from South America. Wollaston named the element "rhodium" from the Greek word "rhodon" meaning "rose," because of the rose-red color of some of its compounds. He dissolved platinum ore in aqua regia, neutralized the acid with sodium hydroxide, and precipitated platinum with ammonium chloride. From the remaining solution, he isolated rhodium by reduction with zinc and treatment with nitric acid.

Isotopes

Rhodium has one stable isotope:

  • Rhodium-103 (¹⁰³Rh) - The only stable isotope, making up 100% of natural rhodium

Rhodium has several radioactive isotopes, with rhodium-102 being the most stable radioactive isotope:

  • Rhodium-102 (¹⁰²Rh) - Radioactive isotope with half-life of 207 days
  • Rhodium-101 (¹⁰¹Rh) - Radioactive isotope with half-life of 3.3 years
  • Rhodium-99 (⁹⁹Rh) - Radioactive isotope with half-life of 16.1 days
  • Rhodium-105 (¹⁰⁵Rh) - Radioactive isotope with half-life of 35.36 hours
  • Rhodium-106 (¹⁰⁶Rh) - Radioactive isotope with half-life of 29.80 seconds

Rhodium-102 is used in some industrial applications and research, but rhodium isotopes are less commonly used than other platinum group metal isotopes.

Common Uses

  • Automotive Catalytic Converters - Rhodium as catalyst in three-way catalytic converters to reduce nitrogen oxide emissions (NOx reduction)
  • Jewelry - Rhodium plating for white gold and silver jewelry to enhance brightness, durability, and prevent tarnishing
  • Electrical Contacts - Rhodium in electrical contacts for its low electrical resistance, hardness, and corrosion resistance
  • Optical Instruments - Rhodium coatings for mirrors, searchlights, and other optical devices due to high reflectivity
  • Thermocouples - Rhodium-platinum thermocouples for high-temperature measurements (up to 1800°C)
  • Chemical Catalysts - Rhodium compounds as catalysts for hydrogenation, hydroformylation, and other industrial chemical processes
  • Fountain Pen Nibs - Rhodium plating on fountain pen nibs for durability and smooth writing
  • Neutron Flux Detectors - Rhodium in self-powered neutron detectors in nuclear reactors
  • Laboratory Equipment - Rhodium crucibles and other labware for high-temperature applications
  • Investment - Rhodium bars and coins as precious metal investment

Biological Role

Rhodium has no known essential biological role but has some medical and research applications:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • Low Toxicity - Elemental rhodium and most compounds have low toxicity; considered biologically inert
  • Medical Research - Rhodium complexes investigated as potential anticancer agents, similar to platinum drugs but with different mechanisms
  • Diagnostic Agents - Some rhodium compounds studied as contrast agents for medical imaging
  • Environmental Exposure - Trace amounts in human body from environmental sources; no known health effects
  • Allergic Reactions - Rhodium can cause allergic contact dermatitis in sensitive individuals, especially from jewelry
  • Biocompatibility - Rhodium's corrosion resistance and inertness make it suitable for some medical implants
  • Research Tools - Rhodium compounds used as catalysts in biochemical research and as probes in biological systems

Rhodium's biological significance is minimal, with its value primarily in industrial applications rather than biological systems.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Rhodium is one of the rarest elements in Earth's crust (0.0002 ppm). It occurs:

  • In Platinum Ores - As native alloy with other platinum group metals in alluvial deposits
  • In Pentlandite - (Fe,Ni)₉S₈ (nickel sulfide ore, contains trace amounts)
  • In Copper-Nickel Sulfide Ores - Associated with Sudbury Basin (Canada) and Norilsk (Russia) deposits
  • As Byproduct - Always recovered as byproduct of platinum, nickel, and copper mining
  • In Meteorites - Higher concentrations in some iron meteorites

Industrial Production: Rhodium is recovered from platinum group metal concentrates:

  1. Mining - Platinum group metals mined from sulfide ores or alluvial deposits
  2. Concentration - Ore processed to produce copper-nickel matte containing platinum group metals
  3. Separation - Matte treated to separate copper, nickel, and precious metals
  4. Dissolution - Precious metal concentrate dissolved in aqua regia; rhodium partially dissolves
  5. Precipitation - Rhodium precipitated as sodium hexachlororhodate(III) or other insoluble compounds
  6. Reduction - Rhodium compound reduced with hydrogen to produce rhodium powder
  7. Purification - Chemical methods or zone refining produces high-purity rhodium

Global Production: About 30 tons annually. South Africa produces about 80%, followed by Russia.

Environmental Impact

Rhodium mining and use have several environmental considerations:

  • Mining Impact - Platinum group metal mining produces large volumes of waste rock and tailings
  • Automotive Emissions Reduction - Rhodium in catalytic converters significantly reduces harmful vehicle emissions (NOx, CO, hydrocarbons)
  • Chemical Processing - Use of aqua regia and other aggressive chemicals in refining
  • Resource Intensity - Extremely low natural abundance means mining large volumes for small amounts of rhodium
  • Recycling - Rhodium recycled from spent catalytic converters (about 30% of supply); recycling economically important due to high value
  • Price Volatility - Rhodium prices extremely volatile (reached over $29,000/oz in 2021), affecting recycling economics
  • Supply Concentration - Production heavily concentrated in South Africa creates geopolitical and supply chain risks
  • Low Toxicity - Rhodium compounds generally have low environmental toxicity
  • Catalyst Deactivation - Rhodium catalysts can be poisoned by lead, sulfur, or other contaminants

Rhodium's crucial role in reducing automotive emissions provides significant environmental benefits that outweigh mining impacts.

Discovery and History

In 1803, English chemist William Hyde Wollaston was investigating crude platinum ore from South America. After discovering palladium earlier that year, he continued working with the ore residues.

Wollaston dissolved the platinum ore in aqua regia and neutralized the acid with sodium hydroxide. He then precipitated platinum with ammonium chloride. From the remaining solution, which had a rose-red color, he isolated a new metal by reduction with zinc and treatment with nitric acid.

He named the new element "rhodium" from the Greek word "rhodon" meaning "rose," referring to the rose-red color of some rhodium compounds. Wollaston was a skilled experimentalist who kept his methods secret to protect his business interests, only revealing his discoveries after establishing commercial production.

For many years, rhodium had limited applications due to its rarity and high cost. Its importance grew dramatically in the 1970s with the introduction of automotive catalytic converters, which use rhodium to reduce nitrogen oxide emissions. Today, about 80% of rhodium demand comes from catalytic converters.

Safety Information

Metal Dust and Powder: Rhodium powder is flammable and can form explosive mixtures with air.

Chemical Compounds: Most rhodium compounds have low toxicity. Soluble compounds more toxic than insoluble forms.

Rhodium Chlorides: Can cause skin and respiratory irritation. May be sensitizers causing allergic reactions.

Inhalation: Rhodium dust may cause respiratory irritation. Chronic inhalation of high concentrations may cause lung damage.

Eye and Skin Contact: Generally low hazard for metal; compounds may cause irritation. Rhodium can cause allergic contact dermatitis.

First Aid: For inhalation, move to fresh air. For skin contact, wash with soap and water. For eye contact, flush with water for 15 minutes.

Protective Equipment: Use gloves, safety glasses, and dust masks when handling rhodium powder or compounds. Use local exhaust ventilation.

Fire Fighting: For rhodium fires, use Class D extinguishers (metal fire extinguishers). Do NOT use water. Use dry sand, dry salt, or special metal fire extinguishing powder.

Storage: Store rhodium powder in airtight containers away from oxidizers and moisture.

Environmental: Rhodium compounds have low environmental toxicity but should still be disposed of properly. Recycling preferred due to high value and rarity.
