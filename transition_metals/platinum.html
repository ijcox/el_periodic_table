Overview

Platinum is a dense, malleable, ductile, highly unreactive, precious, silverish-white transition metal. It is a member of the platinum group metals (PGMs) and is known for its exceptional corrosion resistance, even at high temperatures. Platinum occurs naturally in the alluvial sands of various rivers and is one of the rarest elements in Earth's crust.

The metal was used by pre-Columbian Americans, but the first European reference to platinum appears in 1557 in the writings of Italian humanist Julius Caesar Scaliger. Spanish explorers named it "platina" (little silver) when they first encountered it in Colombia. Platinum's true value was recognized in the 18th century, and it became highly prized for jewelry and industrial applications.

Isotopes

Platinum has six naturally occurring isotopes:

  • Platinum-190 (¹⁹⁰Pt) - 0.012% abundance, radioactive with very long half-life
  • Platinum-192 (¹⁹²Pt) - 0.782% abundance
  • Platinum-194 (¹⁹⁴Pt) - 32.864% abundance
  • Platinum-195 (¹⁹⁵Pt) - 33.775% abundance
  • Platinum-196 (¹⁹⁶Pt) - 25.211% abundance
  • Platinum-198 (¹⁹⁸Pt) - 7.356% abundance

Platinum has several artificial radioactive isotopes:

  • Platinum-188 (¹⁸⁸Pt) - Half-life of 10.2 days
  • Platinum-191 (¹⁹¹Pt) - Half-life of 2.8 days
  • Platinum-193 (¹⁹³Pt) - Half-life of 50 years
  • Platinum-197 (¹⁹⁷Pt) - Half-life of 20 hours

Platinum-190 is naturally occurring radioactive isotope with a half-life of 6.5×10¹¹ years, making it essentially stable for most purposes.

Common Uses

  • Catalytic Converters - Primary use (about 50% of demand); reduces harmful emissions from vehicles
  • Jewelry - Precious metal for high-end jewelry, often alloyed with other metals
  • Investment - Bullion bars and coins for investment purposes
  • Chemical Industry - Catalysts for nitric acid production, silicon production, and petroleum refining
  • Electrical Applications - Electrical contacts, electrodes, spark plugs
  • Medical Applications - Chemotherapy drugs (cisplatin, carboplatin), dental alloys, medical implants
  • Laboratory Equipment - Crucibles, forceps, loops, and other equipment for high-temperature work
  • Glass Manufacturing - Production equipment for high-quality optical glass and fiberglass
  • Fuel Cells - Platinum catalysts in proton exchange membrane fuel cells
  • Thermocouples - Platinum-rhodium alloys for high-temperature measurement
  • Standards - International prototype kilogram (90% platinum, 10% iridium)

Biological Role

Platinum has no known essential biological role but has important medical applications:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • Chemotherapy Drugs - Platinum compounds (cisplatin, carboplatin, oxaliplatin) widely used in cancer treatment
  • Mechanism of Action - Cisplatin crosslinks DNA, preventing cell division and causing apoptosis
  • Medical Implants - Used in pacemakers, stents, and other medical devices due to biocompatibility
  • Dental Alloys - Platinum alloys used in dental crowns and bridges
  • Allergy Potential - Platinum sensitivity possible but rare compared to other metals
  • Research - New platinum complexes investigated for anticancer and antimicrobial properties
  • Environmental Exposure - Trace amounts from catalytic converters; generally considered safe at environmental levels
  • Toxicity - Elemental platinum relatively inert; some platinum compounds toxic

Platinum's most significant biological interaction is its medical use in chemotherapy, where it saves thousands of lives annually.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Platinum is a rare element in Earth's crust (0.005 ppm). It occurs:

  • Native Platinum - Usually alloyed with other platinum group metals
  • Placer Deposits - Alluvial deposits where platinum accumulates due to density
  • Primary Ores - Associated with nickel and copper sulfide ores
  • Meteorites - Higher concentration than in Earth's crust
  • Main Sources - South Africa (80% of world reserves), Russia, Zimbabwe, Canada, United States

Industrial Production: Platinum extraction is complex:

  1. Mining - Open-pit or underground mining of platinum-bearing ores
  2. Crushing and Milling - Ore crushed and ground to fine powder
  3. Froth Flotation - Concentrates platinum-bearing minerals
  4. Smelting - Produces matte containing platinum group metals
  5. Converting
  6. Leaching - Base metals dissolved, leaving platinum group metals
  7. Separation
  8. Refining - Complex chemical processes to separate individual metals
  9. Final Purification

Global Production: About 180-200 tonnes annually. South Africa produces about 70% of world supply.

Environmental Impact

Platinum mining and use have significant environmental considerations:

  • Mining Impact - Open-pit mining causes habitat destruction, soil erosion, water pollution
  • Energy Intensive - Platinum refining requires significant energy input
  • Water Usage - Mining operations require large amounts of water
  • Emission Control - Platinum in catalytic converters reduces vehicle emissions (positive impact)
  • Platinum Nanoparticles
  • Recycling - High value drives efficient recycling from spent catalytic converters and jewelry
  • Tailings - Mining waste may contain residual platinum and other metals
  • Carbon Footprint - Significant greenhouse gas emissions from mining and refining
  • Social Impact

Platinum presents environmental trade-offs: mining causes damage but enables clean air technology through catalytic converters.

Discovery and History

Platinum was used by pre-Columbian Americans in Ecuador and Colombia, who produced artifacts of a white gold-platinum alloy. The first European reference to platinum appears in 1557 in the writings of Italian humanist Julius Caesar Scaliger, who described a mysterious metal from Spanish Central America that could not be melted.

Spanish explorers named the metal "platina" (little silver) when they encountered it in Colombia. They initially considered it an impurity in gold and sometimes threw it back into rivers. In the 18th century, platinum was recognized as a distinct element. Antonio de Ulloa published observations of platina del pinto (platinum of the Pinto River) in 1748.

In 1750, British metallurgist William Brownrigg produced pure platinum samples. The metal's true value was recognized, and techniques for working platinum were developed. By the early 19th century, platinum was being used for laboratory equipment and jewelry. The development of catalytic converters in the 1970s dramatically increased platinum demand.

Safety Information

Elemental Platinum: Generally considered biologically inert and non-toxic. Fine dust may cause irritation.

Platinum Salts: Some platinum compounds can be toxic or cause allergic reactions.

Platinum Allergy: Platinum salts can cause platinum sensitivity, with symptoms similar to other metal allergies.

Industrial Exposure: Workers in platinum refining may develop "platinosis" - allergic reactions to platinum salts.

Chemotherapy Drugs: Platinum-based chemotherapy drugs are cytotoxic and must be handled by trained medical professionals.

Dust Hazards: Platinum powder may be flammable. Avoid dust formation and ignition sources.

First Aid: For inhalation of dust, move to fresh air. For skin contact, wash with soap and water. For eye contact, flush with water for 15 minutes.

Protective Equipment: Use appropriate respiratory protection when handling fine powder. Wear gloves when handling platinum compounds.

Storage: Store in cool, dry place away from strong acids and oxidizers.

Environmental: Platinum mining and refining can have significant environmental impacts. Proper waste management essential.
