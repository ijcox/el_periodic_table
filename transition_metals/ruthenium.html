Overview

Ruthenium is a rare, hard, silvery-white transition metal belonging to the platinum group metals (PGMs). It is inert to most other chemicals and does not tarnish in air at room temperature. Ruthenium is found in platinum ores and is obtained as a byproduct of nickel and copper mining. The metal has the ability to harden platinum and palladium alloys and exhibits a wide range of oxidation states in its compounds.

Ruthenium was discovered in 1844 by Russian chemist Karl Ernst Claus at Kazan University. He named it after "Ruthenia," the Latin word for Rus', an historical region that included present-day western Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, and parts of Slovakia and Poland. Claus isolated ruthenium from platinum ore residues that had already yielded other platinum group metals. Earlier, in 1827, Swedish chemist Jöns Jacob Berzelius and German chemist Gottfried Osann had examined platinum residues and suspected the presence of new elements, but Claus was the first to isolate ruthenium definitively.

Isotopes

Ruthenium has seven stable isotopes:

  • Ruthenium-96 (⁹⁶Ru) - 5.54% abundance
  • Ruthenium-98 (⁹⁸Ru) - 1.87% abundance
  • Ruthenium-99 (⁹⁹Ru) - 12.76% abundance
  • Ruthenium-100 (¹⁰⁰Ru) - 12.60% abundance
  • Ruthenium-101 (¹⁰¹Ru) - 17.06% abundance
  • Ruthenium-102 (¹⁰²Ru) - 31.55% abundance
  • Ruthenium-104 (¹⁰⁴Ru) - 18.62% abundance

Ruthenium has several radioactive isotopes with various applications:

  • Ruthenium-106 (¹⁰⁶Ru) - Radioactive isotope with half-life of 373.59 days; beta emitter used in cancer therapy (brachytherapy for eye tumors)
  • Ruthenium-103 (¹⁰³Ru) - Radioactive isotope with half-life of 39.26 days
  • Ruthenium-97 (⁹⁷Ru) - Radioactive isotope with half-life of 2.9 days
  • Ruthenium-105 (¹⁰⁵Ru) - Radioactive isotope with half-life of 4.44 hours

Ruthenium-106 is particularly important for medical applications and is also a fission product from nuclear reactors.

Common Uses

  • Electronics - Ruthenium dioxide in thick-film chip resistors; ruthenium in hard disk drives as magnetic layers
  • Catalysts - Ruthenium compounds as catalysts for ammonia synthesis, hydrogenation reactions, and in fuel cells
  • Jewelry - Ruthenium plating for white gold jewelry to enhance durability and whiteness
  • Pen Nibs - Ruthenium alloys for fountain pen nib tips (ruthenium points)
  • Solar Cells - Ruthenium-based dyes in dye-sensitized solar cells (Grätzel cells)
  • Medical Applications - Ruthenium-106 for brachytherapy of eye tumors; ruthenium complexes investigated as anticancer drugs
  • Electrical Contacts - Ruthenium in electrical contacts for its wear resistance and low contact resistance
  • Superalloys - Ruthenium in nickel-based superalloys for jet engine turbine blades
  • Electroplating - Ruthenium plating for wear resistance and decorative finishes
  • Research - Ruthenium red as biological stain for electron microscopy; ruthenium complexes in photochemistry research

Biological Role

Ruthenium has no known essential biological role but has important medical applications:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • Medical Radionuclide - Ruthenium-106 used in brachytherapy for treating ocular tumors (retinoblastoma, melanoma)
  • Anticancer Research - Ruthenium complexes (e.g., NAMI-A, KP1019) investigated as anticancer drugs with different mechanisms than platinum drugs
  • Ruthenium Red - Dye used in electron microscopy to stain acidic polysaccharides; inhibitor of calcium channels
  • Low Toxicity - Elemental ruthenium and most compounds have low toxicity
  • Bioinorganic Chemistry - Ruthenium complexes used to study electron transfer in biological systems
  • Photosensitizers - Ruthenium polypyridyl complexes investigated for photodynamic therapy of cancer
  • Environmental Exposure - Trace amounts in human body from environmental sources; no known health effects
  • Research Tools - Ruthenium compounds used as probes in cell biology and biochemistry

Ruthenium's medical significance lies in its radioactive isotopes for cancer treatment and its complexes as potential next-generation anticancer drugs.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Ruthenium is one of the rarest elements in Earth's crust (0.001 ppm). It occurs:

  • In Platinum Ores - As native alloy with other platinum group metals in alluvial deposits
  • In Pentlandite - (Fe,Ni)₉S₈ (nickel sulfide ore, contains trace amounts)
  • In Pyroxenite Deposits - Associated with nickel-copper sulfide ores
  • As Byproduct - Always recovered as byproduct of nickel, copper, and platinum mining
  • In Ocean Nodules - Manganese nodules on ocean floor contain trace amounts

Industrial Production: Ruthenium is recovered from platinum group metal concentrates:

  1. Mining - Platinum group metals mined from sulfide ores or alluvial deposits
  2. Concentration - Ore processed to produce platinum group metal concentrate
  3. Aqua Regia Treatment - Concentrate dissolved in aqua regia; ruthenium and osmium not dissolved, remain as residues
  4. Oxidation - Residues fused with sodium peroxide or treated with chlorine to form volatile ruthenium tetroxide (RuO₄)
  5. Distillation - Ruthenium tetroxide distilled and collected in hydrochloric acid solution
  6. Reduction - Ruthenium precipitated as ammonium hexachlororuthenate and reduced with hydrogen to produce metal powder
  7. Purification - Chemical vapor transport or zone refining produces high-purity ruthenium

Global Production: About 30 tons annually. South Africa produces about 85%, followed by Russia and Zimbabwe.

Environmental Impact

Ruthenium mining and production have environmental impacts typical of platinum group metals:

  • Mining Impact - Platinum group metal mining produces large amounts of waste rock and tailings
  • Chemical Processing - Use of aqua regia, chlorine, and other aggressive chemicals
  • Sulfur Emissions - Processing sulfide ores can release sulfur dioxide unless controlled
  • Water Use - Mining and processing require substantial water resources
  • Radioactive Isotopes - Ruthenium-106 from nuclear accidents (Chernobyl) and medical use creates radioactive waste
  • Low Natural Abundance - Mining large volumes of ore for small amounts of ruthenium
  • Recycling - Ruthenium recycled from catalytic converters, electronics, and jewelry; recycling rates increasing
  • Toxicity - Ruthenium tetroxide highly toxic and volatile; other compounds generally low toxicity
  • Green Technology Component - Ruthenium in catalysts for cleaner chemical processes and in solar cells

Ruthenium's use in catalytic converters reduces automotive emissions, providing environmental benefits that partially offset mining impacts.

Discovery and History

In 1827, Swedish chemist Jöns Jacob Berzelius and German chemist Gottfried Osann examined residues left after dissolving crude platinum from the Ural Mountains in aqua regia. Osann believed he had found three new metals, which he named "pluranium," "polinium," and "ruthenium." However, Berzelius could not confirm these findings, and the discoveries were not accepted.

In 1844, Russian chemist Karl Ernst Claus, working at Kazan University, re-examined the platinum residues. He obtained a new metal oxide and succeeded in isolating the metal. Claus named the new element "ruthenium" after Ruthenia, the Latin name for Rus' (ancient Russia). He published detailed studies of ruthenium's properties and compounds.

For many years, ruthenium had few practical applications. In the 20th century, its uses expanded with the development of electronics, catalysis, and medical applications. Today, ruthenium is valued for its unique properties and is an important component of many advanced technologies.

Interesting Facts

  • Ruthenium is named after "Ruthenia," the Latin name for a region that included parts of modern Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus
  • Some fountain pen nibs have ruthenium alloy tips for durability
  • Ruthenium can exist in oxidation states from -4 to +8, one of the widest ranges of any element
  • The element is used in some of the most efficient dye-sensitized solar cells
  • Ruthenium red, a dye used in biology, gets its color from ruthenium
  • Ruthenium is the only platinum group metal that can form a volatile oxide (RuO₄)
  • Some ruthenium compounds are being tested as alternatives to platinum-based chemotherapy drugs

Safety Information

Ruthenium Tetroxide (RuO₄): Highly toxic, volatile liquid. Strong oxidizer that can cause severe burns. Decomposes explosively above 100°C. Handle with extreme caution.

Metal Dust and Powder: Ruthenium powder may be flammable and can form explosive mixtures with air.

Chemical Compounds: Most ruthenium compounds have low to moderate toxicity. Soluble compounds more toxic than insoluble forms.

Radioactive Ruthenium-106: Beta emitter with 374-day half-life. Used in medical applications; handle with radiation precautions.

Inhalation: Ruthenium dust may cause respiratory irritation. Ruthenium tetroxide vapors extremely hazardous.

Eye and Skin Contact: Ruthenium compounds may cause irritation. Ruthenium tetroxide causes severe burns.

First Aid: For inhalation, move to fresh air immediately. For skin contact with compounds, wash with soap and water. For ruthenium tetroxide exposure, seek immediate medical attention.

Protective Equipment: Use gloves, safety glasses, and fume hood when handling ruthenium compounds. For ruthenium tetroxide, use additional protective measures.

Storage: Store ruthenium tetroxide in cool, well-ventilated area away from reducing agents. Store metal powder in airtight containers.

Fire Fighting: For ruthenium fires, use Class D extinguishers. Do NOT use water on ruthenium tetroxide fires.
