Overview

Iridium is a very hard, brittle, silvery-white transition metal of the platinum group. It is the second-densest naturally occurring element (after osmium) and is the most corrosion-resistant metal known, even at high temperatures. Iridium is found in nature as an uncombined element or in natural alloys, especially the iridium-osmium alloys osmiridium and iridosmine.

Iridium was discovered in 1803 by English chemist Smithson Tennant in the residue left when crude platinum is dissolved in aqua regia. He named it after the Greek goddess Iris, personification of the rainbow, because of the striking and varied colors of its compounds. Iridium is one of the rarest elements in Earth's crust, with annual production and consumption of only about 3 tonnes.

Isotopes

Iridium has two stable isotopes:

  • Iridium-191 (¹⁹¹Ir) - 37.3% abundance
  • Iridium-193 (¹⁹³Ir) - 62.7% abundance

Iridium has several radioactive isotopes:

  • Iridium-192 (¹⁹²Ir) - Half-life of 73.83 days, used in industrial radiography and brachytherapy
  • Iridium-194 (¹⁹⁴Ir) - Half-life of 19.3 hours
  • Iridium-192m2 (¹⁹²ᵐ²Ir) - Metastable isomer with half-life of 241 years

Iridium-192 is an important gamma ray source in industrial radiography to check for flaws in metal components, and in brachytherapy for cancer treatment.

Common Uses

  • High-Temperature Applications - Crucibles for growing single crystals, especially oxides at high temperatures
  • Electrical Contacts - Spark plugs, electrical contacts where high durability needed
  • Pen Nibs - Iridium-tipped pen points for fountain pens (historically significant)
  • Medical Applications - Iridium-192 in brachytherapy for cancer treatment
  • Industrial Radiography - Iridium-192 as gamma ray source for non-destructive testing
  • Catalysts - Iridium compounds as catalysts in chemical industry (Cativa process for acetic acid production)
  • Standard of Length - Historical use in international prototype meter bar (10% iridium, 90% platinum)
  • Deep-Water Pipes - Iridium-coated anodes for cathodic protection
  • Fuel Cells - Iridium catalysts in proton exchange membrane fuel cells
  • Jewelry - Occasionally used in high-end jewelry, often alloyed with platinum

Biological Role

Iridium has no known biological role and is generally considered biologically inert:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • Low Bioavailability - Iridium compounds poorly absorbed by biological systems
  • Medical Use - Radioactive iridium-192 used in brachytherapy for cancer treatment
  • Low Toxicity - Elemental iridium considered non-toxic due to corrosion resistance and low solubility
  • Compound Toxicity - Some soluble iridium compounds may be toxic, but limited data available
  • Allergy Potential - Rare cases of iridium sensitivity reported in dental and medical applications
  • Research Applications - Iridium complexes studied for potential anticancer drugs and bioimaging
  • Environmental Exposure - Extremely low background levels in human body

Iridium's most significant biological interaction is its medical use in cancer treatment via iridium-192 brachytherapy.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Iridium is one of the rarest elements in Earth's crust (0.001 ppm). It occurs:

  • In Platinum Ores - Primary commercial source is as byproduct of nickel and platinum mining
  • Native Iridium - Rarely as natural alloys with osmium (osmiridium)
  • Meteoric Abundance - Higher concentration in meteorites than Earth's crust
  • Crustal Depletion - Most Earth's iridium thought to have sunk to core during planetary differentiation
  • Impact Layers - Iridium anomaly at Cretaceous-Paleogene boundary (K-Pg boundary) from asteroid impact

Industrial Production: Iridium is obtained as byproduct:

  1. Nickel and Platinum Mining - Iridium recovered from nickel and platinum ores
  2. Extraction - Iridium separated from other platinum group metals by complex chemical processes
  3. Dissolution - Treated with aqua regia or other agents
  4. Purification - Multiple precipitation and dissolution steps
  5. Reduction - Ammonium hexachloroiridate reduced with hydrogen to produce pure iridium powder
  6. Melting - Powder melted in argon atmosphere or by electron beam melting

Global Production: About 3-4 tonnes annually. South Africa, Russia, and Canada are major producers.

Environmental Impact

Iridium production and use have minimal environmental impact due to low volumes:

  • Low Production Volume - Small annual production minimizes overall environmental impact
  • Byproduct Recovery - Recovery from nickel and platinum mining utilizes existing operations
  • Chemical Processing - Extraction uses strong acids; proper waste management required
  • Radioactive Isotopes - Iridium-192 requires careful handling and disposal
  • Medical Waste - Spent iridium-192 sources from brachytherapy require radioactive waste management
  • Industrial Safety - Industrial radiography sources must be properly secured
  • Recycling - High value drives efficient recycling from industrial scrap and spent catalysts
  • Geochemical Tracer - Iridium used to study meteorite impacts and geological boundaries

Iridium's environmental impact is primarily associated with radioactive isotopes in medical and industrial applications rather than the element itself.

Discovery and History

Iridium was discovered in 1803 by English chemist Smithson Tennant in London. Tennant was examining the black residue left behind when crude platinum was dissolved in aqua regia (a mixture of nitric acid and hydrochloric acid).

French chemists Hippolyte-Victor Collet-Descotils, Antoine François Fourcroy, and Louis Nicolas Vauquelin also observed the black residue at about the same time, but Tennant is credited with the discovery. He identified two new elements in the residue: osmium and iridium.

Tennant named the element "iridium" after Iris, the Greek goddess of the rainbow, because many of its salts are intensely colored. The element's resistance to corrosion made it valuable for special applications.

The K-Pg boundary iridium anomaly, discovered in 1980 by physicist Luis Alvarez and his team, provided strong evidence for the asteroid impact theory of dinosaur extinction. This 66-million-year-old layer contains iridium concentrations much higher than normal crustal levels, suggesting extraterrestrial origin.

Interesting Facts

  • Iridium is the second densest element (22.56 g/cm³), just behind osmium
  • The name comes from the Greek goddess Iris, personification of the rainbow
  • Iridium is extremely corrosion-resistant - it's not attacked by acids, including aqua regia
  • The global indium reserve is estimated to be less than 10,000 tonnes
  • Iridium was used in the standard meter bar and kilogram prototypes
  • The Cretaceous-Paleogene extinction event that killed dinosaurs is marked by an iridium-rich layer
  • Iridium can be used to detect forgeries in art - it wasn't available before 1803
  • Some of the world's most expensive fountain pens have iridium-tipped nibs

Safety Information

Elemental Iridium: Considered relatively non-toxic due to corrosion resistance and low solubility. Fine powder may be irritation hazard.

Soluble Compounds: Some iridium compounds may be toxic if ingested or inhaled. Limited toxicity data available.

Iridium-192 (Radioactive): Significant radiation hazard. Used in industrial radiography and brachytherapy.

Industrial Radiography: Iridium-192 sources emit strong gamma radiation. Proper shielding and handling procedures essential.

Brachytherapy Sources: Medical use requires specialized training and safety protocols.

Dust and Powder: Iridium powder may be pyrophoric. Handle in inert atmosphere.

First Aid: For inhalation of dust, move to fresh air. For skin contact, wash with soap and water. For eye contact, flush with water for 15 minutes.

Protective Equipment: Use appropriate respiratory protection when handling fine powder. For radioactive iridium, follow radiation safety protocols.

Storage: Store in cool, dry place. Radioactive sources must be stored in approved shielded containers.

Disposal: Radioactive iridium requires special disposal as radioactive waste. Consult regulations.
