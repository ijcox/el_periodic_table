Overview

Palladium is a rare, lustrous, silvery-white metal belonging to the platinum group metals (PGMs). It has the lowest melting point and is the least dense of the platinum group metals. Palladium has remarkable catalytic properties, particularly for hydrogenation and dehydrogenation reactions. The metal has an extraordinary ability to absorb hydrogen, capable of absorbing up to 900 times its own volume of hydrogen gas at room temperature.

Palladium was discovered in 1803 by English chemist William Hyde Wollaston. He isolated it from crude platinum ore from South America, naming it after the asteroid Pallas, which had been discovered the previous year. The name "Pallas" itself comes from the Greek goddess Pallas Athena. Wollaston discovered palladium by dissolving platinum ore in aqua regia, neutralizing the acid with sodium hydroxide, and precipitating platinum with ammonium chloride. Palladium remained in solution and was precipitated with mercuric cyanide, then heated to yield the metal.

Isotopes

Palladium has six stable isotopes:

  • Palladium-102 (¹⁰²Pd) - 1.02% abundance
  • Palladium-104 (¹⁰⁴Pd) - 11.14% abundance
  • Palladium-105 (¹⁰⁵Pd) - 22.33% abundance
  • Palladium-106 (¹⁰⁶Pd) - 27.33% abundance
  • Palladium-108 (¹⁰⁸Pd) - 26.46% abundance
  • Palladium-110 (¹¹⁰Pd) - 11.72% abundance

Palladium has several radioactive isotopes with various applications:

  • Palladium-103 (¹⁰³Pd) - Radioactive isotope with half-life of 16.991 days; used in brachytherapy for prostate cancer
  • Palladium-107 (¹⁰⁷Pd) - Radioactive isotope with half-life of 6.5 million years; extinct radionuclide used in meteorite dating
  • Palladium-109 (¹⁰⁹Pd) - Radioactive isotope with half-life of 13.7 hours
  • Palladium-100 (¹⁰⁰Pd) - Radioactive isotope with half-life of 3.63 days

Palladium-103 is particularly important for medical applications, especially in prostate cancer treatment.

Common Uses

  • Automotive Catalytic Converters - Palladium as primary catalyst in gasoline engine catalytic converters (oxidation of CO and hydrocarbons)
  • Jewelry - Palladium in white gold alloys and pure palladium jewelry (hallmarked as 950 Pd)
  • Electronics - Palladium in multilayer ceramic capacitors (MLCCs), connector platings, and hybrid integrated circuits
  • Dentistry - Palladium alloys in dental crowns, bridges, and other restorations
  • Hydrogen Purification - Palladium membranes and alloys for hydrogen purification and separation (hydrogen diffuses through palladium)
  • Chemical Catalysts - Palladium compounds as catalysts for organic synthesis (Heck, Suzuki, Stille reactions), hydrogenation, and petroleum refining
  • Medical Applications - Palladium-103 seeds for brachytherapy of prostate cancer
  • Watchmaking - Palladium in watch cases and components
  • Fuel Cells - Palladium as catalyst in some types of fuel cells
  • Investment - Palladium bars and coins as precious metal investment

Biological Role

Palladium has no known essential biological role but has important medical applications:

  • No Essential Function - Not required for any known biological processes
  • Medical Radionuclide - Palladium-103 used in brachytherapy for prostate cancer treatment
  • Low Toxicity - Elemental palladium and most compounds have low toxicity; considered biologically inert
  • Dental Alloys - Palladium in dental alloys generally well-tolerated; rare cases of allergic reactions
  • Anticancer Research - Palladium complexes investigated as potential anticancer agents
  • Allergic Reactions - Palladium can cause allergic contact dermatitis in sensitive individuals, especially from jewelry
  • Environmental Exposure - Trace amounts in human body from environmental sources; no known health effects at normal levels
  • Biocompatibility - Palladium's corrosion resistance and inertness make it suitable for medical and dental applications

Palladium's primary biological significance is in medical applications, particularly cancer treatment with palladium-103.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Palladium is a rare element in Earth's crust (0.015 ppm). It occurs:

  • In Platinum Ores - As native alloy with other platinum group metals in alluvial deposits
  • In Copper-Nickel Sulfide Ores - Associated with Sudbury Basin (Canada) and Norilsk (Russia) deposits
  • In Pentlandite - (Fe,Ni)₉S₈ (nickel sulfide ore, contains trace amounts)
  • As Byproduct - Primarily recovered as byproduct of nickel, copper, and platinum mining
  • In Meteorites - Higher concentrations in some iron meteorites

Industrial Production: Palladium is recovered from various sources:

  1. Mining - Platinum group metals mined from sulfide ores or alluvial deposits
  2. Concentration - Ore processed to produce copper-nickel matte containing platinum group metals
  3. Separation - Matte treated to separate copper, nickel, and precious metals
  4. Dissolution - Precious metal concentrate dissolved in aqua regia; palladium dissolves
  5. Precipitation - Palladium precipitated as diamminepalladous chloride or other compounds
  6. Reduction - Palladium compound reduced with hydrogen or formic acid to produce palladium powder
  7. Purification - Chemical methods or zone refining produces high-purity palladium
  8. Recycling - Significant amounts recovered from spent catalytic converters and electronic scrap

Global Production: About 210 tons annually. Russia produces about 40%, followed by South Africa, Canada, and USA.

Environmental Impact

Palladium mining and use have several environmental considerations:

  • Automotive Emissions Reduction - Palladium in catalytic converters significantly reduces harmful vehicle emissions (CO, hydrocarbons)
  • Mining Impact - Platinum group metal mining produces large volumes of waste rock and tailings
  • Recycling - About 30% of palladium supply comes from recycling spent catalytic converters and electronics
  • Chemical Processing - Use of aqua regia and other aggressive chemicals in refining
  • Price Volatility - Palladium prices highly volatile due to supply-demand imbalances
  • Substitution Effects - High prices drive substitution with platinum in catalytic converters
  • Electronic Waste - Palladium in electronics contributes to e-waste; recycling important for resource recovery
  • Low Toxicity - Palladium compounds generally have low environmental toxicity
  • Supply Concentration - Production concentrated in Russia and South Africa creates geopolitical risks

Palladium's crucial role in reducing automotive emissions provides significant environmental benefits that outweigh mining impacts.

Discovery and History

In 1803, English chemist William Hyde Wollaston was investigating crude platinum ore from South America. After discovering rhodium earlier that year, he continued working with the ore residues.

Wollaston developed a secret method to purify platinum and discovered palladium in the process. Rather than announcing his discovery immediately, he took an unusual approach: he anonymously advertised the new metal for sale in a mineralogical catalog in 1803, calling it "new silver."

Irish chemist Richard Chenevix purchased the metal, analyzed it, and declared it to be an alloy of platinum and mercury. Wollaston then published a paper in 1805 revealing that he had discovered palladium and describing its properties. He named it after the asteroid Pallas, which had been discovered in 1802.

Palladium's importance grew slowly until the 20th century. Its catalytic properties were discovered in the early 1900s, and its use in catalytic converters began in the 1970s. Today, automotive catalysts account for about 85% of palladium demand.

Safety Information

Metal Dust and Powder: Palladium powder is flammable and can form explosive mixtures with air.

Chemical Compounds: Most palladium compounds have low to moderate toxicity. Soluble compounds more toxic than insoluble forms.

Palladium Chloride: Can cause skin and respiratory irritation. May be sensitizer causing allergic reactions.

Inhalation: Palladium dust may cause respiratory irritation. Chronic inhalation of high concentrations may cause lung damage.

Eye and Skin Contact: Generally low hazard for metal; compounds may cause irritation. Palladium can cause allergic contact dermatitis.

Radioactive Palladium-103: Used in medical applications; handle with radiation precautions.

First Aid: For inhalation, move to fresh air. For skin contact, wash with soap and water. For eye contact, flush with water for 15 minutes.

Protective Equipment: Use gloves, safety glasses, and dust masks when handling palladium powder or compounds. Use local exhaust ventilation.

Fire Fighting: For palladium fires, use Class D extinguishers (metal fire extinguishers). Do NOT use water. Use dry sand, dry salt, or special metal fire extinguishing powder.

Storage: Store palladium powder in airtight containers away from oxidizers and moisture.
