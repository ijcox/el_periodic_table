Overview

Meitnerium is a synthetic chemical element and a member of the transactinide series. It is a highly radioactive metal that does not occur naturally and can only be produced in particle accelerators. Meitnerium is expected to be a solid under normal conditions and is predicted to have properties similar to iridium, directly above it in group 9 of the periodic table, though relativistic effects may cause it to behave more like platinum or gold.

Meitnerium was first synthesized in 1982 by a German research team at the Gesellschaft für Schwerionenforschung (GSI) in Darmstadt, led by Peter Armbruster and Gottfried Münzenberg. The element was named in honor of Lise Meitner, the Austrian-Swedish physicist who contributed to the discovery of nuclear fission. This was particularly significant as Meitner, despite her crucial contributions, had been overlooked for the Nobel Prize awarded for fission discovery.

Meitnerium's chemistry is predicted to be strongly influenced by relativistic effects. Unlike its lighter congener iridium which typically exhibits +3 and +4 oxidation states, meitnerium may favor lower oxidation states like +1 or even -1. Theoretical calculations suggest the 6d orbitals are destabilized and the 7s orbital stabilized by relativistic effects, potentially making meitnerium behave more like a noble metal. Meitnerium is predicted to be less reactive than iridium and may form stable compounds in lower oxidation states that are unusual for group 9 elements.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Meitnerium does not occur naturally. It is entirely synthetic.

Artificial Production Methods:

  1. Cold Fusion Reactions:
    ²⁰⁹Bi + ⁵⁸Fe → ²⁶⁶Mt + n (original GSI discovery reaction, single atom produced)
    ²⁰⁹Bi + ⁵⁸Fe → ²⁶⁷Mt (no neutrons)
  2. Hot Fusion Reactions:
    ²³⁸U + ³⁴S → ²⁷¹Mt + n (produces more neutron-rich isotopes)
    ²⁴⁴Pu + ³⁷Cl → ²⁷⁸Mt + 3n
  3. Other Reactions:
    ²⁴⁹Bk + ³⁴S → ²⁷⁹Mt + 4n
    ²⁵⁴Es + ³⁷Cl → ²⁸⁷Mt + 4n (theoretical)
  4. Separation - Meitnerium atoms separated using recoil separation techniques. Chemical separation is challenging due to short half-lives and uncertain chemistry.
  5. Detection - Individual atoms detected by their characteristic decay patterns using silicon detectors, often requiring correlation with daughter decays through alpha decay chains.
  6. Quantity - Production rates extremely low (a few atoms per year of beam time).

Production Sites: Gesellschaft für Schwerionenforschung (GSI, Germany), Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (Dubna, Russia), RIKEN (Japan). Total world production is only about 10 atoms.

Discovery and History

The discovery of meitnerium was part of GSI Darmstadt's systematic exploration of superheavy elements:

Discovery (1982): A team at the Gesellschaft für Schwerionenforschung (GSI) in Darmstadt, Germany, led by Peter Armbruster and Gottfried Münzenberg, synthesized meitnerium by bombarding bismuth-209 with iron-58 ions. On August 29, 1982, they produced a single atom of meitnerium-266, which decayed by alpha emission with a half-life of about 1.7 milliseconds. The discovery was based on this single atom, demonstrating the sensitivity of modern nuclear detection techniques.

Confirmation: The discovery was confirmed in 1988 at GSI with the production of more atoms. The decay chain was traced to known isotopes of bohrium, dubnium, and lawrencium, providing strong evidence for the new element.

Naming: The GSI team proposed the name "meitnerium" in honor of Lise Meitner. This was particularly significant as Meitner had made crucial contributions to the discovery of nuclear fission but was overlooked for the Nobel Prize (awarded to Otto Hahn alone in 1944). The name recognized both her scientific contributions and the historical injustice.

Official Recognition (1997): After evaluation by the IUPAC/IUPAP Transfermium Working Group, the name "meitnerium" with symbol Mt was officially adopted in 1997. There was little controversy over this name compared to earlier transactinide elements.

Significance: Meitnerium's naming was seen as a correction of historical oversight, finally giving proper recognition to Lise Meitner's contributions to nuclear physics. The element stands as a permanent tribute to one of the most important women in the history of physics.

Interesting Facts

  • The element was discovered based on a single atom produced in 1982

  • Only about 10 atoms of meitnerium have ever been produced and detected worldwide

Safety Information

Radioactivity: All isotopes of meitnerium are highly radioactive. Primary hazard is alpha radiation.

Production: Produced only in specialized nuclear facilities with heavy ion accelerators. Not available outside research laboratories.

Quantity: Only atom quantities produced; insufficient for bulk handling or conventional chemical study.

Alpha Radiation: Most isotopes decay by alpha emission; external hazard minimal but internal hazard serious if ingested or inhaled.

Spontaneous Fission: Some isotopes may undergo spontaneous fission producing neutrons and fission fragments.

Daughter Products: Decay chains include other radioactive elements; secondary radiation hazards.

Handling: Atom-at-a-time techniques; remote handling essential. The short half-lives require extremely fast experimental setups.

Containment: Produced and studied in sealed systems; strict containment protocols to prevent release.

Regulations: Subject to nuclear materials regulations despite minute quantities.

Waste: Radioactive waste from production experiments managed as nuclear waste.

First Aid: Not applicable due to production scale; standard radiation emergency procedures if contamination suspected.

Protective Equipment: Standard radiation laboratory protections.

Storage: Not stored; atoms exist momentarily then decay.

Environmental: No environmental release; all material contained and decays quickly.
