Overview

Cobalt is a hard, lustrous, silver-gray metal that is ferromagnetic like iron but with higher magnetic coercivity. It is found in various ores and is primarily obtained as a byproduct of nickel and copper mining. Cobalt has been used for centuries to produce brilliant blue colors in glass and ceramics.

Cobalt compounds have been used since ancient times for blue coloring. The element was isolated in 1735 by Swedish chemist Georg Brandt, who demonstrated that the blue color in glass came from a new element, not bismuth as previously thought. The name comes from German "Kobold" meaning goblin or evil spirit, because miners found cobalt ore troublesome and sometimes toxic.

Isotopes

Cobalt has one stable isotope:

  • Cobalt-59 (⁵⁹Co) - The only stable isotope, making up 100% of natural cobalt. It has 27 protons and 32 neutrons.

Cobalt has several radioactive isotopes, with cobalt-60 being the most important:

  • Cobalt-60 (⁶⁰Co) - Radioactive isotope with half-life of 5.27 years; decays by beta emission to nickel-60; important gamma ray source for cancer therapy, sterilization, and industrial radiography
  • Cobalt-57 (⁵⁷Co) - Radioactive isotope with half-life of 271.8 days; used in medical diagnostics and as calibration source
  • Cobalt-56 (⁵⁶Co) - Short-lived isotope (77.27 day half-life)
  • Cobalt-58 (⁵⁸Co) - Short-lived isotope (70.86 day half-life)

Cobalt-60 is particularly important as it produces two high-energy gamma rays (1.17 and 1.33 MeV) making it ideal for radiation therapy and sterilization.

Common Uses

  • Rechargeable Batteries - Lithium cobalt oxide in lithium-ion batteries for smartphones, laptops, and electric vehicles
  • Superalloys - Cobalt-based superalloys for jet engines, gas turbines, and high-temperature applications
  • Magnets - Samarium-cobalt and other rare-earth magnets for motors, generators, and magnetic recording
  • Pigments - Cobalt blue (cobalt aluminate) for ceramics, glass, paints, and inks
  • Catalysts - Cobalt compounds in petroleum refining (hydrodesulfurization) and chemical synthesis
  • Medical Applications - Cobalt-60 for radiation therapy (cancer treatment) and sterilization of medical equipment
  • Hard Metals - Cobalt as binder in tungsten carbide for cutting tools, drills, and wear-resistant parts
  • Electroplating - Cobalt plating for wear resistance and appearance
  • Vitamin B12 - Cobalt is central atom in cobalamin (vitamin B12), essential for humans
  • Drying Agents - Cobalt compounds as driers for paints, varnishes, and inks

Biological Role

Cobalt is an essential trace element as a component of vitamin B12:

  • Vitamin B12 (Cobalamin) - Cobalt is the central atom in this essential vitamin; required for DNA synthesis, red blood cell formation, and nervous system function
  • Human Requirements - Adults need about 2.4 μg/day of vitamin B12 (containing about 0.1 μg cobalt); not cobalt itself but as part of B12
  • Dietary Sources - Vitamin B12 from animal products: meat, fish, eggs, dairy; plant foods contain virtually no B12
  • Deficiency - Vitamin B12 deficiency causes pernicious anemia, neurological problems, and increased homocysteine (cardiovascular risk)
  • Microbial Synthesis - Only certain bacteria and archaea can synthesize vitamin B12; animals obtain it from diet or gut bacteria
  • Enzyme Cofactor - Vitamin B12 is cofactor for methionine synthase (homocysteine metabolism) and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase (fatty acid metabolism)
  • Ruminant Nutrition - Cobalt essential for rumen bacteria to synthesize vitamin B12 for the host animal
  • Toxicity - Inorganic cobalt compounds are toxic; can cause cardiomyopathy ("beer drinker's cardiomyopathy" from cobalt additive in beer)
  • Medical Uses - Radioactive cobalt-60 used in cancer therapy; cobalt-chromium alloys for medical implants

Cobalt's biological importance is almost entirely through its role in vitamin B12, one of the most complex vitamins.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Cobalt is the 32nd most abundant element in Earth's crust (25 ppm). It rarely occurs as native metal. Major sources include:

  • Cobaltite - CoAsS, cobalt arsenic sulfide
  • Erythrite - Co₃(AsO₄)₂·8H₂O, "cobalt bloom" (pink secondary mineral)
  • Skutterudite - CoAs₃, important cobalt ore
  • Carrollite - Cu(Co,Ni)₂S₄
  • As Byproduct - About 98% of cobalt is byproduct of nickel and copper mining
  • Seafloor Deposits - Cobalt-rich crusts on seamounts and manganese nodules on abyssal plains

Industrial Production: Cobalt is produced through complex extraction:

  1. Flotation to concentrate cobalt minerals
  2. Roasting to convert sulfides to oxides
  3. Leaching with acid or ammonia
  4. Purification by solvent extraction or ion exchange
  5. Electrowinning or precipitation to produce cobalt metal or salts

Global Production: About 140,000 tons annually. Democratic Republic of Congo produces about 70%, followed by Russia, Australia, and Canada.

Environmental Impact

Cobalt mining and production have significant environmental and social impacts:

  • Artisanal Mining Issues - Small-scale mining in DRC involves dangerous conditions, child labor, and environmental damage
  • Water Pollution - Acid mine drainage from sulfide ores releases cobalt and other metals into water systems
  • Toxic Waste - Processing generates arsenic-containing waste (cobalt ores often contain arsenic)
  • Energy Intensive - Production requires significant energy, especially for electrowinning
  • Battery Revolution - Cobalt essential for electric vehicle batteries, enabling transition from fossil fuels but with mining impacts
  • Recycling - Cobalt recycling from batteries and superalloys is increasing but still limited
  • Supply Chain Ethics - Concerns about "conflict minerals" and human rights in cobalt supply chains
  • Alternative Technologies - Research into cobalt-free batteries (LFP - lithium iron phosphate) to reduce dependency
  • Deep Sea Mining - Potential mining of cobalt-rich crusts raises concerns about deep sea ecosystem disruption

Balancing cobalt's essential role in clean energy technologies with responsible sourcing and environmental protection is a major challenge.

Discovery and History

Cobalt compounds have been used since ancient times. Egyptian and Mesopotamian civilizations used cobalt blue in glass and pottery as early as 2000 BCE. Chinese Tang Dynasty (618-907 CE) and Ming Dynasty (1368-1644 CE) porcelain used cobalt blue extensively.

In medieval Europe, cobalt was used in glassmaking but its source was mysterious. German miners called it "kobold" (goblin) because it resembled valuable copper ores but yielded no copper and released toxic arsenic fumes when smelted.

In 1735, Swedish chemist Georg Brandt isolated cobalt metal and demonstrated it was the source of blue color in glass, not bismuth as previously thought. He is credited with discovering cobalt as an element.

In the 20th century, cobalt's importance grew with the development of superalloys for jet engines (1930s), cobalt-60 for cancer therapy (1950s), and lithium cobalt oxide batteries (1980s). Today, cobalt is crucial for both aerospace and clean energy technologies.

Interesting Facts

  • Cobalt-60 is used to sterilize about 40% of all single-use medical devices worldwide
  • The famous blue color in Chinese Ming Dynasty porcelain comes from cobalt imported from Persia
  • Cobalt is ferromagnetic with a Curie temperature of 1121°C - the highest of any metal except gadolinium
  • Vitamin B12 was the first vitamin discovered to contain a metal atom (cobalt)
  • Some cobalt compounds change color with humidity - used in humidity indicators for packaging
  • The Democratic Republic of Congo has about 50% of the world's cobalt reserves

Safety Information

Cobalt Dust and Fumes: Inhalation of cobalt dust or fumes causes respiratory irritation, asthma, and hard metal lung disease (cobalt lung).

Cardiotoxicity: High cobalt exposure can cause cardiomyopathy, as seen in "beer drinker's cardiomyopathy" when cobalt was used as beer foam stabilizer.

Dermatitis: Cobalt is common cause of allergic contact dermatitis, especially from jewelry, tools, and cement.

Cobalt-60 Radiation: Radioactive cobalt-60 is powerful gamma emitter requiring strict radiation safety protocols. Used in cancer therapy but dangerous if mishandled.

Arsenic Contamination: Many cobalt ores contain arsenic; processing releases toxic arsenic compounds requiring careful control.

Occupational Exposure: Workers in hard metal production, diamond polishing, and cobalt processing are at highest risk. OSHA PEL: 0.1 mg/m³ for cobalt metal dust and fumes.

Environmental Contamination: Cobalt mining and processing can contaminate soil and water, affecting ecosystems and human health.

First Aid: For inhalation, move to fresh air. For skin contact, wash with soap and water. For ingestion, do not induce vomiting; seek medical attention.

Medical Monitoring: Workers with cobalt exposure require regular medical checkups including lung function tests and cobalt blood levels.

Supply Chain Risks: Artisanal cobalt mining in DRC involves significant health, safety, and human rights concerns.
