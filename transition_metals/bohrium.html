Overview

Bohrium is a synthetic chemical element and a member of the transactinide series. It is a highly radioactive metal that does not occur naturally and can only be produced in particle accelerators. Bohrium is expected to be a solid under normal conditions and is predicted to have properties similar to rhenium, directly above it in group 7 of the periodic table, though relativistic effects may cause significant deviations.

Bohrium was first convincingly synthesized in 1981 by a German research team at the Gesellschaft für Schwerionenforschung (GSI) in Darmstadt, led by Peter Armbruster and Gottfried Münzenberg. The element was named in honor of Niels Bohr, the Danish physicist who made fundamental contributions to understanding atomic structure and quantum theory. The naming followed significant controversy, as Soviet researchers had claimed earlier discovery, but the GSI work provided the definitive evidence.

Chemical studies of bohrium are extremely limited due to its short half-lives and production difficulties. Based on its position in group 7, bohrium is predicted to form a stable +7 oxidation state, similar to rhenium. It likely forms volatile oxychlorides (BhO₃Cl) and heptoxide (Bh₂O₇), though these have not been synthesized. Theoretical calculations suggest relativistic effects might make bohrium's chemistry differ significantly from rhenium, possibly making lower oxidation states more stable. Bohrium is expected to be less metallic than its lighter congeners due to relativistic contraction of the 7s orbital.

Bohrium-270 is the most stable known isotope with a half-life of approximately 61 seconds. The relatively short half-lives of bohrium isotopes have made chemical studies extremely challenging, requiring fast separation and detection techniques.

Production and Occurrence

Natural Occurrence: Bohrium does not occur naturally. It is entirely synthetic.

Artificial Production Methods:

  1. Cold Fusion Reactions:
    ²⁰⁹Bi + ⁵⁴Cr → ²⁶¹Bh + 2n (original GSI discovery reaction)
    ²⁰⁹Bi + ⁵⁴Cr → ²⁶²Bh + n
  2. Hot Fusion Reactions:
    ²⁴⁹Bk + ²²Ne → ²⁶⁷Bh + 4n
    ²⁴⁸Cm + ²³Na → ²⁶⁶Bh + 5n
  3. Other Reactions:
    ²⁵⁴Es + ²²Ne → ²⁷²Bh + 4n (produces longer-lived isotopes)
    ²⁵⁷Fm + ²²Ne → ²⁷⁵Bh + 4n
  4. Separation - Bohrium atoms separated using gas-phase thermochromatography or recoil separation techniques. The short half-lives require extremely fast separation methods.
  5. Detection - Individual atoms detected by their characteristic decay patterns using silicon detectors, often requiring correlation with daughter decays.
  6. Quantity - Production rates extremely low (a few atoms per month of beam time).

Production Sites: Gesellschaft für Schwerionenforschung (GSI, Germany), Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (Dubna, Russia), Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (USA), RIKEN (Japan). Total world production is only a few dozen atoms.

Discovery and History

The discovery of bohrium marked the increasing role of European laboratories in superheavy element research:

Early Claims (1976): Soviet researchers at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR) in Dubna, led by Yuri Oganessian, bombarded bismuth-209 with chromium-54 ions. They reported detecting spontaneous fission with a half-life of 1-2 milliseconds, which they tentatively attributed to element 107. However, the evidence was not conclusive.

Definitive Discovery (1981): A team at the Gesellschaft für Schwerionenforschung (GSI) in Darmstadt, Germany, led by Peter Armbruster and Gottfried Münzenberg, definitively synthesized bohrium. They bombarded bismuth-209 with chromium-54 ions using the UNILAC accelerator. They detected alpha decay chains leading to known dubnium and lawrencium isotopes, providing unambiguous evidence for element 107. They proposed the name "nielsbohrium" (Ns) in honor of Niels Bohr.

Confirmation and Naming Controversy: The GSI team confirmed their discovery in 1989 with improved experiments. Meanwhile, Soviet researchers maintained their priority claim. The IUPAC/IUPAP Transfermium Working Group (TWG) evaluated the claims and credited the GSI team with discovery in their 1992 report.

Final Naming (1997): After the usual naming controversies, IUPAC adopted the name "bohrium" (Bh) in 1997, honoring Niels Bohr. This followed the pattern of naming elements after great scientists in physics and chemistry. The shorter name "bohrium" was chosen over the originally proposed "nielsbohrium."

Recent Developments: In the 2000s, more neutron-rich isotopes of bohrium were produced at Dubna and GSI, allowing for slightly longer-lived isotopes that might enable future chemical studies.

Interesting Facts

  • Bohrium is named after Niels Bohr, one of the founders of quantum mechanics and atomic theory
  • It was the first element to be definitively discovered in Germany
  • Bohrium's most stable isotope has a half-life of only about 61 seconds
  • The element was originally going to be called "nielsbohrium" but was shortened to "bohrium"
  • Only about 40 atoms of bohrium have ever been produced and detected worldwide
  • Bohrium's chemical properties are predicted to be significantly affected by relativistic effects
  • The element's discovery marked a shift in superheavy element research from US-Soviet competition to include European laboratories
  • Bohrium is so rare that if all atoms ever produced were collected, they would be invisible to the naked eye

Safety Information

Radioactivity: All isotopes of bohrium are highly radioactive. Primary hazard is alpha radiation (some isotopes may undergo spontaneous fission).

Production: Produced only in specialized nuclear facilities with heavy ion accelerators. Not available outside research laboratories.

Quantity: Only atom quantities produced; insufficient for bulk handling or conventional chemical study.

Alpha Radiation: Most isotopes decay by alpha emission; external hazard minimal but internal hazard serious if ingested or inhaled.

Spontaneous Fission: Some isotopes may undergo spontaneous fission producing neutrons and fission fragments.

Daughter Products: Decay chains include other radioactive elements; secondary radiation hazards.

Handling: Atom-at-a-time techniques; remote handling essential. The short half-lives require extremely fast experimental setups.

Containment: Produced and studied in sealed systems; strict containment protocols to prevent release.

Regulations: Subject to nuclear materials regulations despite minute quantities.

Waste: Radioactive waste from production experiments managed as nuclear waste.

First Aid: Not applicable due to production scale; standard radiation emergency procedures if contamination suspected.

Protective Equipment: Standard radiation laboratory protections.

Storage: Not stored; atoms exist momentarily then decay.

Environmental: No environmental release; all material contained and decays quickly.
